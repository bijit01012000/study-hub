    Voice AI

    1 sections · 819 words

    Module 11 · 13 questions · 1 practical drill

    Build for a conversation that breaks if you are slow.

    This module exists because almost nobody else in this market has it, and because voice is where GenAI stops being a text problem and becomes a real-time systems problem. Anyone can describe the STT-LLM-TTS chain after reading one blog post; the questions here chase the part that only shows up once you've actually tried to make a conversation feel natural, the latency budget, what happens when the caller interrupts, what a wrong transcript does three turns later. A candidate who names the three components and stops reads exactly like a candidate who has never thought about where the milliseconds go or what happens when two systems both think it is their turn to talk. The follow-ups are built to surface that gap fast, and ifYouHaveNotDone matters most here because most readers of this guide have never shipped a voice agent, but plenty have built a streaming pipeline, a WebSocket chat feature or a realtime system with its own latency budget, and that experience answers most of this module honestly if it is framed right.

    Inside this module: Latency budget · Endpointing · Barge-in · Accents & consent

    How to work this module: Answer each question out loud in 90 seconds before reading the model answer. Then check you named a number, the alternative you rejected, and what actually failed. Those three are what the follow-ups are hunting for.

    Concept checkpoints

    Q11.1 — Draw a voice agent end to end. What sits between the caller's voice and the reply they hear?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate can name transport, STT, LLM, TTS and orchestration as distinct pieces, not one blurred "the AI answers the phone" block.

    Q11.2 — What's your latency budget for a natural-feeling voice conversation, and where does each part of it go?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate has a real number for acceptable response time and can decompose it by stage, not just say "as fast as possible."

    Q11.3 — Why do streaming pipelines beat sequential ones for voice, and what actually breaks in a sequential design?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate understands parallel, partial-output pipelines as a latency necessity, not a nice-to-have optimisation.

    Q11.4 — The transcript from STT is wrong. What does your system do downstream when the words are simply mistaken?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate designs for ASR error as a routine input condition, not an edge case handled by hoping STT is accurate enough.

    Q11.5 — How does your agent know the caller has actually stopped speaking, not just paused?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate understands endpointing as a genuine detection problem with a real cost, not an assumed instant event.

    Q11.6 — The caller interrupts mid-sentence while your agent is talking. Walk me through what has to happen.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate understands barge-in as a full-stack cancellation problem across every stage, not just muting playback.

    Q11.7 — You're picking a TTS provider for latency. Why isn't the model's own latency number the one that matters?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate knows total TTS delay is model time plus network plus playback buffer, not the vendor's headline benchmark.

    Q11.8 — How do you prompt an LLM differently when its output becomes speech instead of text on a screen?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate knows voice output needs short, spoken-style sentences with no visual formatting, not a text prompt reused unchanged.

    Q11.9 — A tool call takes three seconds mid-conversation. What does the caller experience while it runs?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate has designed for retrieval latency landing inside the conversational budget, rather than treating it as background work.

    Q11.10 — For a realtime voice agent, when would you choose WebRTC over a plain WebSocket, and when not?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate understands the actual trade-off, WebRTC's media handling versus WebSocket's simplicity, not just a stated preference.

    Q11.11 — How would you handle accents, code switching and Indian language support in a voice agent?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate treats accent and language variation as a measured, designed-for problem, not an afterthought for an English-only build.

    Q11.12 — What needs to be in place for consent, call recording and transcript retention before go-live?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate treats consent and data retention as a design requirement from day one, not a legal afterthought bolted on later.

    Q11.13 — [DRILL] Design a voice agent for inbound support with a 1.5 second response budget.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate can combine architecture, latency budgeting, endpointing, barge-in and error handling into one coherent design under a stated constraint.
