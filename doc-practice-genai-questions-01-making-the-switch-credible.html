      Making the switch credible

      0 sections · 4,446 words

      Module 01 · 9 questions · 1 practical drill

      Explain the move without overclaiming or apologising for it.

      Q1.1 — Most of your experience is backend or full-stack. Why GenAI now?

      Weak answer: "AI is the future and I wanted to be part of it. I've done a few courses and read a lot, and I think this is where software is heading."

      Why it fails: - Every sentence talks about the field, none about anything the candidate has actually done or decided. - Courses and reading show consumption, not a single owned decision or artefact.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot name one thing built or changed because of this interest, only intentions. - No mention of what from the backend years might actually carry over.

      Model answer: 1. The real trigger wasn't the market, it was a specific annoyance. Support kept escalating tickets to me because triage was manual and slow, so I built a small classifier behind an API over two weekends to make it stop. 2. It worked well enough that the team kept using it, and that's when I started reading about retrieval and evaluation properly, because the classifier's mistakes forced me to understand why it was wrong. 3. From backend, what transferred straight across was thinking about idempotency, testing at the boundary, and treating any external call as something that will eventually fail. 4. What didn't transfer at all was any feel for how rewording a prompt changes the output. I only got that by comparing failed versions side by side, which took longer than I expected. 5. Eight months on, what's kept me interested past the first project is evaluation work specifically, working out whether a change actually helped, which is less exciting than building but is the part that matters.

      Follow-ups: 1. OWNERSHIP — What's the first thing you built here that nobody asked you to build? · Self-initiated work versus course consumption dressed up as experience. 2. REFLECTION — What from your backend years transfers directly, and what doesn't transfer at all? · An honest account of the gap rather than a seamless, too-convenient story. 3. REFLECTION — If the hiring market cooled tomorrow, would you still be doing this on weekends? · Whether the motivation survives the removal of market excitement. 4. OWNERSHIP — Which specific project made you decide this was worth pursuing seriously? · A concrete trigger rather than a vague sense of timing.

      Tip: If you haven't built anything yet, talk about a real problem you noticed at work and what you'd try first, not what the field is doing. Do not claim excitement about the field as your reason. Name one thing you've actually read closely enough to disagree with.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Lead with one project, not three - 90 seconds, no more - One number you can defend - One thing you still don't know

      Q1.2 — What in your current work is actually relevant to a GenAI role?

      Weak answer: "I'm a strong problem solver, I learn fast, and I'm used to working with complex systems, so I think those skills will transfer well."

      Why it fails: - Generic strengths like this could describe almost any candidate for almost any role. - Nothing here is specific to what GenAI systems actually require day to day.

      Instant rejection: - No named skill maps onto a concrete part of building or running a GenAI system. - The answer would be identical if the role were something else entirely.

      Model answer: 1. I run on-call for services that page at 3am when a dependency times out, so I already think in retries, circuit breakers and graceful degradation, which is most of what makes a GenAI system reliable in production. 2. I've built alerting around latency percentiles, not just averages, and that habit maps directly onto watching tail latency on a model call, where the slow 5% is usually where users actually suffer. 3. What genuinely doesn't transfer is any intuition for output quality. A timeout is binary, a slightly wrong answer is not, and I've had to learn to think about that differently. 4. I've also spent years writing runbooks for things that fail in ways nobody predicted, which is close to what evaluation and monitoring for a model looks like once it is live. 5. What I expect to matter less than I think is my deployment tooling knowledge specifically, since most of that is already solved by whatever platform the team is on, so I'd rather spend early weeks on evaluation instead.

      Follow-ups: 1. OWNERSHIP — Pick one thing you do today and tell me exactly which part of a GenAI system it maps onto. · Whether the mapping is specific or just a vague claim of transferability. 2. REFLECTION — What part of your current job has nothing useful to say about this role? · Honesty about the limits of transfer, not a claim that everything applies. 3. OWNERSHIP — Tell me about a production incident you handled that taught you about running unreliable systems. · Concrete experience with failure handling that maps onto a GenAI system's own unreliability. 4. REFLECTION — Which of your current skills do you expect to matter less here than you think? · Self-aware calibration rather than blanket confidence.

      Tip: If you haven't touched a GenAI system, pick two things from your current role you can actually explain in mechanism, not just name. Do not claim your whole skill set transfers. Name the one piece that genuinely doesn't, it shows you've actually thought about it.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Two skills, mapped precisely - One gap, named honestly - Specific beats broad, always - 30 seconds per mapped skill

      Q1.3 — You have not shipped this in production. Why should we hire you?

      Weak answer: "That's true, I haven't shipped a GenAI system yet, but I'm confident I could learn quickly and I'm very passionate about this space."

      Why it fails: - Passion and confidence are asserted with nothing behind them, no evidence at all. - The answer concedes the whole premise instead of making any case for what is already true.

      Instant rejection: - No adjacent experience is named that would make the learning curve shorter than average. - Nothing distinguishes this candidate from anyone else saying the same thing.

      Model answer: 1. You're right, I haven't run a GenAI system in production, so I won't pretend otherwise. What I have run in production for three years is a set of pipelines other teams depend on without noticing, until they break. 2. The failure modes overlap more than people assume. A pipeline silently producing wrong numbers because an upstream schema changed is the same shape as a model silently producing a wrong answer because the input drifted. 3. I've owned data quality checks that catch bad output before it reaches a dashboard executives look at, and that instinct, checking output rather than trusting it, is most of what production GenAI work needs. 4. What I honestly expect to be hardest is prompt and retrieval debugging, because the feedback loop is fuzzier than a failed data validation, and I haven't built the muscle for reading model output critically at speed yet. 5. So the case isn't that I already know this, it's that the part which usually takes people longest, taking an unreliable system seriously, is something I've already had to build the habits for.

      Follow-ups: 1. OWNERSHIP — What adjacent thing have you shipped that shares a failure mode with a GenAI system? · Whether real transferable production experience exists, named specifically. 2. REFLECTION — What part of production GenAI work do you expect to be hardest for you, honestly? · Self-awareness about the actual gap rather than blanket confidence. 3. OWNERSHIP — Tell me about the closest thing to an on-call incident you've owned end to end. · Evidence of handling failure and ownership under pressure, a core transferable skill. 4. REFLECTION — If this didn't work out in 90 days, what would the reason most likely be? · Honest self-assessment of the real risk, not a deflection.

      Tip: If you've never shipped anything GenAI, lead with the closest production system you owned and be specific about what broke and how you caught it. Do not say you would learn fast. Say exactly which part of the job you expect to be hard, that is more convincing than confidence.

      Numbers worth quoting: - One adjacent system, named - Three years beats zero courses - Name the hardest part first - No more than one caveat

      Q1.4 — Walk me through the GenAI side project on your CV.

      Weak answer: "I built a chatbot that helps users troubleshoot issues, it handles the full support flow end to end and has been really well received."

      Why it fails: - "Handles the full support flow end to end" describes a demo as if it were a shipped product. - "Well received" is unfalsifiable, no real users or numbers are named.

      Instant rejection: - No mention of scale, real users, or what breaks it, only vague praise. - The word production or live is used, or implied, for something that never carried real traffic.

      Model answer: 1. It's a proof of concept, not a product, and I say that upfront. It's a support chatbot that answers from a fixed set of internal documents, and I tested it myself with about fifteen real questions pulled from old tickets. 2. I come from QA, so the part I actually spent most of my time on wasn't the chatbot itself, it was writing test cases for it, checking what it did when a question had no good answer in the documents. 3. It got about ten of the fifteen clearly right, three partially right in a way I could explain, and two wrong in a way that taught me the retrieval was pulling the wrong document, not that the model reasoned badly. 4. I didn't put it in front of real users and I didn't build authentication, rate limiting or monitoring, because the point was to learn the retrieval and evaluation loop, not to ship a product. 5. If I put it in front of a thousand people tomorrow, the first thing to break would be the fixed document set. Real questions would ask about things outside it, and it has no honest way to say it doesn't know.

      Follow-ups: 1. OWNERSHIP — How many real people used it, and what did you watch them get stuck on? · Whether any real usage exists behind the claim, and specific friction observed. 2. REFLECTION — What would break first if you put this in front of 1,000 real users tomorrow? · Honest awareness of the gap between a proof of concept and a production system. 3. OWNERSHIP — What's the one part of this you're proud of building yourself, versus scaffolding? · Whether the candidate can separate their own contribution from tooling or templates. 4. REFLECTION — What did you deliberately leave out of scope, and why? · Evidence of scoping judgement rather than an accidental toy.

      Tip: If you haven't built anything yet, say so plainly and describe the smallest version you would build first and how you would test it. Do not use the word production, live or shipped for a proof of concept, even a good one. Call it what it is and describe it precisely.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Name the real test count - One thing proud of, one weak - Say "proof of concept" aloud - Zero real users, say it first

      Q1.5 — In your own words, what counts as real GenAI experience?

      Weak answer: "I think any hands-on work with LLMs counts, whether that's a course project, using ChatGPT heavily for my job, or building something, it's all valuable exposure."

      Why it fails: - Flattens very different things, using a chat tool and building a system, into one undifferentiated category. - "Valuable exposure" avoids drawing any actual line, which is what the question asked for.

      Instant rejection: - Using an AI product as a consumer is counted as equivalent to building one. - No distinction is made between reading about a technique and having applied it.

      Model answer: 1. For me the line is whether I had to make a decision the model's output depended on, not just whether I typed into a box. Using ChatGPT to draft an email is using a tool, same as a spell checker. 2. Real experience starts when you own the plumbing, choosing what goes into the prompt, deciding what to do when the output is wrong, and measuring whether a change actually helped. 3. By that standard, the most real thing on my own CV is a small internal tool that summarises pull request diffs for reviewers, because I had to decide what to do when the summary missed something important. 4. The least real thing is a line I nearly wrote about "using AI tools daily", which just meant I used a coding assistant like everyone else on the team, so I cut it rather than let it imply more. 5. Heavy use of a coding assistant is a genuinely grey case. I'd call it adjacent exposure to how these models behave, not GenAI experience on its own, unless you can point to a decision you made because it got something wrong.

      Follow-ups: 1. REFLECTION — Where would you draw the line between using AI tools and having GenAI experience? · A real, defensible boundary rather than an inclusive blur. 2. OWNERSHIP — What's the most 'real' thing on your own CV by that definition, and what's the least? · Whether the candidate applies their own standard honestly to themselves. 3. REFLECTION — Is heavy use of a coding assistant GenAI experience? Why or why not? · Careful reasoning about a genuinely ambiguous case, not a reflexive yes or no. 4. OWNERSHIP — Tell me about the moment something you built with an LLM surprised you by failing. · Whether hands-on building actually happened, evidenced by a specific failure.

      Tip: If most of your exposure is using AI tools as a consumer, say that plainly and separate it clearly from anything you built or decided. Do not let tool usage inflate into experience on your CV. List it separately, and under a different, smaller heading.

      Numbers worth quoting: - One decision beats ten prompts - Draw the line, then apply it - Consumer use is not experience - One surprising failure, named

      Q1.6 — Your CV says "worked with LLMs". What does that actually mean?

      Weak answer: "It means I've integrated LLMs into a few projects, played around with different prompting techniques, and I'm comfortable working with them."

      Why it fails: - "A few projects" and "comfortable working with them" name nothing specific enough to ask a follow-up about. - Describes a feeling, not a piece of work, a decision made, or a task an LLM actually did.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot name which model, which task, or what the LLM actually did in the system. - The phrase survives unchanged even when asked to be more specific.

      Model answer: 1. It means one specific thing, not a category. I called an LLM API from a backend service to turn a free-text bug report into a structured ticket with a category and priority, so triagers didn't read every line. 2. I chose the model mainly on cost and latency, since this ran on every incoming report, and settled on a smaller model plus a stricter prompt after a larger model was noticeably slower for a task this simple. 3. The worst output I got was the model inventing a priority field that wasn't in my schema, plausible sounding but not something my downstream code expected, and it broke silently for a day before anyone noticed. 4. After that I added schema validation on every response and a fallback to "needs manual review" rather than trusting anything that didn't parse cleanly, the closest thing I have to a lesson learned the hard way. 5. If I had to replace the CV line, it would be "built a classifier on top of an LLM API for triaging bug reports", smaller sounding than "worked with LLMs" but actually true and defensible word for word.

      Follow-ups: 1. OWNERSHIP — Name the specific model and task. What did the LLM actually do in that system? · Whether the vague phrase resolves into a real, checkable piece of work. 2. REFLECTION — If I struck 'worked with LLMs' from your CV, what one sentence would you replace it with? · Whether the candidate can write a precise claim instead of a comfortable vague one. 3. OWNERSHIP — What's the worst output you ever got from an LLM in something you built, and what did you do? · Concrete hands-on experience evidenced by a specific failure and response. 4. REFLECTION — Was that work closer to calling an API, or closer to designing how the system uses it? · Honest self-placement on the range from integration to design, not the biggest claim available.

      Tip: If your CV line is vague because the work behind it is thin, shrink the claim to match it exactly, a small true claim survives questions better than a big vague one. Do not let "worked with" stand in for a task you cannot describe. Replace it with the actual verb, called, built, evaluated, whatever is true.

      Numbers worth quoting: - One sentence, fully defensible - Name the model, not "an LLM" - One failure, one fix - Smaller claim, zero gaps

      Q1.7 — Where do you expect to struggle in your first 90 days?

      Weak answer: "Honestly, I go too deep on things and want every detail right before moving on, so I might need to work on shipping faster instead of over-preparing."

      Why it fails: - "Go too deep" and "want every detail right" are strengths reframed as a weakness, not a real gap. - Names nothing specific to GenAI work, this answer could be given for any role unchanged.

      Instant rejection: - The stated weakness would read as a compliment in a performance review. - No specific skill, tool or situation from the new role is named anywhere.

      Model answer: 1. The honest one is reading model output critically at speed. In my current job a failure is usually binary, a check passes or a page fires, and I'm fast at diagnosing those. 2. A model output that's fluent but subtly wrong doesn't trip any alarm, and I don't yet have the instinct for spotting that quickly, I have to slow down and check it against the source every time. 3. I expect my first few weeks of reviewing model output to be genuinely slower than a teammate who's done this for a year, and I'd rather say that now than have it be a surprise in a code review. 4. What I'd do in week one is ask to shadow whatever evaluation or review process the team already has, rather than assume my instincts transfer cleanly, because I suspect they only transfer partly. 5. The gap closes with repetition more than study, so my plan is to look at as many real model outputs against real inputs as I can in the first month, and keep a running list of the mistakes I keep missing.

      Follow-ups: 1. REFLECTION — Is 'going too deep' actually a weakness, or is that a comfortable way to describe yourself? · Resistance to the self-flattering reframe, and a real answer underneath it. 2. OWNERSHIP — Which specific part of this job do you not know how to do yet? · A concrete, checkable gap rather than a personality trait dressed up as one. 3. REFLECTION — What would your manager 90 days in most likely say you were still getting wrong? · Honest anticipation of a real, specific shortfall from someone else's view. 4. OWNERSHIP — What would you do in week one to start closing that gap? · A concrete plan rather than a vague intention to improve.

      Tip: If you haven't reviewed model output before, say that directly and name what skill from your current role is the closest substitute, honestly graded. Do not offer a strength dressed as a weakness. If it would sound good in a performance review, it is not the answer to this question.

      Numbers worth quoting: - One real gap, not a virtue - First month, not first week - Slower than average, admit it - One closing plan, concrete

      Q1.8 — Why did you build directly against model APIs instead of using a framework?

      Weak answer: "I haven't really used an orchestration framework, that's a gap in my experience, but I'm confident I'd pick it up quickly if the team needed me to."

      Why it fails: - Concedes the premise as a real deficiency without checking whether the underlying capability already exists. - "Confident I'd pick it up quickly" is asserted with nothing behind it.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot describe what an orchestration framework actually does, even roughly. - No mapping is offered between anything the candidate built and what the framework provides.

      Model answer: 1. What I actually built is a loop in plain Python behind a Node front end, it calls the model, checks the response against a schema, retries once with a stricter prompt if that fails, and falls back to a fixed message if it fails twice. 2. That's functionally what an orchestration framework's chain or agent step gives you, retries, structured parsing, fallback handling, I just wrote the loop myself because I wanted to see exactly what happened at each step. 3. The honest gap is narrow, I don't know a specific framework's class names or its callback hooks, and closing that is a read of the documentation, not a concept to learn from nothing. 4. Where I'd reach for the framework now is a project with several chained steps and shared memory across them, because at that point the bookkeeping I'd otherwise write by hand starts to genuinely earn its cost. 5. What hand-rolling it taught me is exactly where the retries and failures actually happen, so if I pick up a framework next, I'll be reading its internals with a much better sense of what to check when something breaks.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What does a framework like that actually handle for you that you'd otherwise write yourself? · Whether the candidate can describe the mechanism, not just the name. 2. OWNERSHIP — Show me the part of your own code that does the same job under a different name. · Evidence the underlying capability already exists, just unlabelled. 3. REFLECTION — What would make you reach for the framework next time instead of hand-rolling it? · Calibrated judgement about when the framework earns its cost, not a blanket stance. 4. REFLECTION — What did hand-rolling it teach you that using the framework from day one wouldn't have? · Whether the choice produced real understanding, not just extra work.

      Tip: If you've never built anything against a model API directly, describe the plainest possible version, one call, one retry, one fallback, and be honest that it's small. Do not claim framework familiarity you don't have. Describe the mechanism you understand and say plainly which specific API surface is unfamiliar.

      Numbers worth quoting: - One retry, one fallback, named - Half a day to learn an API - Map the function, not the name - One case you'd use it instead

      Q1.9 — [DRILL] Build a 90 second switch narrative that survives three follow-up questions. (practical drill)

      Weak answer: "N/A, this is a drill. A weak attempt drifts past 90 seconds, talks about the field rather than the candidate, or has no number to defend."

      Why it fails: - Runs well past 90 seconds because nothing was cut, or stays vague because there was nothing to cut. - Contains no number and no named artefact that a follow-up could actually attach to.

      Instant rejection: - The story is about the field's trajectory, not a specific thing the candidate built or decided. - It changes shape under the first follow-up because nothing in it was actually fixed.

      Model answer: 1. Ninety second version: I moved towards GenAI work because a manual ticket-triage problem at work got specific enough to annoy me, so I built a small classifier behind an API over two weekends, and the team kept using it. 2. From my day job the habits that carried over directly were testing at the boundary and treating any external call as something that eventually fails, the retry logic there is closer to database handling than anything new. 3. What's genuinely new is evaluation, building a small reference set and checking output against it rather than trusting my own read, and that's still the part I'm actively getting better at, not something I'd claim mastery of. 4. Cut to three sentences: the classifier, the one number, roughly a third of the incoming queue once the team adopted it, and the honest gap, evaluation, still learning. 5. If someone said I was chasing the hype, the reply is that I built the thing before I applied anywhere, the fashionable move would have been to keep shipping backend features and add "AI adjacent" to a CV afterwards.

      Follow-ups: 1. REFLECTION — Cut it to exactly three sentences, what survives? · Whether the core of the story is load-bearing or padding was doing the work. 2. MEASUREMENT — What's the one number in your narrative an interviewer could ask you to defend? · Whether the story is anchored to something concrete, not pure narrative. 3. OWNERSHIP — What did you leave out that a sceptical interviewer would want to know? · Self-audit for the gap the candidate is hoping goes unasked. 4. TRADEOFF — If I said this sounds like you're just chasing the hype, what's your one-line reply? · A ready, evidenced answer to the obvious pushback, not a rehearsed pitch.

      Tip: If you haven't built anything yet, your 90 seconds should name the real problem you noticed and the smallest thing you'd try first, not a claim you cannot back up. Do not fill the 90 seconds with market talk to cover a thin story. A short true story beats a long vague one under a follow-up.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 90 seconds, not 91 - One project leads, not three - One number, fully defensible - Three sentences is the floor
