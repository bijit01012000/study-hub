      Drive the blank page when the expensive component is non-deterministic.

      Q15.1 — Design a legal-team Q&A system: 2 million documents, under 3-second answers, with checkable citations.

      Weak answer: "I'd chunk the docs, embed them into a vector database, retrieve the top-k chunks, and ask the LLM to answer with citations, using a reranker for quality."

      Why it fails: - Citations are a prompt instruction, not a mechanism that maps a quoted span back to a document and locator a lawyer can check. - The 3-second budget and the 2 million document scale are stated then ignored; nothing in the design responds to either number.

      Instant rejection: - No plan for what the system says when retrieved passages don't actually support a confident answer. - Never distinguishes brute-force search from an indexing approach that can survive 2 million documents.

      Model answer: 1. I spend the 3 seconds deliberately: about 400ms retrieval against the 2M-document index, 300ms rerank of the top 50 down to 6, the rest for generation and checks. 2. I stream the answer as it generates, because streaming buys back perceived latency even though it doesn't cut total compute or cost. 3. At 2 million documents I can't brute-force similarity, so I use a sharded ANN index, and every chunk carries its source document, page and paragraph offset. 4. The model has to quote the exact span it relies on, and I mechanically check that span is a real substring of the retrieved chunk before it reaches a lawyer. 5. If no retrieved passage supports a confident answer, I return "no supporting passage found" rather than let the model paraphrase its way to a plausible one.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — How do you split the 3 seconds across retrieval, rerank and generation? · Whether a latency budget is decomposed by stage, not treated as one number. 2. FAILURE — The citation points to the right document but the wrong paragraph. Win or fail? · Whether a real, verifiable citation is distinguished from one that only looks real. 3. TRADEOFF — Is reranking worth its latency here versus on a lower-stakes search tool? · Whether latency-for-accuracy is a deliberate call tied to the cost of being wrong. 4. MEASUREMENT — How do you check, ongoing, what fraction of citations actually hold up? · Whether citation accuracy is tracked continuously, not checked once at launch.

      Tip: If you've built search with a hard latency SLA, or shown users a source link they can click through and verify, that judgement transfers directly here. Don't claim you've built a legal citation system. Say you'd verify any AI-generated reference mechanically before it reaches someone relying on it.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 400ms retrieval, 300ms rerank - Quote spans, don't paraphrase - Sharded ANN past ~1M docs - Verify citations, don't trust them

      Q15.2 — Design a support assistant for 40,000 tickets a month that can never promise an unauthorised refund.

      Weak answer: "The assistant reads the ticket, checks the knowledge base, and drafts a response. It's told not to promise things it can't keep, and escalates when unsure."

      Why it fails: - "Told not to promise" is a prompt-level control on a requirement that needs structural enforcement outside the model. - Escalating on the model's own confidence uses exactly the signal that fails when the model is confidently wrong.

      Instant rejection: - No authorisation table separating what the model can draft from what it can actually commit to sending. - No link from the 40,000-ticket volume to how many tickets a human review team can actually absorb.

      Model answer: 1. The model's job is to classify intent and draft a response. It never directly issues a refund or credit; that goes through a separate authorisation layer. 2. The authorisation layer is a rule engine checking ticket category, plan and requested action against a table of what's allowed, and only a pass reaches the customer. 3. Escalation triggers are rules, not model confidence: any refund above a set amount, any legal language, any disagreement between draft and authorisation, escalates. 4. At 40,000 tickets a month, a 15% escalation rate is 6,000 tickets of agent time, which I size the review team against rather than treat as a footnote. 5. I sample auto-resolved tickets weekly against what an agent would have said, and a rising disagreement rate tells me to tighten that category's rules.

      Follow-ups: 1. FAILURE — The model confidently promises a refund you never give. What stops that message? · Whether refund authority sits behind a deterministic gate, not the model's own reasoning. 2. MEASUREMENT — At 5% escalation of 40,000 tickets, what does that mean for headcount? · Whether an escalation rate is connected to a real staffing number. 3. TECHNICAL — Model confidence or a rule-based trigger: which decides a human sees this? · Whether a self-reported score is distinguished from an auditable trigger. 4. TRADEOFF — Full autonomy scales further; routing everything ambiguous is safer. Where's the line? · Whether the candidate states and defends a specific line, not just 'balance'.

      Tip: If you've built a permission check, an approval table, or a payment rule separate from the code path that requests it, that's the same structural split. Don't claim you've built this exact assistant. Say you'd separate drafting from authorising, and gate anything with financial weight behind a hard rule.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 15% escalation, sized to staff - Rules escalate, not confidence - Refunds gated, never prompted - Track model-vs-human - disagreement

      Q15.3 — Design a code-review bot for a 12-million-line monorepo: comments within 5 minutes, low false positives.

      Weak answer: "The assistant sends the diff and surrounding file to the LLM, asks it to review for bugs, style and missing tests, and leaves comments as a CI step."

      Why it fails: - Never bounds how much surrounding context is pulled in, and a change in a 12M-line repo can depend on code outside one file. - No plan for false positives: no calibration, no suppression, no way a comment earns or loses trust over time.

      Instant rejection: - No reasoning tying the 5-minute budget to diff size, so a 3,000-line PR gets the same assumed budget as a 30-line one. - Treats "review the code" as one undifferentiated call, with no split between check types of very different cost.

      Model answer: 1. I scope context to the changed files plus a ranked handful of the most relevant call sites, not every dependent, because unbounded context scales with repo size. 2. Fast deterministic checks, linters and existing static analysis, run first for nearly free, and the model is reserved for what those tools structurally can't catch. 3. Checks run in parallel per file, so a large PR's latency tracks its biggest file, and past a size threshold I sample the highest-risk files instead of all of them. 4. Every comment carries a confidence score, and anything below a calibrated threshold is suppressed, because one wrong comment costs more than it looks like. 5. I measure comment-acceptance rate per rule category, and a category with rising dismissals gets its threshold raised or gets pulled, not left running unchanged.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — A changed function is used in 40 places. Do you pull in all 40 for context? · Whether cross-file context is bounded rather than scaling with repo size. 2. FAILURE — The bot is wrong on the same engineer's PR three times this month. Then what? · Awareness that false positives compound into engineers ignoring the bot entirely. 3. TRADEOFF — A 3,000-line PR and a 30-line PR: same 5-minute budget for both? · Whether latency and cost are understood to scale with diff size. 4. MEASUREMENT — How do you know the bot is useful, separate from whether it's running? · Whether usefulness is measured by comments acted on, not uptime or volume.

      Tip: If you've tuned a linter's rule set, or watched a noisy CI check get muted by a team, you've seen the same trust dynamic this question is testing for. Don't claim you've built this bot. Say you understand why unbounded context and unsuppressed false positives both break trust in an automated check.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Top 10 call sites, not all 40 - Cheap checks first, model last - Suppress below confidence line - Track acceptance, not volume

      Q15.4 — Add semantic search beside a keyword search that hits 150ms p99, without regressing its working 90%.

      Weak answer: "I'd add a vector index of the listings, run keyword and semantic search on every query, merge and rerank the results, and return the combined list."

      Why it fails: - Running both systems on every query adds a full vector search to every request, with no reasoning about the 150ms budget. - No check on whether keyword search alone was already sufficient, so semantic search adds cost with no benefit on most traffic.

      Instant rejection: - No plan for what happens when the two systems disagree on ranking; merging is treated as automatically neutral. - No account of catalogue freshness: new listings need to reach both indexes, not just the existing one.

      Model answer: 1. I first score whether keyword search is already likely sufficient, short exact-match queries rarely need semantic help, and gate the new path to exploratory queries. 2. That gating is what protects the working 90%: they never touch the new code path, so they can't regress from it. 3. Where semantic search does fire, I keep the two result sets separate and arbitrate with a rule: strong keyword matches anchor the top, semantic fills in below. 4. Semantic search adds a real cost, so I budget roughly 40ms for it, and if gating misfires past that window the request falls back to keyword-only. 5. New listings hit the vector index on the same ingestion pipeline as keyword search, and I regression-test against queries the old system already handled well.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What decides, per query, whether semantic search even needs to run? · Whether queries are routed selectively rather than both stages running unconditionally. 2. TRADEOFF — Keyword and semantic rank different products first. Who wins? · Whether arbitration between two signals is a designed rule, not a blind blend. 3. FAILURE — A new product line is barely findable by either system for a week. What catches that? · Awareness of cold-start and reindex lag for new inventory. 4. MEASUREMENT — A month on, how do you know semantic search helped, not quietly hurt, the 90%? · Whether there's a real regression check against the segment that worked.

      Tip: If you've added a new code path behind a feature flag next to a working system, and protected existing traffic from it, that's the same instinct. Don't claim you've shipped semantic search. Say you'd gate a new retrieval path selectively and regression-test it against what already worked.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Gate ~15-20% of queries - Budget ~40ms for the new path - Fall back to keyword on miss - Regression-test the working 90%

      Q15.5 — Design a voice support agent handling 30,000 daily calls: first audio under 800ms, even with a KB lookup.

      Weak answer: "I'd build the standard STT to LLM to TTS pipeline, add a knowledge-base lookup as a tool call the model makes, and stream the response back to keep it fast."

      Why it fails: - Treats the knowledge-base lookup as a normal tool call, ignoring that its latency lands inside an 800ms voice budget. - No scale reasoning: 30,000 daily calls and their concurrent-call peak never shape the retrieval or escalation design at all.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot say what the caller experiences in the seconds a retrieval-backed answer takes to actually assemble. - No escalation path for when a lookup can't return in time or returns nothing, only a happy-path design.

      Model answer: 1. I treat retrieval as inside the turn budget, not background work: endpointing alone costs roughly 150-400ms, so retrieval, generation and TTS share what's left of 800ms. 2. I keep the voice knowledge base small and fast, a narrow index of common resolutions, and route anything needing deep search to a slower path with a spoken filler first. 3. The agent says a short acknowledgement, "let me check that", the instant a lookup starts, because silence past a second reads as a dropped call, not a pause. 4. I set a hard timeout on the lookup, around 500ms, and fall back to a narrower cached answer or a human handoff rather than let retrieval blow the whole budget. 5. At 30,000 calls a day with real peaks around business hours, I provision retrieval capacity for peak concurrency, not the daily average, and alert on rising lookup latency.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Where does the 800ms actually go: endpointing, retrieval, generation, TTS? · Whether the budget is decomposed by stage, including retrieval, not stated as one number. 2. FAILURE — The KB lookup takes 900ms. What does the caller hear in that time? · Whether a filler or acknowledgement covers a lookup that blows the turn budget. 3. TRADEOFF — Pre-fetch likely answers to save time, or look up live and risk the budget? · Whether the candidate trades staleness for speed deliberately, not by default. 4. MEASUREMENT — At 30,000 calls a day, how do you catch a retrieval index quietly slowing down? · Whether retrieval latency is monitored at scale, not just checked once.

      Tip: If you've put a cache or a fast path in front of a slow lookup to protect a user-facing latency budget, that's the exact trade-off this question is testing. Don't claim you've shipped a voice RAG system. Say you understand retrieval latency has to fit inside the turn budget, not sit outside it.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Endpointing eats 150-400ms first - ~500ms hard timeout on lookups - Ack before the lookup, always - Provision for peak, not average

      Q15.6 — Design an ops agent that can restart services and open tickets without one bad call taking down more.

      Weak answer: "The agent has tools to query logs, query metrics, restart a service and open tickets. It plans which to call and is told to be careful before restarting."

      Why it fails: - "Told to be careful" is a text instruction, not a permission boundary stopping a restart against the wrong service. - No scoping per tool: restart_service is one global capability instead of being constrained to the incident's actual service.

      Instant rejection: - No human-approval gate on irreversible actions, only a request to "explain its reasoning" first. - No cap on tool calls per incident, so a stuck reasoning loop can keep retrying a failing action.

      Model answer: 1. Each tool is scoped at execution time to the specific service named in the incident, so restart_service is rejected outside it regardless of the model's reasoning. 2. Every tool call is classified upfront: querying logs and metrics auto-executes, but restarting a service or touching customer data needs explicit human approval. 3. That line is drawn by blast radius, not trust in the model: a wrong log query costs nothing, a wrong restart costs an outage, so the gate sits exactly there. 4. I log every tool call with the reasoning behind it, which makes a post-incident review possible, and cap calls per incident to stop a stuck retry loop. 5. I measure mean time-to-resolution and how often a human had to correct a proposed action, not tool-call count, because more calls per incident isn't obviously better.

      Follow-ups: 1. FAILURE — restart_service takes down a service it wasn't investigating. How did that happen? · Whether scoping is enforced structurally, not by trusting the model's reasoning. 2. TECHNICAL — Which actions need human approval, and which can the agent just run? · Whether there's an explicit line between low-risk and irreversible actions. 3. TRADEOFF — Autonomy resolves incidents faster; approval gates are safer but slower. Where's the line? · Whether a specific boundary is defended, tied to blast radius, not vague balance. 4. MEASUREMENT — After a month, is the agent resolving incidents faster, or just doing more? · Whether effectiveness is measured on outcome, not tool-call volume.

      Tip: If you've built role-based access control, or required a second approver for a production change, you've already drawn this kind of permission line. Don't claim you've built an ops agent. Say you'd scope tools at the execution layer and gate irreversible actions behind a human, not a prompt.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Scope tools to the incident - Restarts need human approval - Cap tool calls per incident - Measure correction rate, not - volume

      Q15.7 — Design a description pipeline generating 5,000 pieces weekly, all reviewed by a 4-person team.

      Weak answer: "The pipeline generates a description per product, sends it to a review queue, and a reviewer approves or edits before it goes live. We batch generation for cost."

      Why it fails: - Never checks whether 4 reviewers can actually clear 5,000 pieces a week; review is treated as a formality, not the bottleneck. - Every piece gets identical review effort regardless of risk, when review time is the scarce resource here.

      Instant rejection: - No plan for the backlog when generation, which finishes in an afternoon, outpaces a week of review capacity. - No detection for a reviewer rubber-stamping under queue pressure instead of genuinely reviewing.

      Model answer: 1. I do the arithmetic first: at roughly 40 pieces an hour, one reviewer covers about 280 a day, so four reviewers across a week cover close to 5,600, just over target. 2. That means review, not generation, is the ceiling; finishing generation in an afternoon doesn't help, so I optimise review-minutes-per-piece, not generation speed. 3. I tier content by risk before it reaches a reviewer: a templated colour or size variant gets a light sampled spot-check, freeing time for content needing judgement. 4. Anything with a factual or compliance-sensitive claim, like a health statement, always gets full review regardless of the capacity it costs. 5. I track each reviewer's edit rate over time, because a falling edit rate on the same content mix signals rubber-stamping, not improving quality.

      Follow-ups: 1. MEASUREMENT — At a realistic review pace, do 4 reviewers and 5,000 pieces actually work? · Whether the candidate does the arithmetic on reviewer throughput, not assumes capacity. 2. TECHNICAL — Generation finishes in an afternoon. What happens the rest of the week? · Whether review, not generation speed, is understood to cap end-to-end throughput. 3. TRADEOFF — A colour variant and a health claim: same review treatment? · Whether review effort is allocated by risk, the only way to stretch fixed headcount. 4. FAILURE — A reviewer starts rubber-stamping a huge queue. How would you notice? · Awareness that queue pressure creates a quality-erosion failure mode.

      Tip: If you've sized an on-call rotation or a code-review queue against real headcount and throughput, that's the same arithmetic this question wants. Don't invent a review pipeline you haven't run. Say you'd compute reviewer throughput first and tier effort by risk before trusting the target holds.

      Numbers worth quoting: - ~40 pieces/hour per reviewer - 4 reviewers ≈ 5,600/week capacity - Tier review effort by risk - Watch for falling edit rate

      Q15.8 — Design an eval and monitoring platform for 500,000 daily model calls across four AI features.

      Weak answer: "We'd log every request and response into a database and build a dashboard for accuracy, latency and cost. Engineers can query the logs for a specific request."

      Why it fails: - Logging everything at 500,000 calls a day with no retention or tiering strategy means the store grows without bound. - No sampling strategy for human review, and traces aren't tied to the prompt, model or index version that produced them.

      Instant rejection: - No access difference between an engineer debugging one incident and a weekly reviewer; both see the same raw query. - No separation between sensitive content, like customer PII, and ordinary operational metadata.

      Model answer: 1. Every call gets a structured trace at execution time: input, retrieved context or tool calls, output, and the live version ids of the prompt, model and index. 2. Full traces live in a fast store for about 30 days, the window an engineer actually needs for debugging, then roll into a cheaper, sampled long-term store. 3. For human review I stratify rather than sample randomly: every flagged or low-confidence trace queues for review, plus a small random baseline across the rest. 4. Access is layered by role: an engineer gets full-trace query access for their system, a reviewer gets the curated queue, leadership sees aggregated dashboards. 5. Traces carrying PII or contract text get their own access control and a shorter retention clock, with redaction applied before anything reaches a dashboard.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — 500,000 calls a day, all logged in full forever. What's wrong with that, as infrastructure? · Whether the candidate reasons about storage growth and proposes tiering. 2. TRADEOFF — A human can't review 500,000 traces a day. Who looks at what, and how chosen? · Whether review sampling is deliberate and risk-weighted, not left unspecified. 3. FAILURE — Quality drops on a Tuesday. Can you tell if it was a prompt, model or traffic shift? · Whether traces are tied to the version of every moving part, so regressions are attributable. 4. MEASUREMENT — Traces hold customer PII. Same table as everything else, or different? · Whether sensitive content gets distinct retention and access control.

      Tip: If you've designed logging tiers, sampling for a QA process, or access control around sensitive data, that's the same infrastructure judgement being tested. Don't claim you've built this exact platform. Say you'd tier storage, stratify review sampling, and version every trace rather than log everything forever.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 30-day full trace window - Stratified review, not random - Version every trace: prompt/model - Separate PII retention clock

      Q15.9 — A meeting-summary feature hits 200,000 MAU. Produce the cost and peak-capacity numbers before building.

      Weak answer: "This would cost a fair amount given long transcripts, so I'd watch usage closely, use a cheaper model since it's high-volume, and maybe cache summaries."

      Why it fails: - "A fair amount" has zero arithmetic behind it: no call volume, no token count, no cost figure at all. - Jumps to optimisation, a cheaper model, caching, before establishing the baseline cost those choices would improve on.

      Instant rejection: - No distinction between average and peak load, when meeting activity obviously clusters in business hours. - Treats input and output tokens as costing the same when they are usually priced very differently.

      Model answer: 1. 200,000 users at 3 summaries a week is 600,000 calls a week, about 85,700 calls a day, each sending 4,000 input tokens and returning 300 output tokens. 2. That's 85,700 x 4,000 ≈ 343 million input tokens a day, and 85,700 x 300 ≈ 25.7 million output tokens a day. 3. Using illustrative units, 1 per 1,000 input tokens and 4 per 1,000 output tokens, that's roughly 343,000 units from input and 103,000 from output, about 446,000 a day. 4. Output is a fraction of the tokens but close to a quarter of the cost, so trimming the summary length saves more per token than trimming the transcript. 5. 85,700 calls a day averages about 1 a second, but business-hours clustering means I'd provision for roughly a 5x peak, around 5 calls a second, before optimising.

      Follow-ups: 1. MEASUREMENT — Walk me through the arithmetic: calls a day, and tokens a day, in and out. · Whether MAU times frequency chains cleanly into daily calls and daily tokens. 2. TECHNICAL — This clusters in business hours. What peak multiplier do you provision for? · Whether average and peak are treated as different numbers driving different decisions. 3. TRADEOFF — Output usually costs more per token than input. Which lever saves more? · Whether the input/output cost asymmetry shapes which trim actually helps. 4. REFLECTION — A recurring meeting's transcript is 80% like last week's. Where does that show up? · Whether caching is placed as a later lever, not baked into the baseline estimate.

      Tip: If you've sized infrastructure from a usage forecast, users times frequency times resource per action, that's the identical chain of reasoning, different unit. Don't invent a real provider price. Say you'd use illustrative per-token units to get the shape of the cost right before optimising anything.

      Numbers worth quoting: - ~85,700 calls a day - ~343M input tokens a day - Output costs ~4x input, per token - Provision for ~5x peak

      Q15.10 — DRILL: design a ticket-search tool for 1.2 million tickets in 20 minutes, 5-second budget, cited answers. (practical drill)

      Weak answer: "N/A, this is a drill. A weak attempt draws boxes, never states a number, and treats citation as a formatting detail."

      Why it fails: - No scale estimate: corpus size and query volume are given then ignored, and the 5-second budget is never decomposed. - Citation is asserted, not verified, and nothing says how the growing 1.2M-ticket corpus stays fresh without a risky reindex.

      Instant rejection: - Draws a diagram and stops, waiting to be asked about scale, latency or failure modes instead of naming them. - Reaches the end without naming a trade-off unprompted, which is the entire behaviour this drill is checking for.

      Model answer: 1. The hard constraint is the citation: a suggestion must point to a real ticket an agent can open, so retrieval exists to make it trustworthy, not just findable. 2. Scale: roughly 4,500 searches a day across the support team, small next to the real constraint, a 1.2-million-ticket corpus growing by about 2,000 tickets a day. 3. I spend the 5 seconds: 500ms retrieval against the index, 300ms rerank, up to 3.5 seconds for generation and citation checks, streaming output as it's ready. 4. Tickets are indexed incrementally on close, not in a nightly batch, because a daily full reindex of 1.2 million tickets is itself an outage risk worth avoiding. 5. Two trade-offs unprompted: incremental indexing over a simpler nightly rebuild for freshness, and verifying citations mechanically over trusting the model.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Where does the 5 seconds actually go? Name the stages and estimate each. · Whether a latency budget is decomposed by default, without being asked. 2. FAILURE — 1.2 million tickets, growing daily. What stops a full reindex being an outage risk? · Whether index freshness at scale is a real concern, not an assumed one-time build. 3. TRADEOFF — What would you ship first in two weeks instead of the full design? · Whether a genuine MVP slice is named under a real constraint. 4. MEASUREMENT — A month on, how do you know agents use the suggestions, not ignore them? · Whether actual usage is instrumented, not just uptime or query volume.

      Tip: This is a drill, so lean on structure: name the constraint, estimate scale, decompose the budget, then design, the shape of any question in this module. Don't fabricate specific production experience. Say plainly you're working through it live, the way you would for any system you hadn't built before.

      Numbers worth quoting: - ~4,500 searches a day - 2,000 new tickets a day - 500ms retrieval, 300ms rerank - Incremental index, not nightly
