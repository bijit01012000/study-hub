      AI product reliability and human oversight

      0 sections · 4,509 words

      Module 10 · 10 questions · 1 practical drill

      Ship something useful, not just a working pipeline.

      Q10.1 — Why would you use an LLM here instead of writing deterministic, rule-based code?

      Weak answer: "The inputs are too varied for rules to cover, and LLMs handle that kind of ambiguity well, so it was the obvious choice for this feature."

      Why it fails: - Never names a case regex, rules or a classical classifier would have handled, so "too varied" is asserted, not shown. - No mention of cost, latency, or the deterministic option ever being built or measured against the LLM.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot describe a task the same team decided not to hand to an LLM, on this project or any other. - Treats "LLMs handle ambiguity" as a universal law rather than a claim to test on this input distribution.

      Model answer: 1. We were classifying inbound documents into about 30 categories, and the first thing I did was write a keyword and regex baseline, not build the LLM version. 2. The baseline handled roughly 70% of documents correctly on its own, because most of them were formulaic and the wording barely varied. 3. The LLM earned its place on the remaining 30%, the free-text edge cases where wording varied too much for rules to generalise. 4. So we shipped both: rules take the first pass, and only what fails or falls outside known patterns goes to the LLM. 5. If the baseline had covered 95% of cases I would have argued against adding an LLM at all, because it adds cost, latency and a new failure mode for a small gain.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What fraction of your real inputs would a well-written rule set have handled correctly? · Whether the ambiguity claim was measured or assumed. 2. FAILURE — What happens when the LLM is wrong on a case a deterministic system would have got right? · Awareness of the cost of losing determinism on the easy cases. 3. TRADEOFF — What did you give up in latency and cost by choosing an LLM over rules? · Whether the choice weighed real operating cost, not just capability. 4. MEASUREMENT — How did you measure that the LLM actually beat the deterministic baseline? · Evidence behind the decision, not a default preference.

      Tip: If you've picked a regex, a lookup table or a classic classifier over a fancier option because it was simpler and cheaper to run, that's the same judgement. Don't claim you've shipped an LLM feature if you haven't. Say you've made this build-versus-simple call before, and name the case.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 70% baseline, LLM for the rest - Rules first, LLM as fallback - Compare cost per 1,000 requests - Measure ambiguity, don't assume it

      Q10.2 — What failure rate is acceptable for this feature, and whose call is that?

      Weak answer: "We aimed for the model to be as accurate as possible and kept improving it until the error rate felt low enough to ship."

      Why it fails: - "Felt low enough" has no owner, no number, and no split by failure type or severity. - Treats accuracy as a single dial to maximise, not a budget agreed deliberately before building.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot name who signed off on the threshold, or say it was ever written down anywhere. - No distinction between a failure that mildly annoys a user and one that causes real harm or cost.

      Model answer: 1. Before we built anything, product and I agreed acceptable failure rates by category, because a wrong label costs far less than a wrong refund decision. 2. For low-stakes categorisation we accepted up to about 8% error, because a wrong guess just meant an extra click to correct it. 3. For anything touching money or a customer commitment, we set the bar near 1%, and anything above that had to fall back to a human. 4. Product owned the number, not me, because it's a business risk decision, and I owned proving whether we could actually hit it. 5. We reviewed the real rate weekly against that budget, and a category that drifted above its threshold got pulled back to manual review until fixed.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — How did you split failure types by how much each one actually costs? · Whether severity, not one blended accuracy number, drove the decision. 2. FAILURE — What happened the first time the system exceeded the agreed failure rate? · Whether the budget was monitored and enforced, not just stated once. 3. TRADEOFF — Who chose the threshold, engineering or the product owner, and why? · Whether this was treated as a product decision with real authority behind it. 4. MEASUREMENT — How do you know your measured failure rate reflects real production traffic? · Whether the eval set matches production, not a curated sample.

      Tip: If you've set an SLA, an error budget or an alert threshold together with a product owner, you've already made this kind of call. Don't claim you've picked an AI failure threshold. Say you've agreed an acceptable error or downtime budget with a non-engineering owner before.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 8% low-stakes, 1% high-stakes - Budget owned by product, not eng - Review the real rate weekly - Split failure by cost, not blend

      Q10.3 — When should this system abstain instead of giving an answer?

      Weak answer: "We always try to return the best answer the model can produce, because saying 'I don't know' feels like a worse experience for the user."

      Why it fails: - Treats abstention as purely bad UX, missing that a wrong confident answer is often worse than no answer. - No criteria at all for when the system should decline, defer, or ask a clarifying question instead.

      Instant rejection: - Says the system should "always try to help" with zero exceptions, even for out-of-scope or high-stakes requests. - Cannot name a real query type this system should refuse or defer on, only speaks in generalities.

      Model answer: 1. On our internal search tool, the system abstains and says it couldn't find a confident answer whenever retrieval returns nothing above a similarity threshold. 2. It also abstains when the question asks for something outside the indexed content, like live headcount numbers, instead of guessing from stale documents. 3. We treat abstaining as a real product outcome we track weekly, alongside accuracy, because a rising abstain rate usually means our index has a gap. 4. Early on we abstained too often and people stopped trusting the tool, so we lowered the threshold slightly and added a 'closest match' fallback instead. 5. The rule I use: a wrong confident answer that sends someone the wrong way is worse than an honest 'not sure, here's what I found instead'.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What signal actually triggers an abstain, and where does it live in the pipeline? · Whether abstention is a real decision point, not an afterthought. 2. FAILURE — Tell me about a query you saw that should have triggered an abstain but didn't. · Whether this was tested against real failures, not designed on paper only. 3. TRADEOFF — How do you stop over-cautious abstaining from making the feature useless? · Balance between safety and the product still being worth using. 4. MEASUREMENT — How do you measure whether your abstain rate is too high or too low? · Whether abstention itself is tracked as a metric, not left unmeasured.

      Tip: If you've written code that returns null, throws, or shows "no results" instead of guessing, you've already built an abstain path. Don't claim you've tuned an AI abstain threshold. Say you've chosen to fail loudly and honestly instead of silently guessing before.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Abstain rate as a tracked metric - No result beats a wrong one - Similarity threshold gates answer - Rising abstains signal index gap

      Q10.4 — How do you design the deterministic fallback for when the model fails?

      Weak answer: "If the model call fails or times out, we retry it a couple of times, and if it still fails we show the user an error message."

      Why it fails: - The "fallback" is just retrying the same failing component, not a genuinely different path. - An error message isn't a fallback, it just tells the user the feature failed with nothing offered instead.

      Instant rejection: - No distinction between the model erroring and the model succeeding with low confidence, one path handles both badly. - The fallback was never load-tested or exercised, so nobody knows if it actually works when the model is down.

      Model answer: 1. For our data extraction pipeline, the deterministic fallback is a narrower rule-based extractor that pulls only the handful of fields we can get reliably without a model. 2. If the LLM call errors, times out, or returns confidence below our threshold, we route to that rule-based extractor instead of blindly retrying the same call. 3. The fallback deliberately does less: it returns fewer fields than the LLM would, clearly marked as partial, rather than pretending to match full coverage. 4. We exercise the fallback path in staging every week with a forced failure, because a path nobody tests is a path that silently rots. 5. About 3% of production traffic hits the fallback in a normal week, and we alert if that climbs past 8%, because it usually means the model provider is degraded.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Walk me through what actually runs when your primary model call fails or scores low confidence. · Whether a real alternate path exists and was built, not assumed. 2. FAILURE — What's the last time your fallback path itself failed, and what did you do? · Whether the fallback is treated as production code with its own reliability. 3. TRADEOFF — What did the fallback cost you in scope or quality compared to the primary path? · Honesty about the fallback being narrower, not a free equivalent. 4. MEASUREMENT — How often does traffic actually hit the fallback path in production? · Whether the fallback rate is measured and watched, not invisible.

      Tip: If you've built a cache-miss path, a failover replica, or a degraded mode for when a downstream API is down, that's the same design instinct. Don't claim you've built an LLM fallback. Say you've designed a narrower path for a failing dependency, and explain how you tested it actually works.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 0.45 failure rate, no fallback - Fallback narrower, not equal - Test the fallback path weekly - Alert on rising fallback rate

      Q10.5 — Why can't you trust the model's own confidence score, and what do you use instead?

      Weak answer: "We ask the model to rate its own confidence from 0 to 1 alongside the answer, and only show results above 0.8 to users."

      Why it fails: - A number from the same model that produced the answer isn't a calibrated probability of being correct. - No independent signal, like sample agreement or a held-out labelled set, backs up that 0.8 cutoff.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot explain what "calibrated" means, or why a stated 0.8 doesn't mean an 80% chance of being right. - Never checked the self-reported score against actual outcomes on a labelled set.

      Model answer: 1. Early on we used the model's own stated confidence and found it nearly useless: it said 0.9 on answers that were wrong almost as often as ones it called 0.6. 2. Instead we built a labelled set of about 300 real production cases and measured how often the model was actually right at each score band it reported. 3. We also sample the same prompt a few times and use agreement across samples as a signal, because a question answered five different ways is genuinely uncertain. 4. For extraction we check output against a schema and simple sanity rules, since a malformed or out-of-range value is a stronger failure signal than any confidence number. 5. We recheck calibration monthly against fresh labelled cases, because a model or prompt change can quietly shift what a given score actually means.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — How did you build the labelled set you calibrate your confidence signal against? · Whether a real ground-truth comparison exists behind the threshold. 2. FAILURE — Tell me about a case where the model was confidently wrong. What did your score say? · Direct evidence the candidate has seen self-reported confidence fail. 3. TRADEOFF — What's the cost of a better signal, like sampling the same prompt multiple times? · Awareness that better calibration usually costs latency or money. 4. MEASUREMENT — How do you check, ongoing, that your confidence signal stays calibrated? · Whether calibration is monitored over time, not verified once and forgotten.

      Tip: If you've distrusted a system's own health check and built an independent monitor instead, that's the same scepticism this question wants. Don't claim you've calibrated a model's confidence. Say you'd never trust a component's self-report without checking it against real outcomes.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Self-score isn't calibrated - 300 labelled cases to check - Sample multiple times, compare - Recalibrate after prompt changes

      Q10.6 — Which actions need human approval before they happen, and where's that line?

      Weak answer: "Anything that feels high risk, like sending an email or approving a refund, goes to a human reviewer before it happens."

      Why it fails: - "Feels high risk" has no defined criteria, so the line moves with whoever is deciding that day. - No plan for a backed-up review queue, so "human approval" quietly becomes a rubber stamp under load.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot say what makes an action reversible or irreversible, usually the actual line that matters here. - Treats human review as free, with no account of reviewer workload, fatigue or approval rate.

      Model answer: 1. For our approvals workflow, the line was reversibility: anything the system could undo itself, like flagging a document for re-review, ran without a human. 2. Anything irreversible or touching money, like a refund over a set amount or an external communication, always went to a human first, no exceptions. 3. We also routed anything below our confidence threshold to a human, even actions that would otherwise auto-run, because low confidence and high stakes compound. 4. When the queue backed up during a spike, reviewers started approving almost everything unread, so we added a hard queue cap that paused new automation instead. 5. About 15% of items reached human review in a normal week, and we watched the reject rate closely, because a near-zero reject rate usually meant the gate wasn't doing anything.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What's the actual rule you use to decide an action needs approval, not a feeling? · Whether reversibility, cost or authority defines a real, working line. 2. FAILURE — What happened when your review queue backed up? Did approvals get rushed? · Whether the human gate stays meaningful under real load, not just on paper. 3. TRADEOFF — How much slower did the feature get once you added human approval, and was that accepted? · Honesty about the latency and throughput cost of a human gate. 4. MEASUREMENT — What fraction of reviewed actions actually get rejected, and what does that tell you? · Whether the approval rate is tracked to check the gate is doing real work.

      Tip: If you've required a second approver for a production deploy, a large payment, or a schema migration, you've already drawn this kind of line. Don't claim you've set an AI approval threshold. Say you've decided what needs a second sign-off before, using reversibility and cost as the test.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 0.35 failure rate, no human gate - Reversible actions skip review - Cap queue size under load - Watch reject rate, not volume

      Q10.7 — How do you collect user feedback on this feature without poisoning your own data?

      Weak answer: "We added a thumbs up and thumbs down button, and we feed that straight back in to keep improving the model over time."

      Why it fails: - Only unhappy or highly engaged users tend to click feedback buttons, so the signal is biased before it's used. - "Feed straight back in" with no review step means a wave of bad or gamed feedback can retrain the system on noise.

      Instant rejection: - No mention of who reviews feedback before it changes anything, so a small vocal minority can silently steer the system. - Cannot describe how they'd detect feedback being manipulated or unrepresentative of real usage.

      Model answer: 1. On our support assistant, thumbs up and down feed a review queue, not the model or prompt directly, because unsupervised retraining on raw feedback is how you get poisoned. 2. We separate implicit signal, like whether the user rephrased their question right after, from explicit thumbs, because explicit feedback skews towards frustrated users. 3. A person reviews a sample of negative feedback weekly, checks the complaint is actually valid, and only confirmed issues turn into prompt or retrieval changes. 4. We watch for feedback clustering from a small number of accounts, since a handful of people hammering thumbs down can look like a trend if unchecked. 5. Positive feedback gets sampled too, not just negative, because only listening to complaints biases every change towards whoever is most annoyed that week.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — How do you separate feedback that's a real quality signal from noise or spam? · Whether feedback goes through filtering or review before it acts on the system. 2. FAILURE — Tell me about a time feedback led you somewhere wrong before you caught it. · Direct evidence the candidate has seen a poisoned or biased signal in practice. 3. TRADEOFF — What's the cost of human review before feedback is used, versus applying it automatically? · Awareness that a review step is slower but protects against poisoning. 4. MEASUREMENT — How do you know your feedback sample actually represents your real user base? · Whether selection bias in who gives feedback is checked, not assumed away.

      Tip: If you've treated a spike of bug reports from one customer differently to a broad pattern across many, you've already handled this kind of bias. Don't claim you've tuned a model on feedback. Say you've reviewed user reports before acting on them instead of trusting raw signal automatically.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Feedback to a queue, not the model - Review before retrain, always - Check for feedback clustering - Sample positive feedback too

      Q10.8 — How do citations, explanations and disclosure change how users trust this feature?

      Weak answer: "We show a citation next to every answer, which builds user trust because they can see the answer is backed by a real source."

      Why it fails: - A citation next to a wrong answer, or one that doesn't actually support the claim, builds false confidence, not real trust. - No mention of checking the cited source actually supports what was said, so the citation itself is never verified.

      Instant rejection: - Assumes showing sources always increases trust correctly, with no awareness of automation bias over-trusting confident output. - Never considered that disclosure, like labelling output as AI-generated, can be a requirement independent of accuracy.

      Model answer: 1. On our internal search tool, every answer shows the source passage it was built from, and we check the passage actually supports the claim before showing it, not after a complaint. 2. We label answers built from one weak match differently to ones backed by several consistent sources, because a citation alone doesn't say how solid the match really is. 3. We disclose clearly that answers are AI-generated, separate from accuracy, because that's a trust and expectation-setting requirement even when the system works well. 4. We also watched for the opposite problem, users trusting a wrong answer more because it had a citation attached, which is automation bias, not real trust. 5. So we treat citations as something to verify before display, not a trust badge bolted on afterwards to make an unreliable answer look credible.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — How do you check a citation you show actually supports the claim next to it? · Whether citation accuracy is verified, not just present. 2. FAILURE — Tell me about a citation or explanation that made a wrong answer more convincing. · Awareness of automation bias and false reassurance from surface trust signals. 3. TRADEOFF — What's the cost, in latency or complexity, of verifying a citation before showing it? · Honesty that trustworthy disclosure has an engineering cost, not just a UI cost. 4. MEASUREMENT — How do you measure whether disclosure changes user behaviour, not just sentiment? · Whether trust signals are tested for real effect, not assumed to work.

      Tip: If you've added a source link, an audit trail, or a changelog to make a system's output checkable, that's the same instinct behind good citations. Don't claim you've designed AI disclosure. Say you've made a system's reasoning or source checkable before, rather than asking a user to trust it blindly.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Verify citation before showing it - Citations can create false trust - Disclosure separate from accuracy - Automation bias inflates trust

      Q10.9 — What has to be true before you're willing to launch this feature?

      Weak answer: "Once the model's accuracy on our test set was above our target, we felt confident enough to launch the feature."

      Why it fails: - One accuracy number on a test set says nothing about monitoring, fallback readiness, or production drift from that set. - No mention of who owns the feature after launch, or what triggers a rollback if things go wrong live.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot describe a rollback plan or a kill switch for the feature at all. - Test-set accuracy is the only launch gate, with no fallback rehearsal or live monitoring mentioned.

      Model answer: 1. Before launch we needed four things true, not just one: eval accuracy above target, the fallback tested under a forced failure, monitoring live with real alerts, and a named owner for incidents. 2. We ran a small share of real traffic through the feature in shadow mode first, comparing its output to the existing process without acting on it, to see the eval numbers held up live. 3. We agreed a rollback trigger in advance, a failure rate above a set number sustained for an hour, and rehearsed actually flipping the feature off before we ever needed to. 4. We launched to 5% of traffic first, not everyone at once, specifically so a bad surprise would be small and contained rather than a full incident. 5. Nobody signed off on launch without a name attached to who gets paged if this breaks at 2am, because a feature with no on-call owner isn't actually ready.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What monitoring had to be live and alerting before you'd flip this on for real users? · Whether observability is part of the launch bar, not added afterwards. 2. FAILURE — What's your rollback plan if the failure rate spikes an hour after launch? · Whether a concrete, rehearsed rollback exists, not an assumption things will be fine. 3. TRADEOFF — What did you deliberately leave out of the first launch to ship sooner, and why was that safe? · Whether scope was cut with a reasoned safety argument, not carelessly. 4. MEASUREMENT — How did you know your test-set accuracy would hold up against real production traffic? · Whether the eval set was checked against production distribution first.

      Tip: If you've shipped a feature behind a percentage rollout, with monitoring and a rollback plan agreed before launch, that's exactly this bar. Don't claim you've launched an AI feature. Say you've used a staged rollout, live monitoring and a rollback trigger for a risky change before.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Shadow mode before real traffic - 5% rollout, not 100% - Rollback trigger agreed pre-launch - Named on-call owner required

      Q10.10 — Design launch criteria, failure handling and rollback for a feature that auto-approves refunds. (practical drill)

      Weak answer: "N/A, this is a drill. A weak attempt names accuracy, a threshold and a gradual rollout, with no numbers and no rollback trigger."

      Why it fails: - No number anywhere: what accuracy, what threshold, what rollout share, what rollback trigger. Every noun is vague. - "Watching the numbers" isn't a rollback plan. No stated trigger, no named owner, no rehearsed kill switch.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot state a maximum value or risk tier the system may auto-approve without a human. - Treats this like a low-stakes launch, with no extra rigour for money moving without a human in the loop.

      Model answer: 1. I'd cap auto-approval at a fixed value, well under what one bad decision could meaningfully hurt the business, and route everything above that to a human by default, no exceptions. 2. Launch criteria: eval accuracy above target on a labelled historical set, the human-review fallback tested under simulated load, live monitoring wired up, and a named incident owner, before any real money moves. 3. I'd roll out to one low-value refund category at low volume first, watch it for a full week against a pre-agreed failure rate, then expand category and volume in steps. 4. Rollback trigger: failure or reversal rate above an agreed number sustained for a set window, which pages the owner and drops the feature back to full human review automatically, no debate in the moment. 5. After any rollback I'd require a written root cause and a re-launch gate, not a quiet re-enable once things look calmer, because lost trust from an auto-approve mistake has to be earned back, not assumed back.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What's the exact confidence threshold, and how did you validate it before launch? · Whether a real, tested number exists rather than a placeholder. 2. FAILURE — The auto-approval rate spikes 4x overnight. Walk me through what happens next. · Whether the candidate has a concrete incident response, not general reassurance. 3. TRADEOFF — Why not require human approval on every refund and skip the automation risk? · Whether the candidate can justify automation's value against its risk, not assume it. 4. MEASUREMENT — How do you tell normal variance apart from a real model regression in production? · Statistical thinking about noise versus signal in a live failure rate.

      Tip: If you've capped an automated process by value, staged a rollout by segment, or built an alert-triggered rollback for a risky change, say that plainly. Don't invent a refund-automation story. Say honestly you'd apply the same staged, capped, monitored approach you'd use for any high-stakes automated change.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Cap auto-approval by value - One category, low volume, first - Alert triggers automatic rollback - Root cause required to re-launch
