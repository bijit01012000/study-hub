    Leadership

    7 sections · 2,901 words

    Module 05 · 6 questions · 1 practical drill

    Lead through influence and the outcomes you enabled, not a title you once held.

    Leadership questions for engineers are rarely about managing people - they're about influence without authority, driving alignment, and multiplying your impact through others. The failure mode is claiming leadership for things the team already agreed with, or describing a title rather than an act of leadership. The follow-ups probe who resisted, what you conceded, and whether the outcome was a TEAM result you enabled rather than a solo win you're relabelling.

    Inside this module

    • Team-level outcome
    • Motivating others

    Q5.1 Tell me about a time you led a project or an initiative.

    What it really tests. Whether you can drive a piece of work through people and ambiguity to a result, owning the coordination and the outcome, not just the code.

    Weak answer

    "I led a project to build a new feature. I coordinated with the team, we split up the work, and everyone did their part. It went smoothly and we shipped on time. As the lead I made sure everything came together."

    Why it fails

    • "Coordinated" and "made sure everything came together" describe a role, not leadership acts.
    • No hard moment - everything "went smoothly", which is implausible and unmemorable.
    • No decision the candidate actually made as lead.
    • No conflict, blocker, or trade-off navigated.
    • No team-level outcome beyond "shipped".

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "What was the hardest call you had to make as the lead?" - A real leadership decision under uncertainty.
    2. Failure - "What went wrong, and how did you handle it?" - Leading through a problem, not just good weather.
    3. Technical - "How did you get people who reported to no one to deliver?" - Influence without authority.
    4. Measurement - "What did the project achieve, measurably?" - A team-level outcome.

    Answer framework

    1. The initiative and why it mattered.
    2. Your leadership scope.
    3. How you set direction and split work.
    4. The hardest call you made.
    5. A blocker you led through.
    6. How you kept people aligned.
    7. The measured outcome.
    8. What you learned about leading.

    Rejection signals

    • Describes a title, not a leadership act.
    • Everything went smoothly - no hard moment.
    • No decision they personally made.
    • Outcome is "we shipped", no result.

    Model answer skeleton

    I led our migration off a shared monolith database to per-service databases - cross-cutting work touching four teams, none of which reported to me. The hardest call was sequencing: two teams both wanted to go last because migration was painful, so I made the unpopular decision to migrate the highest-risk service first, while we had the most attention and energy, rather than easing in.

    I got the other teams to deliver by making their work legible - I built a shared migration checklist and a dashboard showing each service's progress, so it was visible and a bit competitive rather than nagging. Halfway through, a dual-write bug caused a data drift incident; I led the response, we paused, added reconciliation, and resumed rather than plough on. We finished a quarter's migration with one contained incident and no data loss, and the services could finally scale independently.

    What I learned is that leading without authority is mostly about making the work visible and taking the risky decisions yourself so others don't have to.

    Q5.2 Tell me about a time you influenced a decision without having the authority to make it.

    What it really tests. Persuasion without positional power - the defining senior-engineer skill of changing minds with evidence and coalition rather than a title.

    Weak answer

    "I suggested we should refactor a messy part of the codebase and the team agreed it was a good idea, so we did it. People generally listen to my suggestions because they trust my technical judgement."

    Why it fails

    • "The team agreed" - no resistance, so no real influence was needed.
    • "People generally listen to me" is a vague self-image, not an instance.
    • Refactoring everyone already wanted isn't a test of influence.
    • No stakes if the idea hadn't landed.
    • No evidence or method of persuasion.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Who was against it, and how did you bring them round?" - Real persuasion against resistance.
    2. Technical - "What evidence or demo did you use to make the case?" - Data-driven influence vs opinion.
    3. Measurement - "What was at stake if you hadn't got buy-in?" - Real consequence.
    4. Tradeoff - "What did you concede to win the rest?" - Negotiation, not just being right.

    Answer framework

    1. The decision and why it mattered.
    2. Who you needed to convince and their resistance.
    3. How you built the case (data, prototype, allies).
    4. What you conceded.
    5. How the decision changed.
    6. The outcome.
    7. What you learned about influence.

    Rejection signals

    • Vague "people listen to me".
    • No resistance overcome.
    • No evidence-based persuasion.
    • No outcome.

    Model answer skeleton

    I believed we needed a sprint to fix our deploy pipeline, but I was a mid-level engineer with no authority to divert a sprint, and my manager was resistant because it meant slipping a feature. Rather than argue in the abstract, I brought data: I tracked that we were losing roughly a day a week to manual deploys and rollback firefighting, and I built a weekend proof-of-concept of automated canary deploys to make it concrete rather than hypothetical.

    I pulled in two teammates who'd felt the same pain so it wasn't just my opinion. I conceded on scope - I proposed doing it incrementally over two sprints instead of one big bang, which lowered the perceived cost enough to get a yes. We built it, deploy-related incidents dropped noticeably, and release frequency went up.

    What I learned is that influence without authority runs on evidence, a working demo, and meeting the decision-maker's real constraint halfway - not on being loudest or most senior.

    Q5.3 Tell me about a time you mentored someone or helped a teammate grow.

    What it really tests. Whether you multiply your impact through others deliberately, teaching rather than solving for them, with evidence they actually grew.

    Weak answer

    "I always help the junior developers when they have questions and I review their code carefully. I'm happy to jump in and help whenever anyone's stuck. I think being approachable and helpful is part of being a good senior engineer."

    Why it fails

    • "Answer questions" and "jump in" are reactive, not mentoring.
    • No specific person or growth arc.
    • No deliberate investment in someone's development.
    • "Jump in and help" can mean doing it for them.
    • No evidence anyone actually grew.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Pick one person - what did they struggle with and what did you do?" - Specific, invested mentoring.
    2. Technical - "How did you help without just doing it for them?" - Teaching vs solving.
    3. Measurement - "How do you know they actually grew?" - A growth outcome, not intention.
    4. Reflection - "What did mentoring them teach YOU?" - Two-way growth and humility.

    Answer framework

    1. The person and their specific gap.
    2. Your deliberate approach.
    3. How you taught rather than took over.
    4. Evidence they grew.
    5. The team-level impact.
    6. What you learned.
    7. The ongoing relationship.

    Rejection signals

    • Only reactive "answered questions".
    • No specific person or outcome.
    • Did the work for them.
    • No evidence of growth.

    Model answer skeleton

    A junior on our team was strong at building features but froze during production incidents - he'd go quiet and wait for a senior to take over, so his debugging never developed. Rather than keep jumping in, I deliberately paired with him through the next few non-critical incidents and had HIM drive while I asked guiding questions - 'what changed recently, what's the metric telling you' - instead of handing him answers. I also walked him through two past postmortems so he saw the method, not just the fix.

    Over a couple of months he went from freezing to running lower-severity incidents solo and writing his own postmortems, and eventually he handled a real Sev-2 on call competently. The team-level impact was one more capable on-call engineer, which took genuine load off the seniors.

    What it taught me is patience - my instinct to just fix it fast had been quietly stunting him. We still catch up regularly, and he now mentors the newer joiner the same way.

    Q5.4 Tell me about a time you had to make an unpopular decision or drive alignment when the team disagreed.

    What it really tests. Whether you can make a hard call, bring people along, and hold the line under pushback - versus caving to keep everyone happy or steamrolling dissent.

    Weak answer

    "I made a decision the team wasn't thrilled about, but I explained my reasoning and eventually they accepted it because I was the one responsible. Sometimes as a leader you just have to make the call and people have to follow it."

    Why it fails

    • "They accepted it because I was responsible" leans on authority, not persuasion.
    • "People have to follow it" is a slightly authoritarian tell.
    • No account of engaging the dissent seriously.
    • No specifics about the decision or the disagreement.
    • No outcome validating (or challenging) the call.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Tradeoff - "What was the strongest objection, and did it have a point?" - Engaging dissent vs overriding it.
    2. Ownership - "How did you bring the dissenters along rather than just overruling?" - Alignment vs authority.
    3. Measurement - "Did the outcome prove the call right?" - Result over conviction.
    4. Reflection - "If it had gone wrong, how would you have handled that?" - Accountability for the hard call.

    Answer framework

    1. The decision and why it was unpopular.
    2. The strongest objection and whether it was valid.
    3. How you decided.
    4. How you brought dissenters along.
    5. How you held the line.
    6. The outcome.
    7. What you learned about hard calls.

    Rejection signals

    • Relies on authority to end disagreement.
    • Dismisses the objections rather than engaging them.
    • Caved as soon as it was uncomfortable.
    • No outcome.

    Model answer skeleton

    I pushed our team to freeze a beloved internal framework we'd built and migrate to a standard open-source one, which was genuinely unpopular - people had pride in the in-house tool and it worked. The strongest objection, which was fair, was that migration was pure cost with no new features for users.

    I didn't dismiss that; I acknowledged it openly and framed the decision around a cost they'd all felt: we were spending real time maintaining our framework and onboarding new hires took weeks because nothing was googlable. I brought the dissenters along by involving the most sceptical engineer in choosing the replacement, so it was partly his decision too, and by phasing the migration so it never blocked feature work. It paid off: onboarding time for new engineers roughly halved, and we stopped burning days on framework bugs. Had it gone wrong, I'd made clear it was my call to answer for, not the team's.

    What I learned is that holding the line means engaging the best objection honestly, not talking over it.

    Q5.5 Tell me about a time you had to motivate a demoralised team or deal with an underperforming teammate without being their manager.

    What it really tests. Whether you can lift a team's morale or address performance through peer influence and empathy, rather than complaining upward or ignoring it.

    Weak answer

    "When someone on the team wasn't pulling their weight, I let my manager know so they could deal with it. For team morale, I try to stay positive myself and hope it rubs off. I think a good attitude is contagious."

    Why it fails

    • Immediately escalates the underperformer to the manager - no peer attempt.
    • "Stay positive and hope it rubs off" is passive, not motivating.
    • No direct, constructive engagement with the person.
    • No specific situation or outcome.
    • Treats both morale and performance as someone else's job.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Before escalating, what did you try directly with them?" - Peer-level intervention vs passing it up.
    2. Technical - "What was actually behind their underperformance?" - Diagnosing the cause with empathy.
    3. Reflection - "For the demoralised team - what concrete thing did you do?" - Active vs passive morale work.
    4. Measurement - "Did it actually improve - how could you tell?" - A real change.

    Answer framework

    1. The situation.
    2. How you engaged directly and empathetically.
    3. What you found was really going on.
    4. What you did to help or motivate.
    5. When (if ever) escalation was right.
    6. The outcome.
    7. What you learned about peer leadership.

    Rejection signals

    • Escalates immediately without trying peer-level.
    • Passive "stay positive" as the whole strategy.
    • No empathy for why someone was struggling.
    • No outcome.

    Model answer skeleton

    A teammate had gone flat after the project he'd poured six months into was cancelled in a reorg - his output dropped and, because people noticed, it was starting to pull the team's mood down with it. I wasn't his manager, so escalating was the easy option, but demoralisation isn't a performance case to hand upward, it's someone who's lost the thread of why the work matters.

    So I spoke to him directly, not about his output but about the cancellation - 'that was rough, how are you doing with it'. What came out was that he'd quietly decided his work was disposable and had stopped investing. I couldn't un-cancel his project, but I could reconnect him to something that clearly mattered: I pulled him into a visible slice of the payments-reliability work and gave him a real piece to own, then made sure his progress got airtime in our demos. Over about a month he re-engaged and his output came back, and the team's mood lifted with it - I never needed to escalate.

    What I learned is that demoralisation is usually a broken sense of purpose rather than laziness, and the fix is genuine ownership of something that visibly matters, not a pep talk.

    Q5.6 Practical drill - Leadership

    Write a leadership story whose RESULT is a team-level outcome you enabled (not a personal win), and script the "what did you personally do" follow-up so your contribution is visible without stealing the team's credit.

    What it really tests. Forces the hardest leadership-story balance: a result that's about the team succeeding, while still making YOUR enabling contribution clear.

    Weak answer

    N/A - drill. A weak attempt either relabels a solo achievement as 'leadership' (no team outcome) or describes the team's win with no visible personal contribution (nothing to credit you for).

    Why it fails

    • The "leadership" story is actually a solo win with no team dimension.
    • Or the team succeeded but the candidate's own role is invisible.
    • No clear articulation of what the candidate uniquely enabled.
    • Can't answer "what did YOU do" without either shrinking or stealing credit.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Measurement - "What's the team-level result, stated as a number or a capability the team gained?" - A genuine team outcome, not a personal metric.
    2. Ownership - "What did you personally do that the team couldn't have without you?" - A visible, honest enabling contribution.
    3. Reflection - "Where's the line between your leadership and the team's execution?" - Crediting the team while owning your part.

    Answer framework

    1. Pick a story where the team achieved something.
    2. State the result as a team outcome.
    3. Identify the specific thing you enabled (direction, unblocking, a decision, coalition).
    4. Write the "what did you do" answer that names it without claiming the team's work.
    5. Credit the team explicitly.
    6. Rehearse holding both truths at once.

    Rejection signals

    • Result is a personal win, not the team's.
    • Personal contribution invisible.
    • Steals the team's credit when pushed.
    • Can't hold "the team did it AND I enabled it" together.

    Model answer skeleton

    Drill output: Story - 'Our four-team database migration finished in a quarter with one contained incident and services that could finally scale independently.' Team-level result: a capability the whole org gained, plus a clean risk-managed delivery, not my personal metric.

    What I personally enabled - the honest 'what did you do' answer: 'I set the sequencing (highest-risk service first), built the shared progress dashboard and checklist that made the work visible across teams, and led the incident response when the dual-write drift hit - but the four teams did their own migrations.' The line: I owned direction, visibility, and the risky calls; they owned execution.

    I'd explicitly say 'the teams did the actual migrations; my job was making it a coordinated effort instead of four uncoordinated ones.' Rehearsed so my contribution is clear without a single sentence that erases the teams.
