    Design a Job Scheduler

    3 sections · 3,322 words

    Three levels: Level 1 — The Shape, Level 2 — The Defence, Level 3 — The Edges.

    Level 1 — The Shape (Foundation)

    Pacing: 3 min ask · 5 min trace flow · 7 min read why.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Accept a job — cron or one-time, plus payload
    • Durably persist it — survives a crash
    • Exactly one execution attempt per due occurrence

    Non-Functional

    • Schedule outlives the scheduler process ← forces a durable Job Store
    • Exactly one active scheduler, N replicas ← forces leader election
    • At-least-once ≠ unsafe duplicates ← forces idempotency

    Agree These First

    500,000 active job definitions, ~48 fires/day each on average. 1 s granularity, 10 s lease TTL, 24 h idempotency retention. Payload execution and auth are someone else's system.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Job Submitter — Calling service or user — schedule + payload, right now
    • (2) Job Store — Durable · indexed by next_run_at — source of truth for what's due; 500,000 active job definitions
    • (3) Scheduler — Active leader · polls Job Store for due jobs, enqueues execution tasks
    • (4) Worker Pool — Executes the job payload — decoupled from the scheduling decision
    • submit — 202 Accepted · job_id only — not a result

    Data model: jobs job_id PK → next_run_at, payload.

    Line = flow type: SCHEDULE · job → dispatch; ACK · immediate response. Throughput: ~278/s avg · 833/s peak.

    Say It Like This

    "This is work that outlives the request — nothing here can assume any one process, including this one, is alive when it's due. So the schedule lives in a durable Job Store, not memory. A Scheduler polls it and hands due jobs to a separate Worker Pool, so one slow job can't stall the next scan."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (2) Durable Job Store Durable store, indexed by next_run_at In-memory timer → dies with the process
    (2) Who fires the job The system holds it — no process has to stay alive Caller fires it itself → ties the job to the caller's uptime
    (4) Separate Worker Pool Decoupled — a slow job can't stall the poller Scheduler executes itself → one slow webhook blocks the scan

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Executions 500,000 defs × ~48 fires/day avg 24,000,000/day
    Avg rate 24,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s ~278/s
    Peak (×3) peak 3x the average 833/s
    Duration assumed avg execution time 2.0 s
    Worker pool peak × duration, × 1.5 headroom 2,500 workers

    Round hard, out loud. ~278/s sounds calm — it's the 833/s peak and the 2,500-worker pool behind it that the design actually has to hold, every second, forever.

    Before You Draw Anything — The Four Moves

    1. Clarify, don't assume. Cron-style recurring, or a one-time future timestamp? What's the payload — a webhook call, a task run? — Two minutes of questions buys the whole design.
    2. Agree the scale out loud. "500,000 active job definitions, ~24 million executions a day, peaking near 833/s." Now every choice has a reason. — An unstated number is an undefendable design.
    3. Draw the happy path only. Submitter → Job Store → Scheduler → Worker Pool. Get agreement before adding anything. — Boxes you add before agreement are boxes you'll defend alone.
    4. Name one trade-off unprompted. Even at L1: "The schedule has to survive this process dying — so it lives in a durable store, never in memory." — This is the single move that separates L1 from a junior answer.

    What L1 Is Testing

    • Do you name the durability requirement unprompted?
    • Do you reach for leader election, not "just run one instance"?
    • Did you separate "the schedule is due" from "the work got done"?

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. A job is due in three days. Where does that fact live between now and then? (ref 2 — tests: nothing — not even this process — can hold the schedule in memory)
    2. The client that submitted the job restarts halfway through its shift. Does the job still fire? (ref 2 — tests: the core idea — no single process, including the caller, has to stay alive)
    3. Why is a slow webhook the Worker Pool's problem and not the Scheduler's? (refs 3,4 — tests: decoupling "is it due" from "is it done")

    At L1 you are being judged on one thing: can you produce a coherent shape and defend three choices inside it. Depth is page 2 — do not reach for it here. Ghosted boxes are what arrives next; you should be able to guess two of them.

    Level 2 — The Defence (Intermediate)

    Pacing: 5 min ask · 15 min trace flow · 10 min read why · 30 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Accept a job — cron or one-time, plus payload
    • Durably persist it — survives a crash
    • Exactly one execution attempt per due occurrence
    • Execute via a worker pool, decoupled from scheduling
    • Retry with backoff; dead-letter after bounded attempts

    Non-Functional

    • Schedule outlives the scheduler process ← forces a durable Job Store
    • Exactly one active scheduler, N replicas ← forces leader election
    • At-least-once ≠ unsafe duplicates ← forces idempotency
    • A leader's crash must auto-hand-off ← forces heartbeat/lease
    • Leadership needs a trustworthy clock ← forces a clock-skew gate

    Out of Scope — Say It Aloud

    • Job payload execution itself
    • Auth/authz for who may schedule what
    • Cross-job dependencies / DAG orchestration

    Agree These First

    500,000 active job definitions, ~48 fires/day each on average. 1 s granularity, 10 s lease TTL, 24 h idempotency retention. Payload execution and auth are someone else's system.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Job Submitter — Calling service or user — schedule + payload, right now
    • (2) Job Store — Durable · indexed by next_run_at — source of truth for what's due; 500,000 active job definitions
    • (3) Scheduler — Active leader · polls Job Store for due jobs, enqueues execution tasks; polls every 1 s · ~833 due rows/poll @ peak
    • (5) Leader Election — Distributed lock — only one active Scheduler; only one leader dispatches at a time
    • (6) Heartbeat / Lease Renewal — 10 s TTL lease · renewed every 3.3 s
    • (7) Task Queue — Decouples "due" from "done"
    • (4) Worker Pool — Executes the job payload — decoupled from the scheduling decision; 2,500 workers · peak 833 execs/s
    • (8) Execution Lease — Per-job-run lock — can't be claimed twice
    • (9) Job Status / Result Store — Queryable execution history
    • submit — 202 Accepted · job_id only — not a result

    Data model:

    • jobs job_id PK · schedule (cron | one-time) · next_run_at · payload · status
    • job_runs job_run_id PK · job_id · attempt · status · idempotency_key

    Line = flow type: SCHEDULE · job → dispatch; ACK · immediate response; CHECK · leader / gating. Throughput: ~278/s avg · 833/s peak.

    Say It Like This

    "This is work that outlives the request — nothing here can assume any one process, including this one, is alive when it's due. So the schedule lives in a durable Job Store, not memory. A Scheduler polls it and hands due jobs to a separate Worker Pool, so one slow job can't stall the next scan."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (2) Durable Job Store Durable store, indexed by next_run_at In-memory timer → dies with the process
    (2) Who fires the job The system holds it — no process has to stay alive Caller fires it itself → ties the job to the caller's uptime
    (4) Separate Worker Pool Decoupled — a slow job can't stall the poller Scheduler executes itself → one slow webhook blocks the scan
    (5) Leader election One elected leader polls — others stay standby Every replica polls → the same job enqueued N times
    (6) Heartbeat renewal Lease renewed every 3.3 s — a dead leader's seat opens Held forever once won → a crash stops scheduling silently
    (8) Per-job execution lease A narrower lock — closes the race election alone can't Election only → outgoing/incoming leader briefly both own one job

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Executions 500,000 defs × ~48 fires/day avg 24,000,000/day
    Avg rate 24,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s ~278/s
    Peak (×3) peak 3x the average 833/s
    Duration assumed avg execution time 2.0 s
    Worker pool peak × duration, × 1.5 headroom 2,500 workers
    Concurrency Little's Law: 833/s × 2.0 s — what 2,500 covers 1,667

    Storage was never the constraint here — concurrency was. Little's Law turns 833/s peak into 1,667 concurrent executions, which is why the worker pool is sized at 2,500, not 833.

    What Breaks — Say It Before They Ask

    • (3) Leader crashes mid-poll-cycle. Some due jobs it just read from ② might not have been enqueued into ⑦ yet. — Fix: the new leader re-polls ② from scratch — the source of truth, never the dead leader's memory
    • (8) Two leaders overlap. A partition or slow handoff lets the outgoing and incoming leader both try to enqueue the same due job. — Fix: ⑧ Execution Lease stops the duplicate from actually executing twice
    • (2) Polling falls behind. An extended leader outage leaves a backlog of now-due jobs piled up in ②. — Fix: drain it at a steady, bounded rate into ⑦ — never fire the whole backlog into ④ at once

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. A job is due in three days. Where does that fact live between now and then? (ref 2 — tests: nothing — not even this process — can hold the schedule in memory)
    2. The client that submitted the job restarts halfway through its shift. Does the job still fire? (ref 2 — tests: the core idea — no single process, including the caller, has to stay alive)
    3. Why is a slow webhook the Worker Pool's problem and not the Scheduler's? (refs 3,4 — tests: decoupling "is it due" from "is it done")
    4. You run three Scheduler replicas for availability. What stops all three from polling at once? (ref 5 — tests: leader election, not every replica racing to enqueue the same job)
    5. The leader hasn't crashed — it's just gone quiet for ten seconds. What happens next? (ref 6 — tests: the bounded lease/heartbeat window, not an indefinite wait)
    6. You already have leader election. Why does one due job also need its own lease? (ref 8 — tests: the narrower handoff race leader election alone can't close)

    Cover The Sheet · Answer Aloud

    Use it as revision: cover the right rail and the two panels to your left, then walk the numbered path out loud. If you cannot say why a box is there and what breaks it, that box is your gap.

    Level 3 — The Edges (Advanced)

    Pacing: 10 min ask · 25 min trace flow · 15 min read why · 40 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Accept a job — cron or one-time, plus payload
    • Durably persist it — survives a crash
    • Exactly one execution attempt per due occurrence
    • Execute via a worker pool, decoupled from scheduling
    • Retry with backoff; dead-letter after bounded attempts

    Non-Functional

    • Schedule outlives the scheduler process ← forces a durable Job Store
    • Exactly one active scheduler, N replicas ← forces leader election
    • At-least-once ≠ unsafe duplicates ← forces idempotency
    • A leader's crash must auto-hand-off ← forces heartbeat/lease
    • Leadership needs a trustworthy clock ← forces a clock-skew gate

    Out of Scope — Say It Aloud

    • Job payload execution itself
    • Auth/authz for who may schedule what
    • Cross-job dependencies / DAG orchestration

    Agree These First

    500,000 active job definitions, ~48 fires/day each on average. 1 s granularity, 10 s lease TTL, 24 h idempotency retention. Payload execution and auth are someone else's system.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Job Submitter — Calling service or user — schedule + payload, right now
    • (2) Job Store — Durable · indexed by next_run_at — source of truth for what's due; 500,000 active job definitions
    • (3) Scheduler — Active leader · polls Job Store for due jobs, enqueues execution tasks; polls every 1 s · ~833 due rows/poll @ peak
    • (5) Leader Election — Distributed lock — only one active Scheduler; only one leader dispatches at a time
    • (6) Heartbeat / Lease Renewal — 10 s TTL lease · renewed every 3.3 s
    • (13) Clock Sync Check — Gates leadership — reject drift > 2 s
    • (7) Task Queue — Decouples "due" from "done"
    • (4) Worker Pool — Executes the job payload — decoupled from the scheduling decision; 2,500 workers · peak 833 execs/s
    • (8) Execution Lease — Per-job-run lock — can't be claimed twice
    • (12) Idempotency Store — Records executed job_run_ids; 576 MB @ 24 h retention
    • Retry + Backoff — Failed executions retried with increasing delay
    • Dead Letter Queue — Exhausted retries land here — not retried forever
    • (9) Job Status / Result Store — Queryable execution history
    • submit — 202 Accepted · job_id only — not a result

    Data model:

    • jobs job_id PK · schedule (cron | one-time) · next_run_at · payload · status
    • job_runs job_run_id PK · job_id · attempt · status · idempotency_key

    Line = flow type: SCHEDULE · job → dispatch; ACK · immediate response; CHECK · leader / gating; AT-LEAST-ONCE · retry cost. Throughput: ~278/s avg · 833/s peak.

    Say It Like This

    "This is work that outlives the request — nothing here can assume any one process, including this one, is alive when it's due. So the schedule lives in a durable Job Store, not memory. A Scheduler polls it and hands due jobs to a separate Worker Pool, so one slow job can't stall the next scan."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (2) Durable Job Store Durable store, indexed by next_run_at In-memory timer → dies with the process
    (2) Who fires the job The system holds it — no process has to stay alive Caller fires it itself → ties the job to the caller's uptime
    (4) Separate Worker Pool Decoupled — a slow job can't stall the poller Scheduler executes itself → one slow webhook blocks the scan
    (5) Leader election One elected leader polls — others stay standby Every replica polls → the same job enqueued N times
    (6) Heartbeat renewal Lease renewed every 3.3 s — a dead leader's seat opens Held forever once won → a crash stops scheduling silently
    (8) Per-job execution lease A narrower lock — closes the race election alone can't Election only → outgoing/incoming leader briefly both own one job
    (12) Idempotency, not just a lease job_run_id keyed — the side effect survives a replay Trust the lease alone → a retry after a slow success fires twice
    (13) Clock-skew gate Leadership needs a trustworthy clock — drift > 2 s disqualifies Elected but drifting → fires jobs before they're actually due

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Executions 500,000 defs × ~48 fires/day avg 24,000,000/day
    Avg rate 24,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s ~278/s
    Peak (×3) peak 3x the average 833/s
    Duration assumed avg execution time 2.0 s
    Worker pool peak × duration, × 1.5 headroom 2,500 workers
    Concurrency Little's Law: 833/s × 2.0 s — what 2,500 covers 1,667
    Failover 10 s TTL + 3.3 s renewal buffer, worst case ~13.3 s
    Delayed 833/s × ~13.3 s outage — delayed, never lost ~11,111

    At-least-once has a real, nameable cost. 576 MB of idempotency keys and up to ~11,111 delayed — not lost — jobs during a worst-case ~13.3 s failover. Neither number is optional once you accept the honest guarantee.

    What Breaks — Say It Before They Ask

    • (3) Leader crashes mid-poll-cycle. Some due jobs it just read from ② might not have been enqueued into ⑦ yet. — Fix: the new leader re-polls ② from scratch — the source of truth, never the dead leader's memory

    • (2) Polling falls behind. An extended leader outage leaves a backlog of now-due jobs piled up in ②. — Fix: drain it at a steady, bounded rate into ⑦ — never fire the whole backlog into ④ at once
    • (12) Worker crashes mid-side-effect. It sent a webhook, then crashed before acknowledging — at-least-once redelivery re-executes the task. — Fix: ⑫ Idempotency Store makes the retried side effect safe, not just retried
    • (13) Clock skew. A leader's clock drifts ahead of the group and fires jobs before they're actually due by consensus time. — Fix: ⑬ Clock Sync Check should have disqualified it from leadership before this could happen

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. A job is due in three days. Where does that fact live between now and then? (ref 2 — tests: nothing — not even this process — can hold the schedule in memory)
    2. The client that submitted the job restarts halfway through its shift. Does the job still fire? (ref 2 — tests: the core idea — no single process, including the caller, has to stay alive)
    3. Why is a slow webhook the Worker Pool's problem and not the Scheduler's? (refs 3,4 — tests: decoupling "is it due" from "is it done")
    4. You run three Scheduler replicas for availability. What stops all three from polling at once? (ref 5 — tests: leader election, not every replica racing to enqueue the same job)
    5. The leader hasn't crashed — it's just gone quiet for ten seconds. What happens next? (ref 6 — tests: the bounded lease/heartbeat window, not an indefinite wait)
    6. You already have leader election. Why does one due job also need its own lease? (ref 8 — tests: the narrower handoff race leader election alone can't close)
    7. A lease already stops duplicate scheduling. Why does execution still need an idempotency key? (ref 12 — tests: at-least-once delivery can still redeliver to a worker, lease or not)
    8. Two replicas both won an election, on different clocks. What's actually wrong? (ref 13 — tests: leadership needs a trustworthy clock, not just a won election)
