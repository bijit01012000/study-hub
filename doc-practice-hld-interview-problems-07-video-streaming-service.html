    Design a Video Streaming Service

    3 sections · 3,119 words

    Three levels: Level 1 — The Shape, Level 2 — The Defence, Level 3 — The Edges.

    Level 1 — The Shape (Foundation)

    Pacing: 3 min ask · 5 min trace flow · 7 min read why.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Browse catalog, request playback
    • Get a manifest → stream, adapt quality
    • Resume playback where left off

    Non-Functional

    • Bytes never touch app servers ← forces direct CDN delivery
    • Playback starts fast, stays smooth ← forces a ladder + client ABR
    • Global audience, uneven latency ← forces CDN near viewers

    Agree These First

    5,000,000 daily active viewers · ~15% concurrent at peak · ~5 Mbps average bitrate · a 20,000-title catalog averaging ~15 GB/title · ~2,000 new titles/day needing encoding. Round, illustrative numbers, not any named company's.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client — Video player — browses, plays, and (rarely) uploads
    • Playback Service — "What can I watch?" Issues a manifest, nothing else
    • GET /play/{title}
    • Manifest Store — title → renditions → CDN URLs — small, metadata only
    • CDN Edge — Client fetches bytes directly here — never through Playback Service
    • Origin Store — Every encoded rendition lives here · CDN pulls on a cache miss; 300 TB catalog

    Data model: manifests title_id PK → renditions[].

    Line weight = traffic: DATA · 3.75 Tbps peak; CONTROL · modest QPS. Direct from CDN Edge, never Playback Service.

    Say It Like This

    "Bytes never touch my servers — that's what the CDN is for. My servers only handle the control plane: catalog and a manifest telling the client where to fetch the video. Encoding into a rendition ladder happens asynchronously, ahead of time — never live per request — because one encode serves every future viewer. The client then adapts quality itself off that ladder, picking a rendition for its own bandwidth, so nobody buffers on a slow line or wastes bandwidth on a fast one."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (3) CDN delivery Direct from CDN Edge — bytes never enter your app tier at all Proxy through app servers → 3.75 Tbps through code built for modest QPS · tiny payload
    (4) Pre-encode, ahead of time Async, before viewing — one encode serves every future viewer Encode-on-request → every viewer waits minutes before frame one
    (7) Adaptive bitrate A ladder, client picks — each viewer adapts to their own network One fixed quality → buffers on slow lines, wastes bandwidth on fast ones

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Concurrent 5,000,000 DAU × 15% concurrent at peak 750 000
    Peak egress 750 000 × 5 Mbps average bitrate 3.75 Tbps
    After CDN 3.75 Tbps × 5% — the ~5% edge misses, 95% hit ≈187.5 Gbps
    Catalog 20 000 titles × ~15 GB encoded/title 300 TB
    New/day 2 000 new titles/day × ~15 GB 30 TB/day

    The headline number is 3.75 Tbps. Proxying that through app servers built for API requests is physically absurd — that is the entire reason a CDN exists.

    Before You Draw Anything — The Four Moves

    1. Clarify, don't assume. Live streaming or on-demand? Scope to VOD — it's the simpler, more common interview framing, and say so. — Two minutes of questions buys the whole design.
    2. Agree the scale out loud. "5 M daily viewers, ~15% concurrent at peak, ~5 Mbps average." Now every choice has a reason. — An unstated number is an undefendable design.
    3. Draw the happy path only. Client → Playback Service → Manifest Store → CDN Edge → Origin Store. Get agreement before adding anything. — Boxes you add before agreement are boxes you'll defend alone.
    4. Name one trade-off unprompted. "I'd never route video bytes through my own servers — that's what the CDN is for." — This is the single move that separates L1 from a junior answer.

    What L1 Is Testing

    • Do you keep the bytes off your own servers, unprompted?
    • Do you split control-plane (manifest) from data-plane (bytes) on sight?
    • Did you ask VOD vs live before drawing anything?

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. Why doesn't the video stream through your API servers? (ref 3 — tests: control-plane/data-plane split awareness)
    2. Why encode into six different quality levels instead of one? (ref 7 — tests: adaptive-bitrate reasoning)
    3. Encoding a new title takes real time. When does that happen relative to when a viewer hits play? (ref 6 — tests: async/ahead-of-time reasoning, not live transcoding)
    4. What does the client actually get back when it hits play? (ref 2 — tests: do you know what a manifest actually contains)
    5. A brand-new viewer opens the app for the first time. Walk me through everything before a single frame plays. (refs 1,3 — tests: can you walk the happy path in order)

    At L1 you are being judged on one thing: can you produce a coherent shape and defend three choices inside it. Depth is page 2 — do not reach for it here. Ghosted boxes are what arrives next; you should be able to guess two of them.

    Level 2 — The Defence (Intermediate)

    Pacing: 5 min ask · 15 min trace flow · 10 min read why · 30 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Browse catalog, request playback
    • Get a manifest → stream, adapt quality
    • Resume playback where left off
    • Upload a title (creator side, briefly)
    • Encode into a bitrate ladder (ABR)

    Non-Functional

    • Bytes never touch app servers ← forces direct CDN delivery
    • Playback starts fast, stays smooth ← forces a ladder + client ABR
    • Global audience, uneven latency ← forces CDN near viewers
    • Encoding is slow, costly per title ← forces async, ahead of time
    • Storing every quality forever is large ← forces a bounded ladder

    Out of Scope — Say It Aloud

    • Payments, subscriptions, billing
    • Recommendation ranking
    • DRM internals

    Agree These First

    5,000,000 daily active viewers · ~15% concurrent at peak · ~5 Mbps average bitrate · a 20,000-title catalog averaging ~15 GB/title · ~2,000 new titles/day needing encoding. Round, illustrative numbers, not any named company's.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client — Video player — browses, plays, and (rarely) uploads
    • Playback Service — "What can I watch?" Issues a manifest, nothing else
    • GET /play/{title}
    • Manifest Store — title → renditions → CDN URLs — small, metadata only
    • CDN Edge — Client fetches bytes directly here — never through Playback Service; (8) miss ≈5%
    • Origin Store — Every encoded rendition lives here · CDN pulls on a cache miss; 300 TB catalog
    • (5) Upload Ingest — Raw upload lands here — not the core flow
    • (6) Encoding Pipeline — Async, ahead of time — one encode serves every future viewer; renditions written; ≈30 TB/day new
    • origin pull

    Data model:

    • titles title_id PK · metadata · status
    • manifests title_id PK · renditions[] · CDN paths
    • resume_state user+title → position, device — tiny

    Line weight = traffic: DATA · 3.75 Tbps peak; CONTROL · modest QPS; ENCODING · async write. Direct from CDN Edge, never Playback Service.

    Say It Like This

    "Bytes never touch my servers — that's what the CDN is for. My servers only handle the control plane: catalog and a manifest telling the client where to fetch the video. Encoding into a rendition ladder happens asynchronously, ahead of time — never live per request — because one encode serves every future viewer. The client then adapts quality itself off that ladder, picking a rendition for its own bandwidth, so nobody buffers on a slow line or wastes bandwidth on a fast one."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (3) CDN delivery Direct from CDN Edge — bytes never enter your app tier at all Proxy through app servers → 3.75 Tbps through code built for modest QPS · tiny payload
    (4) Pre-encode, ahead of time Async, before viewing — one encode serves every future viewer Encode-on-request → every viewer waits minutes before frame one
    (7) Adaptive bitrate A ladder, client picks — each viewer adapts to their own network One fixed quality → buffers on slow lines, wastes bandwidth on fast ones
    (1) Control vs data plane Kept separate — modest-QPS control plane, Tbps-scale data plane One unified service → a low-traffic control API absorbs Tbps-scale load

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Concurrent 5,000,000 DAU × 15% concurrent at peak 750 000
    Peak egress 750 000 × 5 Mbps average bitrate 3.75 Tbps
    After CDN 3.75 Tbps × 5% — the ~5% edge misses, 95% hit ≈187.5 Gbps
    Catalog 20 000 titles × ~15 GB encoded/title 300 TB
    New/day 2 000 new titles/day × ~15 GB 30 TB/day

    After the CDN absorbs ~95% of requests at the edge, origin only sees ≈187.5 Gbps — still large, but ~20× more tractable than the full peak. That is what justifies a CDN, not more origin servers.

    What Breaks — Say It Before They Ask

    • (8) New-release thundering herd. A hyped title drops; a huge audience watches in hour one — cold at every edge, so cache-fill can't keep pace. — Fix: pre-warm CDN edges ahead of the release — don't rely on organic cache-fill for a known spike
    • (6) Encoding backlog. 2 000 new titles/day queue for transcoding; a capacity dip grows the backlog and delays new content. — Fix: a priority queue lets a scheduled release jump ahead of routine uploads
    • (2) Stale manifest. A title re-encodes or its CDN URLs change, but a cached manifest still points at the old ones. — Fix: manifest TTL/versioning — the same staleness-window honesty as elsewhere on this sheet

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. Why doesn't the video stream through your API servers? (ref 3 — tests: control-plane/data-plane split awareness)
    2. Why encode into six different quality levels instead of one? (ref 7 — tests: adaptive-bitrate reasoning)
    3. Encoding a new title takes real time. When does that happen relative to when a viewer hits play? (ref 6 — tests: async/ahead-of-time reasoning, not live transcoding)
    4. A hyped new release drops and a million people watch in the first hour. What's different about that traffic from a title that's been out a year? (ref 8 — tests: cold-cache/thundering-herd-on-release awareness, and the pre-warming fix)

    Cover The Sheet · Answer Aloud

    Use it as revision: cover the right rail and the two panels to your left, then walk the numbered path out loud. If you cannot say why a box is there and what breaks it, that box is your gap.

    Level 3 — The Edges (Advanced)

    Pacing: 10 min ask · 25 min trace flow · 15 min read why · 40 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Browse catalog, request playback
    • Get a manifest → stream, adapt quality
    • Resume playback where left off
    • Upload a title (creator side, briefly)
    • Encode into a bitrate ladder (ABR)

    Non-Functional

    • Bytes never touch app servers ← forces direct CDN delivery
    • Playback starts fast, stays smooth ← forces a ladder + client ABR
    • Global audience, uneven latency ← forces CDN near viewers
    • Encoding is slow, costly per title ← forces async, ahead of time
    • Storing every quality forever is large ← forces a bounded ladder
    • A whole CDN can fail ← forces multi-CDN

    Out of Scope — Say It Aloud

    • Payments, subscriptions, billing
    • Recommendation ranking
    • DRM internals

    Agree These First

    5,000,000 daily active viewers · ~15% concurrent at peak · ~5 Mbps average bitrate · a 20,000-title catalog averaging ~15 GB/title · ~2,000 new titles/day needing encoding. Round, illustrative numbers, not any named company's.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client — Video player — browses, plays, and (rarely) uploads
    • Playback Service — "What can I watch?" Issues a manifest, nothing else
    • GET /play/{title}
    • Manifest Store — title → renditions → CDN URLs — small, metadata only
    • CDN Edge — Client fetches bytes directly here — never through Playback Service; (8) miss ≈5%
    • Origin Store — Every encoded rendition lives here · CDN pulls on a cache miss; 300 TB catalog
    • (10) 2nd CDN / Failover — Health-checked failover if the primary CDN is down
    • (5) Upload Ingest — Raw upload lands here — not the core flow
    • (6) Encoding Pipeline — Async, ahead of time — one encode serves every future viewer; renditions written; ≈30 TB/day new
    • (11) Priority Encoding Queue — Priority lane — a scheduled release jumps the backlog
    • (9) signed, TTL'd URL — no hotlinking
    • (12) Resume State — position, device — tiny state
    • origin pull

    Data model:

    • titles title_id PK · metadata · status
    • manifests title_id PK · renditions[] · CDN paths
    • resume_state user+title → position, device — tiny

    Line weight = traffic: DATA · 3.75 Tbps peak; CONTROL · modest QPS; ENCODING · async write; FAILOVER · rare. Direct from CDN Edge, never Playback Service.

    Say It Like This

    "Bytes never touch my servers — that's what the CDN is for. My servers only handle the control plane: catalog and a manifest telling the client where to fetch the video. Encoding into a rendition ladder happens asynchronously, ahead of time — never live per request — because one encode serves every future viewer. The client then adapts quality itself off that ladder, picking a rendition for its own bandwidth, so nobody buffers on a slow line or wastes bandwidth on a fast one."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (3) CDN delivery Direct from CDN Edge — bytes never enter your app tier at all Proxy through app servers → 3.75 Tbps through code built for modest QPS · tiny payload
    (4) Pre-encode, ahead of time Async, before viewing — one encode serves every future viewer Encode-on-request → every viewer waits minutes before frame one
    (7) Adaptive bitrate A ladder, client picks — each viewer adapts to their own network One fixed quality → buffers on slow lines, wastes bandwidth on fast ones
    (1) Control vs data plane Kept separate — modest-QPS control plane, Tbps-scale data plane One unified service → a low-traffic control API absorbs Tbps-scale load
    (10) Multi-CDN failover A second CDN, health-checked — one outage doesn't kill playback Single CDN provider → its outage is your outage, for everyone
    (11) Priority encoding queue A scheduled release jumps the queue — a hyped drop isn't stuck behind routine uploads Strict FIFO → a hyped title waits behind yesterday's backlog
    (9) Signed CDN URLs Tokenized, TTL'd URL — can't be hotlinked or replayed forever Public, static URL → anyone with the link streams it free, forever

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Concurrent 5,000,000 DAU × 15% concurrent at peak 750 000
    Peak egress 750 000 × 5 Mbps average bitrate 3.75 Tbps
    After CDN 3.75 Tbps × 5% — the ~5% edge misses, 95% hit ≈187.5 Gbps
    Catalog 20 000 titles × ~15 GB encoded/title 300 TB
    New/day 2 000 new titles/day × ~15 GB 30 TB/day

    Storage (300 TB) and even origin egress (≈187.5 Gbps) are tractable. The 3.75 Tbps peak — and the fact that it must never touch your application code — is the entire problem this sheet solves.

    What Breaks — Say It Before They Ask

    • (8) New-release thundering herd. A hyped title drops; a huge audience watches in hour one — cold at every edge, so cache-fill can't keep pace. — Fix: pre-warm CDN edges ahead of the release — don't rely on organic cache-fill for a known spike
    • (6) Encoding backlog. 2 000 new titles/day queue for transcoding; a capacity dip grows the backlog and delays new content. — Fix: a priority queue lets a scheduled release jump ahead of routine uploads
    • (2) Stale manifest. A title re-encodes or its CDN URLs change, but a cached manifest still points at the old ones. — Fix: manifest TTL/versioning — the same staleness-window honesty as elsewhere on this sheet
    • (10) CDN provider outage. A whole CDN provider goes down — every viewer routed there loses playback. — Fix: multi-CDN with health-check-based failover
    • (12) Resume-position race. Pause on one device, resume on another — eventually-consistent sync can resume at the wrong spot. — Fix: accept a small, stated staleness window here — cheap to be wrong about, unlike the byte stream

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. Why doesn't the video stream through your API servers? (ref 3 — tests: control-plane/data-plane split awareness)
    2. Why encode into six different quality levels instead of one? (ref 7 — tests: adaptive-bitrate reasoning)
    3. Encoding a new title takes real time. When does that happen relative to when a viewer hits play? (ref 6 — tests: async/ahead-of-time reasoning, not live transcoding)
    4. A hyped new release drops and a million people watch in the first hour. What's different about that traffic from a title that's been out a year? (ref 8 — tests: cold-cache/thundering-herd-on-release awareness, and the pre-warming fix)
    5. Your CDN provider goes down. What happens to playback? (ref 10 — tests: multi-CDN/failover awareness)
    6. How does "resume where you left off" work if you paused on your phone and picked it up on your TV? (ref 12 — tests: cross-device sync awareness, and recognising this is small state next to a massive byte stream)
