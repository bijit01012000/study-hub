    Observability and deployment

    9 sections · 3,713 words

    Module 09 - 9 questions - 1 practical drill

    Connect telemetry and release safety to decisions somebody can act on.

    The gap this round finds: candidates who "added logging and Grafana" versus ones who can tell you their SLOs, what pages them at 3am, and how a bad deploy gets caught and reverted automatically. Follow-ups punish dashboards nobody acts on and alerts that cry wolf.

    Inside this module: SLIs & SLOs - Golden signals - Alert quality - Safe deployment

    Q9.1 - How do you know your service is healthy right now, without a user telling you?

    What it really tests: Whether observability is designed around user-facing signals and SLOs, or just infrastructure graphs.

    Weak answer: "We had dashboards in Grafana showing CPU, memory, and request counts, so we could see if something looked off."

    Why it fails: - CPU/memory are cause metrics, not user-facing health. - "Looked off" implies a human watching dashboards, not alerting. - No SLO defining what "healthy" means. - No error-rate or latency-percentile signal tied to user experience.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - Which single metric best reflects the user's experience? - Symptom-based (latency/error) over resource metrics. 2. MEASUREMENT - What's your SLO, and are you meeting it? - Defined target for healthy. 3. TRADEOFF - Why is high CPU not necessarily a problem? - Cause vs symptom distinction. 4. FAILURE - Who watches the dashboard at 3am - or does something page you? - Alerting vs eyeballing.

    Answer framework: 1. Define health as user-facing SLIs 2. Error rate and latency percentiles first 3. Resource metrics for diagnosis, not paging 4. Alert on symptoms, not raw resources 5. SLO as the target 6. Dashboards for humans, alerts for machines 7. Error budget tracking

    Rejection signals: - Health = CPU/memory graphs. - No SLO. - Relies on humans watching dashboards. - Confuses cause metrics with symptoms.

    Model answer skeleton: I define health from the user's perspective, not the machine's. The signals I trust are the golden ones - request error rate and latency percentiles (p95/p99) on the critical endpoints - because those are what the user actually feels.

    Those roll up into an SLO, for example 99.9% of checkout requests succeed under 500ms, so 'healthy' is a defined target I'm either meeting or burning my error budget against, not a vibe.

    CPU and memory I keep, but as diagnostic metrics, because high CPU with healthy latency and errors is fine - it's a cause signal, not a symptom, and paging on it produces noise.

    Nobody watches dashboards at 3am; the SLO burn rate and the symptom metrics drive alerts that page me, and the dashboards are for investigating once I'm paged.

    So I know it's healthy because the SLI is inside the SLO and my error budget is intact, and I'll be paged automatically the moment the user-facing signal degrades.

    Q9.2 - Logs, metrics, and traces - when do you reach for each?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate understands the three pillars and their distinct roles, not just that they exist.

    Weak answer: "We used all three - logs for errors, metrics for monitoring, and traces for performance. They all help you understand the system."

    Why it fails: - Generic one-liner per pillar, no real decision criteria. - Doesn't explain the cardinality/cost trade-offs. - No sense of the workflow: metric alerts -> trace localises -> log details. - No mention of when each is the wrong tool.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - You get a latency alert - which pillar do you go to first and next? - The metric-trace-log workflow. 2. TRADEOFF - Why not just log everything and skip metrics? - Cost/cardinality of logs vs metrics. 3. MEASUREMENT - What can a trace tell you that a log can't? - Cross-service latency attribution. 4. FAILURE - What do you deliberately NOT put in logs? - PII / volume discipline.

    Answer framework: 1. Metrics = cheap aggregate signal, alert on these 2. Trace = localise which service/hop in a request 3. Logs = detailed context for the specific failure 4. Workflow: alert on metric, localise with trace, confirm with log 5. Cost/cardinality trade-offs 6. PII discipline in logs

    Rejection signals: - One vague sentence per pillar. - No workflow linking them. - Logs everything, ignoring cost. - Puts PII in logs.

    Model answer skeleton: I think of them as a workflow, not three separate things. Metrics are cheap, aggregated, and low-cardinality, so they're what I alert on - an error-rate or latency-percentile threshold pages me.

    Once paged, I go to traces to localise: a distributed trace shows which service or which hop in the request added the latency or threw the error, so I'm not guessing across ten services.

    Then I go to logs for the fine-grained context of that specific failing request - the exact input, the stack trace - pulled by the trace id so I read the relevant lines, not everything. So the loop is: metric tells me something's wrong, trace tells me where, log tells me why.

    I don't just log everything and skip metrics, because high-cardinality logging is expensive and slow to aggregate - you can't alert on a p99 by grepping logs.

    And I'm disciplined about never logging PII or secrets, and about sampling high-volume traces, so observability doesn't become a cost or compliance problem itself.

    Q9.3 - What makes an alert good, and how do you stop alert fatigue?

    What it really tests: Alert quality - actionable, symptom-based, low-noise - versus a wall of ignored pages.

    Weak answer: "We set up alerts on our key metrics so we'd get notified if anything went wrong. We had alerts on CPU, memory, disk, errors, and latency."

    Why it fails: - Alerting on every resource metric guarantees noise and fatigue. - No distinction between page-worthy and ticket-worthy. - No concept of actionable alerts. - Alerting on causes (CPU) not symptoms creates false alarms.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TRADEOFF - Which of those alerts should actually wake someone up? - Page vs ticket severity. 2. TECHNICAL - Why is a high-CPU alert often noise? - Symptom-based alerting. 3. MEASUREMENT - What makes an alert actionable? - Every page has a clear response. 4. FAILURE - How do you handle an alert that fires every night and is ignored? - Killing noisy alerts.

    Answer framework: 1. Alert on user-facing symptoms, not causes 2. Every page must be actionable and urgent 3. Separate page from ticket/dashboard 4. Tie alerts to SLO burn rate 5. Tune thresholds to cut false positives 6. Delete or downgrade noisy alerts 7. On-call reviews alert quality

    Rejection signals: - Alerts on every resource metric. - No page-vs-ticket distinction. - Keeps noisy alerts that get ignored. - Alerts on causes, producing false alarms.

    Model answer skeleton: A good alert is urgent, actionable, and tied to user impact - if it fires, a human needs to do something now. The fastest route to alert fatigue is paging on every resource metric, because CPU spikes, memory sawtooths, and disk fills all fire constantly without the user ever noticing, and a team that gets paged for non-problems starts ignoring pages - including the real one.

    So I alert primarily on symptoms: error rate and latency breaching the SLO, or the error budget burning too fast. Those map directly to 'users are hurting'.

    I separate severity: a genuine SLO breach pages someone awake, while slow disk growth is a ticket for business hours, not a 3am wake-up.

    Every page links to a runbook so it's actionable, not just informative. And I treat a noisy alert as a bug - if something fires every night and gets swiped away, I either fix the underlying cause, retune the threshold, or delete it, because an ignored alert is worse than no alert.

    On-call regularly reviews which alerts actually helped.

    Q9.4 - Walk me through how you deploy a change safely to production.

    What it really tests: Whether deploys are gradual and observable with automated rollback, or a risky all-at-once push.

    Weak answer: "We had a CI/CD pipeline that ran tests and then deployed to production. If something broke we'd deploy a fix or roll back manually."

    Why it fails: - All-at-once deploy exposes 100% of users to a bad change instantly. - No canary or progressive rollout. - Rollback is manual and reactive, after damage is done. - No automated health-gating of the rollout.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TRADEOFF - Canary, blue-green, or rolling - which and why? - Deployment-strategy fit. 2. MEASUREMENT - What metrics gate the canary, and what triggers auto-rollback? - Automated health gating. 3. TECHNICAL - How does a feature flag decouple deploy from release? - Deploy vs release separation. 4. FAILURE - A bad change passes tests - how does the rollout still catch it? - Observability-driven safety.

    Answer framework: 1. CI gates (tests, contracts) 2. Progressive rollout (canary %) 3. Automated metric gates (error/latency) 4. Auto-rollback on regression 5. Feature flags to decouple release 6. Full rollout on green 7. Fast manual revert as backstop 8. Post-deploy monitoring

    Rejection signals: - Deploys to 100% at once. - No canary or progressive rollout. - Rollback is purely manual/reactive. - No metric gating of the rollout.

    Model answer skeleton: Tests in CI are table stakes but they don't catch everything, so the deploy itself has to be safe. I roll out progressively: a canary first, sending a small slice of production traffic - say 5% - to the new version while the rest stays on the old one.

    Automated gates watch the canary's error rate and latency against the baseline, and if they regress the pipeline rolls back automatically before most users ever see it - the rollback is a machine decision on metrics, not a human noticing damage after the fact.

    If the canary stays healthy, it expands in stages to 100%. I also separate deploy from release using feature flags: the code can ship dark and I switch the feature on gradually, so a problem is a flag flip away from off, no redeploy needed.

    For high-stakes changes I use blue-green so I can flip all traffic back to the known-good environment instantly.

    And I keep watching the SLIs for a while after full rollout, because some issues only appear at full scale.

    Q9.5 - Define SLI, SLO, and SLA, and tell me how you actually used one.

    What it really tests: Whether these are memorised definitions or tools the candidate has used to make decisions (error budgets).

    Weak answer: "SLI is an indicator, SLO is an objective, SLA is an agreement. They're about measuring service quality and uptime."

    Why it fails: - Correct-ish definitions but no usage. - No error-budget concept. - Never used an SLO to make a real decision. - No concrete numbers.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - What's an error budget and how did it change a decision? - Error-budget-driven decisions. 2. MEASUREMENT - Give me a real SLI and SLO with numbers. - Concrete, not abstract. 3. TRADEOFF - You're burning your error budget fast - what changes? - Feature-velocity vs reliability trade-off. 4. REFLECTION - Why is 100% uptime the wrong target? - Cost-of-nines understanding.

    Answer framework: 1. SLI = measured signal 2. SLO = internal target on it 3. SLA = external contract with penalties 4. Error budget = 1 - SLO 5. Use budget to gate risk/velocity 6. Concrete numbers 7. Why not 100%

    Rejection signals: - Definitions with no application. - Never heard of error budgets. - No real numbers. - Thinks 100% uptime is the goal.

    Model answer skeleton: An SLI is the measured signal - say the percentage of checkout requests served successfully under 500ms. An SLO is the internal target on that SLI - 99.9% over 30 days.

    An SLA is the external, contractual promise to customers, usually looser than the SLO with financial penalties if breached, so the SLO is the stricter internal early-warning.

    The tool I actually used is the error budget: if the SLO is 99.9%, I have a 0.1% budget of failures, and how I spend it drives decisions.

    When we had budget to spare we shipped features aggressively; when a rough month burned the budget fast, we froze risky launches and spent the sprint on reliability instead - the budget turned 'reliability vs velocity' from an argument into a number.

    And 100% uptime is the wrong target because the cost of each extra nine grows exponentially while the user barely notices, so a deliberate SLO below 100% is what lets you move fast within a known reliability envelope.

    Q9.6 - How do you handle a database migration as part of a deploy without downtime?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate knows migrations and code deploys must be decoupled and backward-compatible (echoes M3 but from the deploy angle).

    Weak answer: "We ran the migration as part of the deploy pipeline so the schema and code updated together."

    Why it fails: - Coupling migration and code deploy means old code can hit new schema (or vice versa) during rollout. - No backward-compatible / expand-contract sequencing. - A rollback of code can't easily roll back a migration. - No consideration of the mixed-version window during a rolling deploy.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - During a rolling deploy, old and new code run at once - how must the schema behave? - Backward/forward compatibility. 2. FAILURE - You need to roll back the code - what about the migration? - Irreversible-migration awareness. 3. TRADEOFF - Describe the expand/contract sequence across deploys. - Multi-step safe migration. 4. MEASUREMENT - How do you backfill without locking or blocking the deploy? - Batched, decoupled backfill.

    Answer framework: 1. Decouple migration from code deploy 2. Expand: additive schema first 3. Deploy code compatible with both 4. Backfill in batches 5. Switch reads 6. Contract in a later deploy 7. Keep each step reversible 8. Handle mixed-version window

    Rejection signals: - Migration and code deploy coupled in one step. - Ignores the mixed-version window. - No rollback story for the schema. - Big-bang migration with locks.

    Model answer skeleton: I decouple the migration from the code deploy, because during a rolling deploy old and new instances run simultaneously, so the schema has to be compatible with both versions at once.

    I use expand/contract: first an additive, backward-compatible migration - add the new nullable column - deployed on its own, so old code ignores it and new code can use it.

    Then I deploy code that writes both old and new, backfill existing rows in small batches over time so I never take a long lock or block the pipeline, switch reads to the new column once the backfill is verified, and only in a later, separate deploy do I contract by dropping the old column.

    This ordering means a code rollback at any point is safe, because the schema still supports the previous version - the classic trap is a deploy that adds a NOT NULL column and new code together, so rolling the code back leaves old code violating the new constraint.

    Each step is independently reversible, which is what makes the whole thing zero-downtime.

    Q9.7 - Your dashboards look green but users are complaining. How is that possible?

    What it really tests: Understanding of monitoring blind spots - averages hiding tails, wrong aggregation, missing the user's perspective.

    Weak answer: "Maybe the users were wrong, or it was a client-side issue on their end rather than our service."

    Why it fails: - Reflexively blames the user instead of questioning the monitoring. - Doesn't consider averages masking a bad tail. - No awareness of aggregation hiding a specific segment. - Assumes green dashboards = reality.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - How can a healthy average latency hide a broken experience? - Percentiles vs averages. 2. MEASUREMENT - How might one region or one customer be broken while the global metric is fine? - Aggregation/segmentation blind spots. 3. FAILURE - What could be failing that your metrics don't even measure? - Unknown-unknowns / coverage gaps. 4. TRADEOFF - How do you monitor the actual user experience? - Real-user / synthetic monitoring.

    Answer framework: 1. Distrust the green first 2. Averages hide tail latency 3. Global metrics hide per-segment failures 4. Missing instrumentation on the failing path 5. Client-side or CDN issues invisible to backend 6. Add percentile + segmented views 7. Real-user and synthetic monitoring

    Rejection signals: - Blames the user. - Only looks at averages. - No segmentation of metrics. - Assumes dashboards capture everything.

    Model answer skeleton: My first instinct is to distrust the green, not the user. The most common cause is averaging: a mean latency of 200ms looks fine while p99 is 8 seconds, so a real slice of users has a terrible experience that the average hides - I always look at percentiles, not means.

    Second is aggregation across segments: a global success rate of 99% can hide that one region, one API version, or one big customer is at 50%, because they're a small fraction of total volume - so I slice by region, endpoint, and tenant.

    Third, the failing path might not be instrumented at all - if a specific error isn't counted, the dashboard can't show it, which is a coverage gap, not health.

    And plenty happens outside my backend's view: a CDN issue, a broken client build, or DNS problems make users suffer while my server metrics are pristine.

    So I add percentile and segmented views, and I run real-user monitoring and synthetic probes from the outside in, because the only ground truth is the user's actual experience, not my server's self-report.

    Q9.8 - How do you make logs actually useful when you have hundreds of services?

    What it really tests: Structured logging, correlation, centralisation, and log levels - versus unsearchable free-text spew.

    Weak answer: "We logged important events and errors to files, and could search through them when we needed to debug something."

    Why it fails: - Per-service log files don't scale to hundreds of services. - Free-text logs are hard to query and aggregate. - No correlation id to follow a request. - No structured format or centralisation.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - How do you follow one request across services in the logs? - Correlation/trace id in logs. 2. TRADEOFF - Why structured (JSON) logs over free text? - Queryability. 3. MEASUREMENT - How do you control log volume and cost? - Levels, sampling, retention. 4. FAILURE - What should never appear in a log line? - PII/secret hygiene.

    Answer framework: 1. Structured (JSON) logging 2. Correlation/trace id on every line 3. Centralise/ship to one searchable store 4. Consistent levels 5. Sample and set retention for cost 6. Redact PII/secrets 7. Make logs queryable by field 8. Link logs to traces

    Rejection signals: - Per-service files, no centralisation. - Free-text, unqueryable logs. - No correlation id. - Logs PII, or logs everything at debug.

    Model answer skeleton: At hundreds of services, grepping per-host files is hopeless, so logs have to be structured, correlated, and centralised. Structured means JSON with consistent fields - level, service, trace id, and typed attributes - so I can query 'all errors for tenant X on endpoint Y' instead of regexing free text.

    Every line carries the trace id, so I can reconstruct one request's journey across every service it touched, and jump straight from a trace to its logs.

    All logs ship to a central store - ELK or a hosted equivalent - that's actually searchable and retained per policy. I control volume and cost with disciplined levels (info in prod, debug behind a flag), sampling on high-volume noisy paths, and shorter retention for chatty logs than for audit ones.

    And I'm strict that PII, tokens, and secrets never hit a log line - that's both a cost and a compliance issue - so sensitive fields are redacted at the logging boundary.

    The goal is that during an incident I can find the exact lines for the exact failing requests in seconds, keyed by trace id.

    Q9.9 - [DRILL] Define the SLO, the top three alerts, and the deploy strategy for a new payments API you're launching tomorrow.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate can assemble observability and safe-deploy practice into a concrete launch plan.

    Weak answer: N/A - drill. A weak attempt says "monitor errors and deploy carefully" with no specifics.

    Why it fails: - No numeric SLO. - Alerts on causes (CPU) rather than user-facing symptoms. - No progressive rollout or auto-rollback. - No error budget or runbook.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. MEASUREMENT - What's your SLI and the exact SLO number? - Concrete target. 2. TRADEOFF - Which alert pages someone at 3am and which is a ticket? - Severity discrimination. 3. FAILURE - What auto-rolls-back the canary? - Automated deploy safety.

    Answer framework: 1. SLI + numeric SLO 2. Error budget 3. Alert 1: SLO burn/error rate (page) 4. Alert 2: latency p99 breach (page) 5. Alert 3: dependency/DLQ or saturation (ticket/page by severity) 6. Canary rollout with metric gates 7. Auto-rollback thresholds 8. Runbook links

    Rejection signals: - No numeric SLO. - Alerts on CPU/memory only. - All-at-once deploy. - No auto-rollback.

    Model answer skeleton: Drill output: SLI is the fraction of payment requests that succeed under 800ms; SLO is 99.95% over 30 days, giving a small error budget I'll watch from hour one.

    Alert 1 pages: error rate breaching the SLO burn rate - a fast burn wakes someone because failed payments are revenue and trust.

    Alert 2 pages: p99 latency over 800ms sustained for a few minutes, since a slow payment path fails checkouts even without errors.

    Alert 3 is severity-scaled: DLQ growth or a downstream processor circuit breaker opening - page if it's blocking payments, ticket if it's a degraded non-critical path.

    Deploy: canary to 5% of traffic with automated gates comparing the canary's error rate and p99 to the baseline, auto-rollback if either regresses beyond threshold, then staged expansion to 25/50/100% on green, with the new capture path behind a feature flag so I can kill it instantly without a redeploy.

    Every alert links a runbook, and I'll baby-sit the SLIs through the first full traffic peak.
