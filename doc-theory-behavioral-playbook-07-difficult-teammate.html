    Difficult teammate

    7 sections · 2,927 words

    Module 07 · 6 questions · 1 practical drill

    Handle a hard colleague with no villain and a clear account of your own part.

    This is conflict's close cousin, but the focus is an ongoing working relationship rather than a single disagreement. The trap is unmistakable: make the teammate the villain and yourself the reasonable one, and the interviewer hears how you'll talk about THEM one day. The strongest answers show empathy for what was driving the teammate's behaviour, a direct attempt to work it out peer-to-peer, and a lesson about your own part in the friction.

    Inside this module

    • The no-villain test
    • Your part in it
    • Repair the relationship

    Q7.1 Tell me about a time you had to work with a difficult teammate.

    What it really tests. Whether you can work productively with someone hard to work with, understand what drove their behaviour, and address it directly without making them a villain.

    Weak answer

    "I had a teammate who was really hard to work with - arrogant, dismissive of other people's ideas, and never listened. I just kept my head down and worked around them as much as possible until the project was over. Some people you just can't change."

    Why it fails

    • Pure character attack - "arrogant, dismissive, never listened".
    • "Kept my head down and worked around them" avoids rather than addresses.
    • "Some people you can't change" is a fatalistic, uncurious stance.
    • No attempt to understand what drove the behaviour.
    • No growth or resolution - just endurance.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "What do you think was actually driving their behaviour?" - Curiosity and empathy vs villainising.
    2. Ownership - "What did you try directly, rather than working around them?" - Addressing vs avoiding.
    3. Reflection - "What was YOUR part in the friction?" - Owning half the relationship.
    4. Measurement - "Did anything actually improve between you?" - A relationship outcome.

    Answer framework

    1. The teammate and the friction.
    2. What you think drove their behaviour.
    3. How you engaged directly.
    4. What you tried.
    5. Your own contribution to the friction.
    6. How it improved (or how you made it workable).
    7. What you learned about difficult relationships.

    Rejection signals

    • Teammate is a one-dimensional villain.
    • Avoided rather than addressed.
    • No empathy or curiosity about the cause.
    • No ownership of their own part.

    Model answer skeleton

    I worked with an engineer who shot down ideas hard in reviews and design meetings, and it was easy to read as arrogance. Rather than just endure it, I tried to understand what was behind it, and paying attention I noticed he only got sharp when changes touched a fragile legacy service HE'd get paged for at 3am - he wasn't dismissive, he was scared of instability he'd have to own alone.

    My own part in the friction was that I'd been proposing changes to that service without acknowledging his on-call burden, which put him on the defensive. So I changed my approach: I started looping him in early and explicitly addressing 'here's how we keep this safe for on-call', and the sharpness largely evaporated because his real concern was being heard. We ended up collaborating well, and he became one of my better reviewers.

    What I learned is that 'difficult' is usually a rational response to a pressure you haven't clocked, and that my framing was half the problem.

    Q7.2 Tell me about a time a teammate wasn't pulling their weight. How did you handle it?

    What it really tests. Whether you address underperformance directly and fairly, seeking the cause, rather than silently resenting it, covering for them, or running to the manager first.

    Weak answer

    "A teammate was slacking off and I ended up picking up their work to keep the project on track. I didn't want to make a big deal of it or get anyone in trouble, so I just quietly did extra to cover the gap. It was frustrating but I got it done."

    Why it fails

    • "Quietly covered the gap" enables the problem and breeds resentment.
    • "Didn't want to make a big deal" avoids the necessary direct conversation.
    • "Slacking off" assumes laziness without seeking the cause.
    • Solves it with personal overwork.
    • The teammate never gets a chance to address it.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "How did you find out whether it was capability, workload, or something personal? Why you didn't just assume laziness." - Diagnosing before judging.
    2. Ownership - "Did you ever actually talk to them about it?" - Direct conversation vs silent resentment.
    3. Reflection - "Covering for them silently - did that help or enable it?" - Recognising enabling behaviour.
    4. Measurement - "What changed after you addressed it?" - A real outcome.

    Answer framework

    1. The situation.
    2. How you raised it directly and privately.
    3. What you found was really going on.
    4. What you did to help or set expectations.
    5. When escalation became right.
    6. The outcome.
    7. What you learned.

    Rejection signals

    • Silently covered and resented it.
    • Never spoke to the person.
    • Assumed the cause without checking.
    • No outcome or change.

    Model answer skeleton

    A teammate's contributions had dropped and I was quietly picking up the slack, getting more resentful each week - which helped nobody. Instead of continuing to cover or going straight to our lead, I spoke to him directly and privately, opening with 'I've noticed things seem harder lately, what's going on' rather than an accusation.

    It turned out he was struggling with a family situation and also stuck on a part of the codebase he didn't understand and was too proud to ask about. So I did two things: I paired with him to unblock the technical side, and I quietly flagged to our manager that he might need some flexibility for a while, with his knowledge. His output recovered over a few weeks, and I stopped silently absorbing his work, which had only been hiding the problem.

    What I learned is that covering for someone feels kind but is actually enabling, and that a direct, curious conversation almost always beats either resentment or escalation as the first move.

    Q7.3 Tell me about a time someone's working style clashed with yours.

    What it really tests. Whether you can adapt to different working styles and find a working accommodation, rather than insisting everyone match your way.

    Weak answer

    "I like to move fast and this teammate was really slow and over-cautious, wanting to discuss everything for ages before doing anything. It clashed with how I work. Eventually they came around to being a bit more decisive, which helped."

    Why it fails

    • Frames the other style as simply wrong ("slow", "over-cautious").
    • "They came around to my way" implies the clash was resolved by them changing.
    • No adaptation on the candidate's own part.
    • No recognition that the cautious style might have value.
    • Positions the candidate's style as the correct default.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "What did YOU adapt, not just them?" - Two-way accommodation.
    2. Technical - "How did you find a way of working that used both strengths?" - Synthesis over one side winning.
    3. Reflection - "What was actually good about their more cautious style?" - Valuing a different approach vs dismissing it.
    4. Measurement - "Did the combined approach produce a better result?" - An outcome from the accommodation.

    Answer framework

    1. The two styles.
    2. Why they clashed.
    3. What you recognised was valuable in theirs.
    4. What you adapted.
    5. How you found a shared way of working.
    6. The outcome that used both strengths.
    7. What you learned about style differences.

    Rejection signals

    • Own style framed as objectively right.
    • Resolution is the other person changing.
    • No self-adaptation.
    • No value seen in the other approach.

    Model answer skeleton

    I like to prototype fast and iterate; a teammate I paired with wanted to design thoroughly and discuss trade-offs before writing any code, and early on it genuinely frustrated me. But watching the results, I had to admit his caution caught two design flaws on a previous project that my speed would've shipped straight into production.

    So rather than push him to be faster, I adapted: on the risky, hard-to-reverse parts - schema and API contracts - we did it his way and designed up front, and on the reversible, low-risk parts - internal logic, UI tweaks - we did it my way and prototyped. That split played to both strengths instead of one of us winning. The result was that we shipped a payments feature with noticeably fewer post-launch bugs than my solo work usually had, without the whole thing bogging down in analysis.

    What I learned is that a style clash is usually two calibrations that are each right for different risk levels, and the trick is matching the style to the reversibility of the decision.

    Q7.4 Tell me about a time you had to give difficult feedback to a peer.

    What it really tests. Whether you can deliver hard feedback to an equal directly and kindly, focused on behaviour and impact, rather than avoiding it or being clumsily blunt.

    Weak answer

    "I had to tell a peer their code quality wasn't good enough. I was honest and direct about it - I told them straight that the code had a lot of problems and needed to improve. I believe in being upfront even if it's uncomfortable for the other person."

    Why it fails

    • "Told them straight the code had a lot of problems" is blunt to the point of unkind.
    • Focuses on a verdict ("not good enough") rather than specific behaviour and impact.
    • "Uncomfortable for them" treats their discomfort as acceptable collateral.
    • No thought about how the feedback would be received or actioned.
    • No outcome - did anything actually change?

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "How did you make it about specific behaviour, not a verdict on them?" - Actionable feedback vs a judgement.
    2. Ownership - "How did you deliver it so they could hear it, not just so you'd said it?" - Feedback for their benefit vs to discharge yours.
    3. Reflection - "What was their reaction, and how did you handle it?" - Managing the response.
    4. Measurement - "Did their behaviour actually change afterwards?" - A real outcome.

    Answer framework

    1. The feedback and why it mattered.
    2. How you prepared it (specific, behaviour-focused).
    3. How and where you delivered it.
    4. How you made it hearable.
    5. Their reaction and how you handled it.
    6. The change that followed.
    7. What you learned about peer feedback.

    Rejection signals

    • Blunt to the point of unkind.
    • A verdict, not specific actionable behaviour.
    • Delivered to discharge frustration, not to help.
    • No change resulted.

    Model answer skeleton

    A peer I respected had a habit of merging large PRs late on Fridays with thin descriptions, and it was quietly causing weekend incidents that on-call - sometimes me - had to clean up. Telling him 'your code quality is bad' would've been both unfair and useless.

    So I made it specific and about impact, not character: I asked to chat privately and said 'the last three Friday-evening merges each caused a weekend page, and the thin descriptions made them slow to diagnose - could we move big merges to earlier in the week?'. I led with the concrete pattern and the cost to on-call, not a verdict on him. His first reaction was a bit defensive, so I made clear I rated his work and this was one specific habit, which took the heat out. He shifted to merging big changes midweek with proper descriptions, and our weekend pages dropped.

    What I learned is that hard feedback lands when it's a specific behaviour tied to a real impact, delivered privately, and clearly on the person's side.

    Q7.5 Tell me about a time a senior person was dismissive of your input or blocked your work.

    What it really tests. Whether you can advocate for your ideas against seniority with composure and evidence, without either backing down entirely or turning it into a fight.

    Weak answer

    "A senior engineer kept shutting down my ideas in meetings, probably because I was more junior. It was demoralising. I mostly stopped sharing ideas with him and just went to other people instead, since he clearly wasn't interested in what I had to say."

    Why it fails

    • Assumes the motive ("because I was junior") without checking.
    • "Stopped sharing ideas with him" is disengagement, not advocacy.
    • Routes around the person rather than addressing it.
    • No attempt to make the ideas land better.
    • Ends demoralised and avoidant, with no growth.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Reflection - "Did you check whether it was really about seniority, or something else?" - Checking assumptions about motive.
    2. Technical - "How did you try to make your input harder to dismiss?" - Strengthening the case vs giving up.
    3. Ownership - "Did you address it with him directly?" - Confronting vs avoiding.
    4. Measurement - "What changed - did you ever get through?" - An outcome.

    Answer framework

    1. The situation.
    2. Whether you checked the real reason.
    3. How you strengthened your input to be harder to dismiss.
    4. How you raised it directly.
    5. How it shifted.
    6. The outcome.
    7. What you learned about advocating upward.

    Rejection signals

    • Assumes motive, disengages.
    • Routes around rather than addressing.
    • No attempt to improve how ideas were presented.
    • Ends in avoidance.

    Model answer skeleton

    A staff engineer repeatedly waved off my suggestions in design reviews, and my first read was 'he doesn't rate juniors'. Before deciding that, I paid attention to what he DID engage with, and I noticed he only took ideas seriously when they came with data or a prototype - my suggestions were verbal and hand-wavy by comparison, so they were easy to dismiss.

    So rather than disengage, I changed how I brought ideas: for the next proposal I built a small benchmark showing my approach halved p99 latency and brought the numbers to the review. He engaged seriously with it and we adopted it. Later I also told him directly, lightly, that his quick dismissals in the room sometimes shut down ideas before they were fully formed, and he actually took that on board. The outcome was that I got through, and we ended up on good terms.

    What I learned is to test my assumption about someone's motive before acting on it, and that the fix for 'not being heard' is often to make the idea impossible to wave off rather than to stop talking.

    Q7.6 Practical drill - Difficult teammate

    Write a difficult-teammate story that passes the "no-villain" test - the teammate's behaviour must be understandable given their pressures, and you must name your own part in the friction. Then script the "what was your role in the friction" follow-up.

    What it really tests. Forces you to build a difficult-teammate story with empathy and self-ownership, defusing the biggest red flag in this category: sounding like you'll badmouth colleagues.

    Weak answer

    N/A - drill. A weak attempt makes the teammate a villain, gives them no understandable motive, and claims the candidate had no part in the friction.

    Why it fails

    • Teammate is a one-dimensional villain.
    • No understandable reason for their behaviour.
    • Candidate is the blameless hero.
    • No answer for "what was your role in it".

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Reflection - "State their behaviour so it sounds reasonable given their situation - can you?" - Empathy that defuses the villain framing.
    2. Ownership - "What's your honest half of the friction?" - Self-ownership of the relationship.
    3. Tradeoff - "Where in this story might an interviewer hear badmouthing?" - Auditing for the badmouthing red flag.

    Answer framework

    1. Pick a real difficult-teammate situation.
    2. Write their behaviour with its underlying, understandable cause.
    3. Name your own contribution to the friction honestly.
    4. Describe what you did to improve it.
    5. State the outcome.
    6. Script the "your role in the friction" answer.
    7. Read it back for any line that badmouths.

    Rejection signals

    • Teammate stays a villain.
    • No understandable motive.
    • No ownership of the candidate's part.
    • Badmouthing leaks through.

    Model answer skeleton

    Drill output: Story - 'An engineer got sharp and defensive whenever changes touched a legacy service; understandable cause: he alone got paged for it at 3am, so instability was HIS problem, not abstract risk.' My honest half of the friction: 'I'd been proposing changes to that service without ever acknowledging his on-call burden, which naturally put him on the defensive - I was treating his fear as obstruction.' What I did: looped him in early, explicitly addressed on-call safety in proposals; the friction dissolved and he became a strong reviewer.

    Scripted 'your role in the friction' answer around that - I own that my framing was half the problem. Badmouthing audit: I cut the word 'defensive' as a label and instead describe the behaviour and its cause, so he sounds like a reasonable person under pressure, which he was. Rehearsed to ~80 seconds.
