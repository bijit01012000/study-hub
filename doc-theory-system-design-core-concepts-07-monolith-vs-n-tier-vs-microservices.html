    Monolith vs N-Tier vs Microservices

    6 sections · 600 words

    One spectrum, three stops. Architecture, concept 07 of 36.

    The Idea

    Monolith: one codebase, one process. Fast to ship early, risky to change as the codebase and the team grow, since everyone edits the same code and one bug can take the whole thing down.

    N-Tier: split by responsibility, not by team: a presentation tier, a business-logic tier, a data tier, classically as genuinely separate deployable processes, not just layers inside one codebase (that is a layered monolith, still one deploy). Still not microservices: no tier is independently owned, versioned, or released by its own team.

    Microservices: split by business capability into independently deployable services, each with its own codebase and often its own database. Function calls become network calls, and the team now needs service discovery, monitoring, and a plan for partial failure.

    The progression is one spectrum with three stops:

    1. Monolith: one codebase, one process
    2. N-Tier: separate tiers, one team
    3. Microservices: independent teams and deploys
    4. more splitting means more independence and more operational cost.
    • Start with a monolith.
    • Move toward N-tier when the system needs its layers as separate deployable tiers, but one team can still own the whole thing.
    • Move to microservices when team size and the need to deploy independently justify the operational cost, not by default.

    Watch Out For

    • The usual failure mode is going to microservices too early: paying the network-call tax and the on-call tax before the team is large enough to need the independence.
    • A useful test: does this piece need to scale, fail, or deploy on a different schedule than the rest of the system? If not, it is probably still one deployable.

    Where You Will See It

    • Classic three-tier web apps (presentation, application, database) are still the default for a huge share of internal business software.
    • Netflix and Uber are the commonly cited large-scale microservices examples, built after years of monolith growth.
    • Most successful products, including Amazon, Netflix, and Shopify, started as a monolith and split out services as scale demanded it.

    How It Connects

    • Service Discovery: a piece of infrastructure microservices need that a monolith never has to think about.
    • API Gateway: the front door that makes many small services look like one system to the outside world.
    • Circuit Breaker: the safety net microservices need, since a failing service can no longer take the whole process down, but can still cascade.

    Follow-Up Questions

    1. A five-person startup is choosing an architecture on day one. What should they pick? A monolith. Almost every large system, including ones now famous for microservices, started as one.

    2. A team of 200 engineers keeps blocking each other on one shared deploy. What is the signal? That is the signal to split into services, so teams can deploy independently.

    3. What is the most common architecture mistake made by small teams? Adopting microservices before the team is large enough to need the independence, paying network and on-call costs for nothing.

    4. N-tier splits an app into layers. Does each layer deploy separately? Classic N-tier tiers usually are separate deployable processes, a web tier, an app tier, a database tier, which is different from a layered monolith organizing one codebase internally with no separate deploys at all. What N-tier still lacks, versus microservices, is independent ownership: the tiers are not released on their own schedules by separate teams.

    Recall It As

    More splitting means more independence and more operational cost. Choose based on team size, not fashion.
