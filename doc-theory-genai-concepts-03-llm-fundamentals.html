    LLM fundamentals

    1 sections · 603 words

    Module 03 · 12 questions · 1 practical drill

    Know the mechanics under the vocabulary on your CV.

    This is the round that checks whether the vocabulary on your CV is load-bearing. Nobody expects you to derive attention or explain a training loop, you are being hired to build applications, not models. What gets checked here is narrower and harder to fake: what a token actually is when you're billed for one, what happens as a context window fills, what temperature and top_p actually change, and what an embedding does and does not capture. Model selection, fine-tuning versus RAG versus prompting, and LoRA now live in Module 4. This module stays on the mechanics underneath every LLM call you make, the ones with a confident wrong answer circulating widely.

    Inside this module: Tokens & windows · Attention & KV cache · Sampling · Embeddings

    How to work this module: alternative you rejected, and what actually failed. Those three are what the follow-ups are hunting for.

    Concept checkpoints

    Q3.1 — What's a token, and why is cost, latency and limits all counted in them?

    What it really tests: Whether tokenisation is understood as a real mechanism with consequences, not "tokens are roughly words".

    Q3.2 — What actually happens as the context window fills up?

    What it really tests: Whether the advertised window and the reliably usable window are known to be different numbers.

    Q3.3 — What do temperature and top_p actually do, and when does it matter?

    What it really tests: Whether sampling parameters are understood mechanically, not treated as one creativity dial.

    Q3.4 — What does an embedding actually represent, and what does it fail to capture?

    What it really tests: Whether embeddings are understood as a lossy projection with specific blind spots, not a general meaning vector.

    Q3.5 — Cosine, dot product, Euclidean distance, which do you use, and does it matter?

    What it really tests: Whether the metric choice is known to be dictated by the embedding model, not personal preference.

    Q3.6 — DRILL: a user uploads a 60-page PDF to summarise. Budget the tokens and context.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate can reason concretely about a token and context budget, not just say "it depends".

    Q3.7 — What does attention actually do, at the level you'd explain it to a colleague?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate has a working mechanical picture of attention, or only the recruiting-brochure definition.

    Q3.8 — What is the KV cache, and why does it change your cost and latency?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate connects the KV cache to input/output pricing, first-token latency and prompt caching, not just its name.

    Q3.9 — Why is generating output slower than reading the same amount of input?

    What it really tests: Whether prefill and decode are understood as different operations with different bottlenecks, not just "output is slower".

    Q3.10 — Beyond temperature, what do top_k, repetition penalties and seeds control?

    What it really tests: Whether sampling controls are understood as distinct levers with distinct failure modes, not interchangeable randomness settings.

    Q3.11 — Mechanically, why does a model hallucinate instead of saying it doesn't know?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate has a real mechanical account of hallucination, not "it's a black box" or "it makes things up".

    Q3.12 — What changes mechanically when the input is an image or audio, not text?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate knows non-text input is converted into a token-like representation, and what that costs and breaks.
