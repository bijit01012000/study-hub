      The project you can defend

      0 sections · 3,840 words

      Module 02 · 9 questions · 1 practical drill

      Defend what you actually built, at whatever scale you built it.

      Q2.1 — Walk me through the most substantial thing you've built with an LLM, start anywhere.

      Weak answer: "I built a support bot for my side project with LangChain and Pinecone. It retrieves chunks and answers, and has run fine since I shipped it."

      Why it fails: - Names tools, not decisions, and "retrieves chunks and generates an answer" just describes RAG, not this system. - No number anywhere, no volume, no time frame, no split between what was built and what a framework supplied.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot trace a single request through the system without generalising back to how RAG works. - "It's running fine" is the entire status report, with nothing behind it to check.

      Model answer: 1. I built the answer half of a support bot for a side project of mine, a note-taking app, so users could ask free-text questions against my help docs instead of emailing me. 2. On a request we embed the query, pull the top 8 chunks from my vector index, and I wrote the prompt that assembles those plus the last two turns, forcing an honest 'I don't know'. 3. I owned the retrieval-to-prompt step and the fallback logic specifically, not the embedding pipeline, which came from a tutorial I adapted. 4. It served around 40 questions a week at peak, and roughly 70% resolved without me stepping in to answer by hand. 5. The failure that stuck with me: a question like 'how do I export and will I lose my tags' only retrieved chunks for the first clause, so the answer silently dropped the second half.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Walk me through one real request, start to finish. What's the exact sequence of calls? · Whether they trace their own system, not describe RAG generically. 2. MEASUREMENT — How many people actually used this, and how do you know? · Real usage evidence, even a small number, not an assumed one. 3. FAILURE — Give me an input that produced a genuinely bad output. What happened to it? · Whether a failure ever surfaced and can be produced on demand. 4. OWNERSHIP — Which part did you write yourself, and which came from a template you adapted? · Separating individual contribution from assembled tooling.

      Tip: Even a weekend script or a work task that called an LLM API once counts here. Describe it with this same level of precision. Don't invent scale or usage you never had. Say honestly it had five users or none yet, and describe the one real request you actually tested.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 8 chunks retrieved per query - ~40 questions a week, peak - 70% resolved unassisted - Last 2 turns kept in prompt

      Q2.2 — Why use an LLM here at all? What did you consider and rule out first?

      Weak answer: "An LLM was really the only option. The questions were too varied for keyword search to handle every phrasing, so it was the obvious choice."

      Why it fails: - "The only option" with no alternative ever actually tried, measured, or even sketched out. - No acknowledgement that a good chunk of the questions were probably simple enough for something cheaper.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot describe what the simplest non-LLM version would have looked like, even roughly. - Treats "understands language" as a complete justification, with no cost side mentioned at all.

      Model answer: 1. Before touching an LLM I tried plain keyword search against my help doc titles, on the real questions people had actually emailed me. 2. It matched the right article maybe half the time, and missed almost every question that compared two options, like 'should I use tags or folders'. 3. That measured gap, not a hunch that keyword search felt primitive, is what justified the LLM. A miss meant I answered the email myself, by hand. 4. It cost me real latency, plain search returned instantly and this took a couple of seconds, plus an ongoing token bill where before there was none. 5. I kept the keyword match as a first pass. A confident match skipped the LLM entirely, so easy questions stayed on the free, instant path.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What would the simplest non-LLM version have actually looked like? · Whether a real alternative was designed, not just gestured at. 2. MEASUREMENT — Did you measure the simple approach, or assume it would fail? · Evidence versus assumption behind the decision. 3. TRADEOFF — What did the LLM cost you that the simple approach wouldn't have? · Whether the cost side was weighed, not just the capability side. 4. REFLECTION — Was any slice still better handled the old way? Did you keep that path? · Whether the decision was binary or a kept hybrid.

      Tip: If you've picked a regex, a lookup table, or plain search over a fancier option because it was simpler and good enough, that's the same judgement. Don't claim you ruled out an alternative you never tried. Say honestly you went straight to the LLM, and what you'd check first if you rebuilt it.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Keyword search matched ~50% - Missed comparison questions - Kept the cheap path for easy Qs - LLM added real latency and cost

      Q2.3 — Which parts of that system were the model's, and which parts were yours?

      Weak answer: "The model handled the understanding and the answer. I just wrapped it with a bit of code and a simple interface on top."

      Why it fails: - "Just wrapped it" hides exactly the work worth naming: validation, retries, prompt assembly, guardrails. - No example of the model being technically right while the system still gave a wrong answer.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot separate "the model did X" from "the system did X", treating them as identical. - Every piece of logic is credited to the model, none of it to the candidate's own code.

      Model answer: 1. The model's job, strictly, was to take a question plus up to eight retrieved chunks and write an answer with a citation back to the source doc. 2. Everything before that was mine: the retrieval call, and a step that deduplicated near-identical docs so they didn't both eat context for nothing. 3. I also wrote a citation check that rejected any answer citing a doc ID that wasn't actually retrieved, since the model invented a plausible one a few times early on. 4. That's the model-right-system-wrong case: the sentence read fine, but nothing had verified the citation existed until I added that check. 5. I kept pricing and plan-limit facts out of the prompt entirely, and had code inject them as facts to restate, not reason about, since a wrong answer there mattered more.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Which one step could you swap for a different model without touching anything else? · Whether the model's contract with the rest of the code is actually decoupled. 2. FAILURE — Give me a case where the model was right but the system still answered wrong. · Whether model failure is distinguished from integration failure. 3. OWNERSHIP — What's a piece of plain code you wrote just to stop the model doing something? · A concrete guardrail, not just a wrapper. 4. TRADEOFF — Where did you keep logic in code on purpose, even though the model could do it? · Judgement about when not to delegate to the model.

      Tip: If you've ever wrapped a flaky API or library call with validation and a fallback, that's the same instinct this question is testing. Don't claim you've drawn this exact line around an LLM if you haven't. Say where you've done it around any other unreliable component instead.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Model: input to draft answer only - Citation check rejects bad cites - Facts injected, not generated - A few bad cites a week, caught

      Q2.4 — What did the first version get wrong once real people actually used it?

      Weak answer: "Early on there were a few hallucination edge cases. I tuned the prompts and added some validation, and it's been solid since."

      Why it fails: - "A few edge cases with hallucinations" with no actual example of what the model said. - "Tuned the prompts" is the generic non-answer that could describe any change to any prompt anywhere.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot produce a real example of a bad output on request. - No detection method named, implying it was noticed by luck rather than anyone watching.

      Model answer: 1. A user asked if they could export their notes and keep their tags, and the model confidently said yes, full tag export, we had no such feature. 2. It had reached for plausible-sounding boilerplate instead of admitting my docs didn't cover it, and no retrieved chunk said anything close. 3. I found it because the user emailed me confused, not because I was checking for it, which is exactly the gap I fixed next. 4. I added a weekly habit of rereading ten real transcripts myself, checking every claim against a retrieved chunk. 5. I also changed the system prompt to say 'I don't have that information' when no chunk supports a claim, backed by the citation check from before.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Show me one actual bad output, close to verbatim. What did it say, and why? · Whether a real example exists or the story is generic. 2. MEASUREMENT — How did you find out, a check you ran, or pure chance? · Whether detection was systematic or accidental. 3. FAILURE — What was the actual blast radius? Did anyone act on the wrong answer? · Whether the real consequence is understood and stated. 4. REFLECTION — What exists now that didn't exist in version one because of this? · Whether the fix was durable, not a one-off edit.

      Tip: If you've tested a side project yourself and found it confidently wrong about something, that counts. Describe that specific moment. Don't invent an incident you didn't have. Say honestly you tested it yourself before real users touched it, and what you found there.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Confident wrong answer, no chunk - Found via user email, not a check - 10 transcripts reread weekly - "Don't know" beats a guess

      Q2.5 — How did you actually know it was working? What did you measure?

      Weak answer: "I tried a bunch of prompts myself before launch and the answers looked good, and I didn't get many complaints after."

      Why it fails: - "Looked good" and "no complaints" are impressions, not a measurement against anything. - No evaluation set exists, so there's no way to know if a later change made things better or worse.

      Instant rejection: - No number can be produced when pressed, "it felt better" is the ceiling of the answer. - Never describes a case where their own impression was wrong and a check caught it.

      Model answer: 1. Before I called it done I pulled 25 real questions from my own inbox and wrote what I considered the correct answer for each, by hand. 2. I scored the pipeline against that set myself, using a simple rubric: correct, partially correct, wrong, or unsupported claim. 3. The first version scored 60% fully correct. After adding the citation check, that moved to 80% on the same set. 4. A longer prompt template felt like an improvement when I read it, but scored two points lower, since the extra detail added small unsupported claims. 5. I didn't measure tone or helpfulness regularly, that read took a while each time, so I ran it monthly and leaned on the citation check in between.

      Follow-ups: 1. MEASUREMENT — What's the actual number, accuracy against a set, a pass rate? Give me it. · Whether a real quantified metric exists at all. 2. TECHNICAL — How many examples were in your test set, and who wrote the expected answers? · Whether the set is real and has any rigour behind it. 3. FAILURE — Tell me about a change that looked better to you but actually regressed something. · Whether manual impression was ever shown to be insufficient. 4. TRADEOFF — What did you deliberately not measure, because it was too slow to check often? · Awareness of the cost of evaluation itself.

      Tip: If you've written even five test cases with expected answers and checked your code against them by hand, that's the same instinct as an eval set. Don't claim you built a rigorous eval suite if you didn't. Say honestly you tested a handful of cases yourself, and what you'd add first.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 25 real questions, hand scored - 60% to 80% after one fix - Rubric: correct, partial, wrong - Monthly full check, not per change

      Q2.6 — What did it actually cost to run, and how did you find that out?

      Weak answer: "Cost wasn't a big concern, the calls were fairly cheap individually and I just kept an eye on the monthly bill."

      Why it fails: - "Kept an eye on the bill" is not attribution, no per-request or per-feature number exists. - No discussion of what actually drives the spend: output length, context size, or model choice.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot produce any per-request number when asked directly. - No idea what fraction of spend is input tokens versus output tokens.

      Model answer: 1. I logged the token counts on every call myself, so I could work out cost per answered question rather than just read the provider dashboard total. 2. It landed at a small fraction of a rupee per question, and the eight retrieved chunks were most of that, not the generated answer itself. 3. So I cut retrieval from eight chunks to five with a simple reranker, which cut input tokens by about 40% for a two-point drop on my rubric from before. 4. That felt worth it for a side project, but it was a real trade, not a free win, and I wrote down why so I'd remember it later. 5. The spike I didn't expect: a retry-on-timeout I added would resend the full context on any error, and one bad night it burned several days of normal spend in an hour.

      Follow-ups: 1. MEASUREMENT — What was the cost per request, the actual figure, and how did you get it? · Whether real attribution exists, not a dashboard total. 2. TECHNICAL — What was the biggest driver of spend, output tokens, context size, or retries? · Whether the actual cost structure is understood. 3. TRADEOFF — Did you ever trade quality for cost? What did you give up, specifically? · A real named decision, not cost as background noise. 4. FAILURE — Was there a moment the cost spiked? What caused it, and how did you catch it? · Whether spend was watched at all, or only noticed later.

      Tip: If you've ever worked out what a feature actually costs per use, cloud spend, an API bill, anything, rather than just watching a total, say that. Don't claim precise production cost figures you don't have. Say honestly what you tracked for your own project and how rough those numbers were.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Logged tokens per call myself - 8 to 5 chunks cut cost ~40% - 2-point accuracy drop, accepted - One bad retry burned days' spend

      Q2.7 — If you were rebuilding this today, what would you do differently?

      Weak answer: "I'd probably invest more in evaluation earlier and add a bit more error handling, but the overall approach held up fine."

      Why it fails: - "More evaluation" and "more error handling" are the two safest, most generic answers available. - "Held up fine" avoids naming any actual regret, nothing here would cost anything to say.

      Instant rejection: - The answer is generic enough to apply to almost any project, not this specific one. - Cannot describe the alternative concretely when pressed on what they'd build instead.

      Model answer: 1. I'd write my own chunking from the start instead of using the tutorial's default splitter, which I only replaced under pressure months in. 2. It got me to a working demo fast, which seemed reasonable at the time, but it split my step-by-step guides mid-instruction more than I'd realised. 3. I only found that by reading failure cases by hand, exactly the thing I should have checked in week one instead of trusting the default. 4. What I'd build instead is a chunker that splits on my docs' own headings, which I did eventually build, just later than I should have. 5. Rebuilding it today would cost a weekend, and it's worth it. That one change drove more of the quality gain than any prompt edit I made.

      Follow-ups: 1. REFLECTION — Name the one decision you'd reverse first, and what it actually cost you. · Whether a specific, consequential regret exists. 2. TRADEOFF — What would rebuilding that piece cost you now? Is it actually worth doing? · Whether the reflection is grounded in real cost. 3. TECHNICAL — What would you replace it with, concretely, not 'a better approach'? · Whether a real design exists or it's just abstract. 4. OWNERSHIP — Was that decision yours, or copied from a tutorial you followed? · Whether the regret is genuinely owned.

      Tip: If you've ever followed a tutorial's default setup and later found it wrong for your actual case, that's the same regret this question wants. Don't invent a regret you don't have. Say honestly what you'd check first before trusting any default, based on what you've seen go wrong elsewhere.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Default splitter broke mid-guide - Found it by reading failures - A weekend to rebuild properly - Biggest single quality gain

      Q2.8 — This was a side project. What would have to change to run it at real scale?

      Weak answer: "I'd probably just add more servers and maybe some caching, and it should scale pretty much the same as it does now."

      Why it fails: - "More servers" ignores that the model API, not compute, is almost certainly the real bottleneck and cost driver. - No mention of monitoring, cost climbing with volume, or concurrent users hitting rate limits.

      Instant rejection: - Assumes the system would behave identically at 100x volume, with no new failure mode named. - No mention of what currently only works because volume is low, like manual review.

      Model answer: 1. Right now I personally reread a sample of transcripts weekly, that doesn't survive past a few hundred users, it would need to become a real sampling process. 2. My retry-on-timeout bug from before would be far more dangerous at volume, so I'd add rate limiting and a spend cap before trusting it unattended. 3. Cost would stop being a rounding error, it scales roughly with request volume, so I'd need per-request budgets and an alert, not a monthly glance at the bill. 4. I have no concurrency handling today since I'm usually the only user testing it, so I'd need to check the vector store and API client actually handle parallel calls. 5. I'd also want a real rollback path. Right now if a change goes wrong I just notice and fix it myself, which doesn't work once other people depend on it.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What in your current setup only works because volume is low right now? · Whether informal, manual safety nets are recognised as not scaling. 2. MEASUREMENT — What would you need to monitor that you don't monitor today? · Awareness that observability at scale is a real, separate build. 3. TRADEOFF — What would get meaningfully more expensive, and what would you do about it? · Whether cost scaling with volume is understood concretely. 4. FAILURE — What's the first thing likely to break as real traffic grows fast? · Whether a specific, plausible failure point is named, not a vague worry.

      Tip: If you've ever taken a personal script or tool and thought through what breaks if a hundred people used it at once, that's this exact exercise. Don't pretend you've handled real scale. Say plainly what currently only works because you're the only user, and name that specifically.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Manual review breaks past ~100 - Per-request budgets, not monthly - Rate limits before unattended use - Concurrency untested at volume

      Q2.9 — DRILL: rewrite five bullets about your LLM project as decisions, not features. (practical drill)

      Weak answer: "N/A, this is a drill. A weak attempt lists tools, "built with LangChain and Pinecone", with no alternative or number named."

      Why it fails: - Bullets describe what the project is, not what was decided, tool names appear where decisions should. - No bullet names a rejected alternative or attaches a real number to its outcome.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot produce five decisions without at least one collapsing into "I used X". - The five bullets could describe almost any LLM project, not this specific one.

      Model answer: 1. Chose a citation check over trusting the model's grounding, after it invented plausible source IDs a few times a week, cut that to near zero. 2. Rejected the tutorial's default top-2 retrieval for top-8 plus a reranker, since my docs needed corroborating detail, moved my rubric from 60% to 80%. 3. Chose a heading-based chunker over the default fixed-token splitter, after guides were splitting mid-instruction, my single biggest quality gain. 4. Rejected fine-tuning in favour of prompting plus retrieval, since my answers changed often and a fine-tune would go stale within days. 5. Cut retrieved context from eight chunks to five to cut cost, accepting a measured two-point accuracy drop rather than an unmeasured one.

      Follow-ups: 1. TRADEOFF — For each bullet, what did you seriously consider and reject? · Every decision has a real discarded alternative, not just an outcome. 2. MEASUREMENT — What number would you put in front of an interviewer for each one? · Decisions map to a measured result, not a feeling. 3. REFLECTION — Which bullet collapses first under two follow-up questions? · Honest self-audit of the weakest claim before it's found. 4. OWNERSHIP — Which bullet still describes a tutorial's behaviour, not your own choice? · Whether a template default has been mistaken for a decision.

      Tip: This drill works on any LLM project, however small, run it on the smallest thing you've built rather than skipping it for lack of scale. Don't pad a bullet with an invented number. Leave it as a reasoned, unmeasured decision and say so honestly if you never measured it.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Five decisions, five numbers - Name what you rejected, always - Rehearse your weakest bullet - A tool name is not a decision
