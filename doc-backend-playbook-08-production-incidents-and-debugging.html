    Production incidents and debugging

    9 sections · 3,719 words

    Module 08 - 9 questions - 1 practical drill

    Tell an evidence-led incident story: mitigate, diagnose, prevent.

    This is the round most senior interviews weight heavily, and it's scored on tone as much as technique: blameless, timeline-driven, evidence-based. Follow-ups chase what you personally did during the incident and what you changed so it can't recur the same way.

    Inside this module: Triage - Mitigation - Root cause - Blameless prevention

    Q8.1 - Tell me about the worst production incident you were involved in.

    What it really tests: Whether you can narrate an incident as a timeline with your specific actions, impact, and follow-up - blamelessly.

    Weak answer: "We had an outage once when the database went down. It was stressful but we got it back up and everything was fine after a while."

    Why it fails: - No timeline: when detected, mitigated, resolved. - No customer-impact quantification. - "The database went down" - no root cause, and passive framing. - Doesn't say what the candidate personally did. - No follow-up action to prevent recurrence.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. OWNERSHIP - What did YOU do in the first ten minutes? - Personal action under pressure. 2. MEASUREMENT - What was the customer impact, in numbers? - Quantified blast radius. 3. TRADEOFF - Did you mitigate first or find the root cause first? - Mitigation-before-diagnosis instinct. 4. REFLECTION - What did you change so it can't happen the same way? - Closing the loop.

    Answer framework: 1. Detection (how/when) 2. Impact quantified 3. First mitigation action 4. Diagnosis 5. Root cause 6. Permanent fix 7. Blameless postmortem 8. Prevention shipped

    Rejection signals: - No timeline. - Can't quantify impact. - Passive voice hides who did what. - No preventive follow-up. - Blames a person.

    Model answer skeleton: Our checkout started returning 500s at about 14:10; I was on call. Impact was roughly 40% of checkout attempts failing for about 25 minutes - real lost revenue - which I could see from the checkout success-rate dashboard.

    My first move was mitigation, not diagnosis: the errors correlated with a deploy 15 minutes earlier, so I rolled that deploy back within a few minutes, which restored checkout before I fully understood the cause.

    Then I diagnosed: the new release had introduced an N+1 query that exhausted the database connection pool under load, so every request queued for a connection and timed out. The permanent fix was to eager-load the association and add a connection-pool-saturation alert.

    In the blameless postmortem we focused on why the N+1 got through - our load test didn't exercise that path - rather than on who wrote it, and the actions were the alert plus a load-test scenario for that endpoint.

    The key habit was rolling back to stop the bleeding first and understanding it second.

    Q8.2 - Requests are hanging and latency is spiking. You have no idea why. Where do you start?

    What it really tests: A systematic debugging method under uncertainty - the actual skill being scored.

    Weak answer: "I'd check the logs to see if there were any errors, and probably restart the service to see if that fixed it."

    Why it fails: - Restart-first can destroy the evidence and only masks the problem. - No hypothesis-driven method. - Logs alone without metrics/traces is a needle-in-haystack. - No mention of recent changes as the prime suspect.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - What changed recently - deploy, config, traffic? - Change-correlation instinct. 2. MEASUREMENT - Which signal tells you if it's CPU, memory, DB, or downstream? - Resource isolation via metrics. 3. FAILURE - Why might restarting be the wrong first move? - Preserving evidence. 4. TRADEOFF - How do you narrow it to one component in a chain? - Tracing to localise.

    Answer framework: 1. Check what changed recently 2. Look at the golden signals (latency, traffic, errors, saturation) 3. Isolate the resource (CPU/mem/DB/IO/downstream) 4. Use traces to localise the slow hop 5. Form a hypothesis 6. Test safely 7. Mitigate 8. Preserve evidence for the postmortem

    Rejection signals: - Restarts before investigating. - No hypothesis, just pokes around. - Ignores recent changes. - Logs-only, no metrics or traces.

    Model answer skeleton: My first question is always 'what changed' - a deploy, a config flip, a traffic surge, a downstream degradation - because most incidents correlate with a recent change, and that's the fastest path to mitigation. In parallel I look at the golden signals: latency, traffic, error rate, and saturation.

    Saturation is what tells me whether it's CPU-bound, memory/GC, a full connection pool, disk IO, or a slow downstream, so I'm isolating the resource rather than guessing.

    Then I pull a trace of a slow request to see which hop actually eats the time - often it's one dependency or one query, not the whole service. From that I form a specific hypothesis and test it safely, for example checking the DB's active-connection count if I suspect pool exhaustion.

    I deliberately don't restart first, because a restart can clear the symptom and destroy the evidence - the heap, the connection state, the thread dump - that I need to actually fix it.

    I mitigate, but I capture diagnostics before I do.

    Q8.3 - Your service has a memory leak in production. How do you confirm and find it?

    What it really tests: Concrete debugging of a memory issue - heap analysis, not guesswork.

    Weak answer: "We noticed memory kept growing so we increased the memory limit and set up automatic restarts when it got too high."

    Why it fails: - Raising the limit and auto-restarting masks the leak, never finds it. - No heap dump or profiling to locate the retained objects. - No confirmation it's a true leak vs expected growth. - Treats the symptom permanently.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - How do you confirm it's a leak and not just a big working set? - Distinguishing leak from load. 2. MEASUREMENT - How do you find what's retaining the memory? - Heap dump / retained-object analysis. 3. TRADEOFF - What are common leak sources in your language? - Language-specific knowledge. 4. FAILURE - Why is auto-restart a band-aid, not a fix? - Symptom vs cause.

    Answer framework: 1. Confirm the trend (memory grows and doesn't reclaim after GC) 2. Rule out expected working-set growth 3. Capture heap dumps over time 4. Diff to find growing retained objects 5. Trace to the retaining reference 6. Fix the root cause 7. Verify memory flattens 8. Keep the alert

    Rejection signals: - Only raises limits / auto-restarts. - Never captures a heap dump. - Can't name common leak sources. - Doesn't confirm it's actually a leak.

    Model answer skeleton: First I confirm it's actually a leak: I watch whether memory keeps climbing and, crucially, fails to come back down after garbage collection - a growing working set under load recovers, a leak doesn't. Then I capture heap dumps at intervals and diff them to see which object types are growing unboundedly; the dominator tree points me at what's retaining them.

    In one Java service the culprit was an unbounded static cache - entries were added and never evicted, so a HashMap held references that GC could never reclaim.

    Common sources I check are caches without eviction, listeners or callbacks never unregistered, thread-locals on pooled threads, and growing collections.

    The fix was bounding the cache with an eviction policy, after which memory flattened into a healthy sawtooth.

    Raising the memory limit and auto-restarting, which is where a lot of teams stop, just delays the crash and hides the bug - I keep an alert on the growth rate but the actual fix is removing what retains the memory.

    Q8.4 - How do you debug an issue that only happens in production and never reproduces locally?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate can reason about environment/data/scale differences and use production observability.

    Weak answer: "I'd try to reproduce it locally by copying the setup, and add more logging until I could see what was going wrong."

    Why it fails: - Fixating on local repro when the whole point is it doesn't reproduce. - Doesn't reason about what's different: data, scale, concurrency, config. - "Add more logging" without a hypothesis is a shotgun. - No use of production tracing/metrics on the actual failing requests.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - What's different about production - data, scale, concurrency, config? - Environment-difference reasoning. 2. MEASUREMENT - How do you observe the real failing requests without local repro? - Production observability. 3. TRADEOFF - How do you add targeted logging without flooding or leaking data? - Surgical instrumentation. 4. FAILURE - How might a race condition explain 'only in prod'? - Concurrency-at-scale awareness.

    Answer framework: 1. Enumerate prod-vs-local differences 2. Suspect data/scale/concurrency/config 3. Use traces and logs on real failing requests 4. Add targeted, safe logging with a correlation id 5. Form a hypothesis about the difference 6. Reproduce the condition, not the environment 7. Fix and verify in prod signals

    Rejection signals: - Only tries to reproduce locally. - No reasoning about environmental differences. - Blind extra logging. - Ignores concurrency/scale as a cause.

    Model answer skeleton: The mistake is chasing a local repro when the signal is that production is different, so I start by enumerating what's different: real data volume and shape, concurrency, actual traffic patterns, production config and secrets, and downstream services that don't exist locally. Those differences are usually the cause.

    Instead of reproducing the environment I reproduce the condition - if I suspect a specific data shape triggers it, I find a real failing request via its trace id and inspect exactly that payload.

    I lean on production observability: tracing to see where the failing requests diverge, and targeted logging keyed to a correlation id so I capture the one code path without flooding logs or dumping PII.

    'Only in prod' very often means a race condition that only appears under real concurrency, or a data edge case that never occurs in my seed data - for example a null in a field that's never null locally.

    Once I've pinned the differing condition, I write a test that recreates just that condition, fix it, and confirm the failure rate drops in the production metric.

    Q8.5 - Mid-incident: do you roll back or roll forward? How do you decide?

    What it really tests: Incident judgement - bias to the fastest safe mitigation, understanding when rollback isn't possible.

    Weak answer: "We'd fix the bug and deploy the fix. Rolling back felt like giving up, so we preferred to push a proper fix forward."

    Why it fails: - Prioritises pride over stopping customer impact. - Rolling forward under pressure risks a second bad deploy. - No consideration of which is faster and safer right now. - Ignores cases where rollback is blocked (irreversible migration).

    Follow-up ladder: 1. MEASUREMENT - Which restores service faster, right now - rollback or a fix? - Speed-to-mitigation bias. 2. TECHNICAL - When can you NOT roll back? - Irreversible migrations awareness. 3. FAILURE - Why is writing a fix under incident pressure risky? - Second-outage risk. 4. TRADEOFF - How does a feature flag change this calculus? - Instant kill-switch option.

    Answer framework: 1. Goal is fastest safe mitigation 2. Rollback if the change is reversible and recent 3. Roll forward only if rollback is blocked or the fix is trivial and certain 4. Beware writing code under pressure 5. Prefer a feature flag kill-switch 6. Restore first, root-cause after 7. Communicate status

    Rejection signals: - Refuses to roll back on principle. - Writes and deploys new code mid-incident by default. - Unaware that some migrations block rollback. - No feature-flag option considered.

    Model answer skeleton: The only goal mid-incident is to stop customer impact as fast as safely possible, and ego about a 'proper fix' has no place in that.

    My default is roll back if the incident correlates with a recent, reversible deploy, because a rollback is a known-good state I can reach in minutes, whereas a forward fix means writing code under pressure - exactly when a second bug is most likely, risking a second outage on top of the first.

    I only roll forward when rollback is genuinely blocked - say a database migration already ran and isn't cleanly reversible - or when the fix is a one-line, certain change.

    The best option, when it exists, is a feature flag: flip the kill-switch to disable the broken path instantly with no deploy at all, which is faster than either.

    So the order is: restore service by the fastest safe route, communicate status while doing it, and only then do the careful root-cause and permanent fix once nobody's bleeding.

    Q8.6 - How do you write a postmortem, and what makes it useful versus theatre?

    What it really tests: Blameless culture, actionable follow-ups, and whether postmortems actually prevent recurrence.

    Weak answer: "We'd write up what happened and who was involved, and note that we'd be more careful next time to avoid it happening again."

    Why it fails: - "Who was involved" hints at blame rather than systemic cause. - "Be more careful" is not an action item - it's a wish. - No timeline, no root cause, no owned follow-ups. - No mechanism to ensure the actions get done.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. REFLECTION - Why blameless, and what does blame actually cost you? - Culture understanding. 2. MEASUREMENT - What makes an action item real versus 'be more careful'? - Actionable, owned, tracked items. 3. TECHNICAL - How do you find the systemic cause, not just the trigger? - Five-whys / contributing factors. 4. OWNERSHIP - How do you make sure the follow-ups actually get done? - Accountability for actions.

    Answer framework: 1. Blameless framing 2. Factual timeline 3. Impact quantified 4. Distinguish trigger from systemic cause 5. Concrete, owned, dated action items 6. Root cause and contributing factors 7. Track actions to completion 8. Share widely to spread learning

    Rejection signals: - Names and blames individuals. - "Be more careful" as an action. - No timeline or quantified impact. - Action items with no owner and never tracked.

    Model answer skeleton: A useful postmortem is blameless, factual, and ends in tracked actions; a useless one names a culprit and promises vigilance. I start with a precise timeline - detection, mitigation, resolution - and quantify the impact, because that anchors everyone in facts rather than feelings.

    Blameless matters practically, not just morally: if people fear blame they hide information, and you lose the very details you need to fix the system - so I frame it as 'the system let a mistake reach production', not 'X made a mistake'.

    I push past the trigger to the systemic cause with something like five-whys: the trigger was a bad query, but why did it get through review, why did the load test miss it, why was there no alert - each 'why' is a place to fix the system.

    Action items have to be concrete, owned, and dated - 'add connection-pool alert, owned by me, by Friday' - not 'be more careful'.

    And critically I track those actions to completion in the backlog, because a postmortem whose actions are never done is theatre that lets the same outage recur.

    Q8.7 - How did you handle communication during an incident?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate understands incident coordination - roles, stakeholder updates, not just heads-down debugging.

    Weak answer: "I focused on fixing the problem as fast as possible. Once it was resolved I let people know it was fixed."

    Why it fails: - Silence during the incident leaves stakeholders and customers in the dark. - No incident-commander/roles concept. - Debugging and communicating done by the same overloaded person. - No regular status cadence.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. OWNERSHIP - Who talks to stakeholders while you're debugging? - Separation of commander and responder roles. 2. MEASUREMENT - How often do you post updates, even with no news? - Update cadence discipline. 3. TRADEOFF - What do you tell customers versus internal stakeholders? - Audience-appropriate comms. 4. REFLECTION - How does clear comms actually speed up resolution? - Coordination value.

    Answer framework: 1. Declare an incident and assign roles 2. Incident commander coordinates, responders debug 3. Regular status cadence even with no news 4. Separate customer and internal messaging 5. Central channel of record 6. Hand off on long incidents 7. Close the loop when resolved

    Rejection signals: - Goes silent while debugging. - One person debugs and communicates. - No update cadence. - No role separation.

    Model answer skeleton: Going heads-down and silent is a classic mistake, because stakeholders imagine the worst and start pinging the very people trying to fix it. So on anything significant we declare an incident and split roles: an incident commander coordinates and communicates while responders actually debug, so the debugging isn't constantly interrupted by 'any update?'.

    I keep a regular status cadence - an update every 15-20 minutes even if the update is 'still investigating, no ETA yet' - because predictable silence is worse than honest uncertainty.

    I tailor the message: internal stakeholders get technical detail and impact, customers get a plain-language status page entry about what's affected and that we're on it, without internal jargon or speculation. Everything goes in one channel of record so the timeline writes itself for the postmortem.

    On a long incident we hand off the commander role so nobody burns out.

    And clear comms genuinely speeds resolution - it keeps the responders focused and pulls in the right people fast instead of everyone guessing.

    Q8.8 - What's an incident that taught you something you still apply?

    What it really tests: Reflection and whether lessons from failure actually changed how the candidate works.

    Weak answer: "I learned that you should always test your code thoroughly before deploying, which I've done ever since."

    Why it fails: - "Test thoroughly" is a platitude, not a specific transferable lesson. - No concrete incident behind it. - The lesson isn't specific enough to have changed real behaviour. - No evidence it's actually applied.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. OWNERSHIP - What specifically do you now do differently because of it? - Behaviour change, not slogan. 2. MEASUREMENT - How did that lesson prevent or shrink a later incident? - Applied and paid off. 3. REFLECTION - What did you initially get wrong about the cause? - Honest re-evaluation. 4. TECHNICAL - How did you spread that lesson to the team? - Scaling the learning.

    Answer framework: 1. The incident 2. The specific lesson 3. The concrete behaviour change 4. Where you applied it since 5. How it paid off 6. What you first misdiagnosed 7. How you shared it

    Rejection signals: - Platitude with no specific incident. - Lesson didn't change any concrete behaviour. - Can't show it applied later. - Never shared it.

    Model answer skeleton: An incident where a config change with no rollout control took down all regions at once taught me a specific, lasting lesson: treat configuration changes with the same caution as code deploys. Before that, config went out globally and instantly, and a single bad value hit 100% of traffic in seconds with no canary and no easy rollback.

    What I do differently now, concretely, is put risky config behind the same staged-rollout and feature-flag machinery as code - canary it to one region, watch the metrics, then expand - and keep every config change instantly revertible.

    That paid off maybe a month later when a bad rate-limit value only degraded one canary region and we caught it before it went wide, turning what would've been an outage into a non-event.

    What I initially got wrong was blaming the specific value; the real cause was that config had no rollout safety at all.

    I wrote it up and we made staged config rollout the default for the whole team, so the lesson didn't stay just mine.

    Q8.9 - [DRILL] You get paged: "checkout error rate 30%, started 5 minutes ago." Narrate your first 15 minutes out loud, step by step.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate has an internalised incident runbook - mitigate, correlate, communicate rather than panic.

    Weak answer: N/A - drill. A weak attempt jumps straight into reading code or randomly restarting.

    Why it fails: - Dives into code before checking what changed. - No mitigation-first instinct. - No communication / incident declaration. - No evidence capture before acting.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - What's the very first thing you look at, and why? - Change-correlation first. 2. TRADEOFF - At what point do you roll back versus keep investigating? - Mitigation threshold. 3. OWNERSHIP - When do you declare an incident and update stakeholders? - Comms discipline under pressure.

    Answer framework: 1. Acknowledge page + declare incident 2. Confirm scope/impact on dashboard 3. Check recent deploys/config/traffic 4. Form top hypothesis 5. Mitigate (rollback/flag) to stop bleeding 6. Capture diagnostics 7. Post status update 8. Then root-cause

    Rejection signals: - Reads code before checking changes. - No mitigation until fully diagnosed. - Never declares the incident or updates anyone. - Restarts blindly, losing evidence.

    Model answer skeleton: Drill narration: Minute 0-2, I ack the page and declare an incident so a commander and comms spin up while I dig - I'm not doing this silently. Minute 2-4, I confirm scope on the checkout dashboard: is it 30% globally, one region, one payment provider? That shapes everything. Minute 4-7, I check what changed - recent deploys, config flips, a traffic spike, or a downstream provider's status - because 'started 5 minutes ago' screams recent change.

    Say I see a deploy at exactly the start time; that's my top hypothesis. Minute 7-10, I mitigate first: roll that deploy back or flip its feature flag to stop the bleeding, before I fully understand it - restoring checkout is the priority, not being right.

    Minute 10-12, while it recovers I capture diagnostics - a heap dump, error samples, the trace - so the postmortem has evidence. Minute 12-15, I post a status update internally and to the status page, confirm error rate is dropping, and only then move into calm root-cause analysis.

    Mitigate, correlate, communicate - code-reading comes after the bleeding stops.
