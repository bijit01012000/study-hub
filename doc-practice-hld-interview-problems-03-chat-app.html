    Design a Chat App

    3 sections · 3,322 words

    Three levels: Level 1 — The Shape, Level 2 — The Defence, Level 3 — The Edges.

    Level 1 — The Shape (Foundation)

    Pacing: 3 min ask · 5 min trace flow · 7 min read why.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Send a 1:1 message
    • Receive in real time while online
    • Delivery status: sent → delivered → read
    • Persists across devices, even offline

    Non-Functional

    • Persistent, addressable connection ← forces sticky routing
    • Survives recipient being offline ← forces store-and-forward
    • In-order within one conversation ← forces a sequence number
    • p99 delivery < 1 s, both online ← forces a direct hop
    • At-least-once, exactly-once felt ← forces client-side dedup

    Agree These First

    50 M DAU · 40 msgs/user/day · ~20% concurrently connected · ~40% of sends land on an offline recipient. Round, illustrative numbers — not a real company's traffic. Every number on this sheet falls out of these three.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client A — Sender · mobile app
    • Connection Server — Holds the open socket — both clients, for now
    • (13) send — ~23.1K/s
    • (4) Message Service — Persists it · forwards it if the recipient's socket is reachable
    • (5) deliver · push, <1 s both online
    • Message Store — Durable log · source of truth · one row per message
    • Client B — Recipient · mobile app

    Data model: messages message_id PK → conversation_id, sender_id, body.

    Line = connection vs traffic: DELIVER · pushed to a client; SEND · client to server.

    Say It Like This

    "This is the first sheet where the connection itself is state — so I'd hold a persistent WebSocket, not poll, for sub-second delivery while both are online. But a large share of sends — call it 40% — reach someone who isn't connected, and I can't tell which in advance, so I'd persist before I ever try to deliver, store-and-forward, not best-effort push. And the client mints the message ID once, so a retry can't become a duplicate."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (1) Persistent connection WebSocket — push in <1 s while both are online Polling → constant "anything new?" checks, mostly for nothing
    (4) Store before delivery Persist first — you can never know if the recipient is connected Live-delivery-only, drop if offline → silently loses a huge share of messages
    (13) Message ID Client generates it once — a retry carries the same ID Server-generated → a retry looks like a new message

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Msgs/day 50 M DAU × 40 msgs/user/day 2.0 B/day
    Msgs/s 2.0 B ÷ 86 400 s ~23.1K /s
    Concurrent 50 M × 20% online at once 10,000,000
    Offline stated — not 1 − concurrency ~40% of sends

    Round hard, out loud. 10 million OPEN CONNECTIONS at once — not requests/second — is the number that makes this sheet different in kind from every other one so far.

    Before You Draw Anything — The Four Moves

    1. Clarify, don't assume. 1:1 first — is message history required from day one? Is group chat in scope? Say so before drawing. — Two minutes of scoping buys the whole design.
    2. Agree the scale out loud. "50M DAU, 40 msgs/day, ~20% concurrent — that's 10 million open connections." Now every later choice has a reason. — An unstated number is an undefendable design.
    3. Draw the happy path only. Client → connection server → message service → store. Get agreement before adding anything. — Boxes you add before agreement are boxes you'll defend alone.
    4. Name one trade-off unprompted. "I'd use a persistent WebSocket, not polling, because push beats poll for sub-second delivery." — This is the single move that separates L1 from a junior answer.

    What L1 Is Testing

    • Can you produce a shape without being led to it?
    • Does every box have a reason you volunteered?
    • Did you reach for "10 million open connections" unprompted?

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. WebSocket or polling — and why? (ref 1 — tests: do you know the trade-off, not just the term)
    2. The recipient isn't connected right now. What actually happens to the message? (refs 4,5 — tests: a real mechanism, not a hand-wave)
    3. Why does the client generate the message ID, not the server? (ref 13 — tests: do you know retries can duplicate)
    4. Two brand-new users message each other for the first time. Walk the happy path. (refs 1,4,5 — tests: can you walk a clean path end to end)
    5. A user closes the app mid-conversation. What happens to a message sent to them a second later? (ref 4 — tests: is "offline" a concrete mechanism or just a word)

    At L1 you are being judged on one thing: can you produce a coherent shape and defend three choices inside it. Depth is page 2 — do not reach for it here. Ghosted boxes are what arrives next; you should be able to guess two of them.

    Level 2 — The Defence (Intermediate)

    Pacing: 5 min ask · 15 min trace flow · 10 min read why · 30 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Send a 1:1 message
    • Receive in real time while online
    • Delivery status: sent → delivered → read
    • Persists across devices, even offline
    • Group chat — smaller fan-out
    • Presence & typing — both ephemeral

    Non-Functional

    • Persistent, addressable connection ← forces sticky routing
    • Survives recipient being offline ← forces store-and-forward
    • In-order within one conversation ← forces a sequence number
    • p99 delivery < 1 s, both online ← forces a direct hop
    • At-least-once, exactly-once felt ← forces client-side dedup

    Out of Scope — Say It Aloud

    • End-to-end encryption
    • Media attachments (images / video)
    • Voice / video calls

    Agree These First

    50 M DAU · 40 msgs/user/day · ~20% concurrently connected · ~40% of sends land on an offline recipient. Round, illustrative numbers — not a real company's traffic. Every number on this sheet falls out of these three.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client A — Sender · mobile app
    • (1) Connection Server — Holds the open socket — Client A's connection
    • (13) send
    • (4) Message Service — Persists it · forwards it if the recipient's socket is reachable
    • (2) Connection Registry — user_id → which server holds their connection
    • (3) who holds user B? Cross-server forward
    • Connection Server 2 — Client B's socket — a different server, the common case at scale
    • Client B — Recipient · mobile app
    • (5) deliver · push, <1 s both online
    • (6) Offline Inbox — Per-user durable queue — flushed the moment they reconnect; only if B isn't connected anywhere
    • (11) Delivery receipts — sent → delivered → read, async
    • Message Store — Durable log · source of truth · one row per message; ≈300 GB/day, growing — no fixed ceiling

    Data model:

    • messages message_id PK · conversation_id · sender_id · seq · body · status
    • conversations conversation_id PK · members[] · type (1:1 / group)
    • registry user_id PK → server_id · region · heartbeat

    Line = connection vs traffic: DELIVER · pushed to a client; SEND · client to server; ASYNC · off critical path.

    Say It Like This

    "This is the first sheet where the connection itself is state — so I'd hold a persistent WebSocket, not poll, for sub-second delivery while both are online. But a large share of sends — call it 40% — reach someone who isn't connected, and I can't tell which in advance, so I'd persist before I ever try to deliver, store-and-forward, not best-effort push. And the client mints the message ID once, so a retry can't become a duplicate."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (1) Persistent connection WebSocket — push in <1 s while both are online Polling → constant "anything new?" checks, mostly for nothing
    (4) Store before delivery Persist first — you can never know if the recipient is connected Live-delivery-only, drop if offline → silently loses a huge share of messages
    (13) Message ID Client generates it once — a retry carries the same ID Server-generated → a retry looks like a new message
    (3) Cross-server forward Registry lookup, then forward directly — one hop Client polls its own server → reintroduces the latency WS removed
    (6) Offline inbox A per-user queue, filled at persist-time — only what's unread Re-scan the whole store on reconnect → worse as it grows forever
    (11) Delivery receipts Async status updates — sent → delivered → read Block the send until read → sender's UI hangs on their phone

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Msgs/day 50 M DAU × 40 msgs/user/day 2.0 B/day
    Msgs/s 2.0 B ÷ 86 400 s ~23.1K /s
    Concurrent 50 M × 20% online at once 10,000,000
    Offline stated — not 1 − concurrency ~40% of sends
    Conn servers 10 M ÷ 50 K conns/server 200
    Per-server 50 K conns × ~10 KB state each ≈500 MB
    Cross-server 200 servers → same-server odds 1 ÷ 200 >99%
    Storage/day 2.0 B msgs × ~150 B/msg ≈300 GB/day

    A large share of sends have an offline recipient, and you never know which — store-and-forward is the default path, not a fallback. It is not 1 − concurrency: chat is correlated, so measure it.

    What Breaks — Say It Before They Ask

    • (1) Connection server crash. Every connection it held drops at once — from outside this looks like a mass-disconnect, not one server's failure. — Fix: clients detect the drop and reconnect elsewhere; the registry updates fast so nothing forwards into a void
    • (3) Registry staleness. If the registry isn't updated the instant a connection moves or drops, another server forwards a message nowhere. — Fix: short heartbeat/TTL on registry entries, plus a forward-fails-then-recheck retry
    • (12) Thundering reconnect. One connection server dies; every client it held reconnects at once, overwhelming the gateway/registry mid-failure. — Fix: jittered client-side reconnect backoff

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. WebSocket or polling — and why? (ref 1 — tests: do you know the trade-off, not just the term)
    2. The recipient isn't connected right now. What actually happens to the message? (refs 4,5 — tests: a real mechanism, not a hand-wave)
    3. Why does the client generate the message ID, not the server? (ref 13 — tests: do you know retries can duplicate)
    4. Two users are on different connection servers. Walk me through how a message gets from one to the other. (refs 2,3 — tests: registry-lookup-then-forward, unprompted)
    5. Two messages land on two different servers within the same second, same conversation. How do you guarantee order? (ref 7 — tests: sequencing vs relying on arrival order)
    6. A user has the app open on their phone and their laptop at once. What happens? (refs 1,2 — tests: one connection per user, or correctly several)

    Cover The Sheet · Answer Aloud

    Use it as revision: cover the right rail and the two panels to your left, then walk the numbered path out loud. If you cannot say why a box is there and what breaks it, that box is your gap.

    Level 3 — The Edges (Advanced)

    Pacing: 10 min ask · 25 min trace flow · 15 min read why · 40 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Send a 1:1 message
    • Receive in real time while online
    • Delivery status: sent → delivered → read
    • Persists across devices, even offline
    • Group chat — smaller fan-out
    • Presence & typing — both ephemeral

    • End-to-end encryption
    • Media attachments (images / video)
    • Voice / video calls

    • (8) Group Fan-out — fans to N members; smaller than sheet 02's fan-out

    (7) Message ordering Per-conversation seq, assigned once, at write time Wall-clock/receipt order → two servers can persist out of send order

    7. Your connection registry goes down. What breaks? (ref 3 — tests: can you name the single most load-bearing state, unprompted)
