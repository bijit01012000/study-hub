    Distributed systems and microservices

    9 sections · 3,600 words

    Module 06 - 9 questions - 1 practical drill

    Turn distributed-systems theory into user-visible engineering choices.

    The trap in this round is reciting CAP and saga from a blog without ever having felt eventual consistency hurt a user. Follow-ups drag every abstraction back to "what did the user see when the network partitioned" and "who owned the compensation logic".

    Inside this module: CAP in practice - Service boundaries - Sagas - Consistency

    Q6.1 - Explain CAP theorem using a real decision you made, not the textbook definition.

    What it really tests: Whether CAP is lived experience or a memorised triangle. Interviewers are sick of the triangle.

    Weak answer: "CAP says you can only have two of consistency, availability, and partition tolerance. Since networks can fail, you must choose between consistency and availability."

    Why it fails: - Pure textbook restatement, no decision. - The "pick two" framing is imprecise - partition tolerance isn't optional. - No user-facing consequence described. - Never says which they chose for a real system or why.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. OWNERSHIP - During a partition, what did YOUR system choose - and what did the user see? - A real CP/AP decision. 2. TRADEOFF - Where in one product did you pick CP for one feature and AP for another? - Per-feature nuance. 3. FAILURE - What's the cost of choosing consistency during a partition? - Understands unavailability cost. 4. MEASUREMENT - How did you measure the eventual-consistency lag users experienced? - Observed staleness.

    Answer framework: 1. Restate CAP as behaviour under partition 2. Identify the feature's requirement 3. CP or AP choice 4. What the user experiences either way 5. Per-feature differentiation 6. Cost accepted 7. How you monitored it

    Rejection signals: - Only recites "pick two". - No real decision attached. - Doesn't tie to a user-visible outcome. - Thinks P is optional.

    Model answer skeleton: The useful framing of CAP isn't 'pick two' - partitions will happen, so the real question is what you do during one: refuse to answer (choose consistency) or answer with possibly-stale data (choose availability).

    In our system I split it by feature.

    For the wallet balance during a partition between regions I chose CP: if a node couldn't confirm it had the authoritative balance, it returned an error rather than risk a double-spend, so the user occasionally saw 'try again' - unavailability was the price of correctness on money.

    For the product catalogue I chose AP: during a partition, serving a slightly stale product list was completely fine, so nodes answered from local state and reconciled later.

    The cost of the CP choice was measurable brief unavailability, which I tracked as failed-balance-check rate, and for the AP path I monitored replication lag so I knew the worst-case staleness a user could see.

    Q6.2 - How did you handle a business transaction that spanned multiple services?

    What it really tests: Saga pattern understanding, and the reality that you can't do a distributed ACID transaction cheaply.

    Weak answer: "We made calls to each service in sequence, and if one failed we'd roll back the previous ones. It worked like a distributed transaction across services."

    Why it fails: - "Roll back the previous ones" hand-waves compensation - there's no free rollback across services. - Implies a distributed ACID transaction, which doesn't exist cheaply. - No mention of saga, compensation, or idempotency. - Doesn't consider partial-failure states.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. FAILURE - Service 3 fails after 1 and 2 committed - how do you undo committed work? - Compensating transactions. 2. TRADEOFF - Orchestration or choreography - which and why? - Saga style trade-offs. 3. TECHNICAL - Why not just use two-phase commit? - Knows 2PC's coordinator/blocking problems. 4. TECHNICAL - How did you make compensations idempotent? - Retry-safe compensation.

    Answer framework: 1. No cross-service ACID 2. Break into local transactions (saga) 3. Define compensating action per step 4. Orchestration vs choreography choice 5. Idempotent steps and compensations 6. Handle partial-failure states 7. Track saga state 8. Why not 2PC

    Rejection signals: - Assumes a magic distributed rollback. - Never heard of compensating transactions. - Reaches for 2PC without knowing its costs. - Compensations aren't idempotent.

    Model answer skeleton: There's no cheap ACID transaction across services, so I used a saga: the business transaction becomes a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating action that semantically undoes it. For 'place order' - reserve inventory, charge payment, create shipment - if the shipment step fails after payment committed, I don't 'roll back' payment, I run a compensation: issue a refund.

    I chose orchestration, a central coordinator that drives the steps and triggers compensations, because with three-plus steps choreography's event chains got hard to follow and debug; choreography's looser coupling wasn't worth the loss of a clear, auditable flow.

    Every step and every compensation is idempotent and keyed by a saga id, so retries don't double-charge or double-refund.

    I rejected two-phase commit because it needs a coordinator holding locks across services and blocks everyone if the coordinator dies mid-commit, which is exactly the availability hit we couldn't take.

    I stored the saga's state so a crashed orchestrator could resume.

    Q6.3 - When would you NOT break a system into microservices?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate has judgement or just believes microservices are always better.

    Weak answer: "Microservices are generally the better architecture because they scale independently and teams can work separately, so we'd usually go with them."

    Why it fails: - Treats microservices as a default good, no downsides. - Ignores the operational and distributed-systems tax they add. - No mention of team size or domain-boundary clarity. - Never names a case where a monolith is correct.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TRADEOFF - What complexity do microservices add that a monolith doesn't have? - Honest about the tax. 2. FAILURE - You split too early - what problems appear? - Premature-split pain. 3. TECHNICAL - How do unclear domain boundaries make microservices worse? - Boundary-driven design. 4. MEASUREMENT - What team size or scale justifies the split? - Concrete threshold thinking.

    Answer framework: 1. Microservices add distributed-systems tax 2. Small team / early product 3. Unclear domain boundaries 4. Chatty cross-service calls 5. Distributed transactions everywhere 6. Prefer modular monolith first 7. Split when a boundary and a scaling/team need are both clear

    Rejection signals: - Microservices always better. - No awareness of the operational tax. - Splits by technical layer, not domain. - No threshold for when to split.

    Model answer skeleton: I wouldn't go microservices for a small team on an early product, because they add a heavy tax: network calls that used to be function calls, distributed transactions where you had a local one, independent deploys to coordinate, and a lot more to observe and operate.

    Splitting before the domain boundaries are clear is the worst outcome - you draw the service seams in the wrong place, then every feature needs a chatty round-trip across three services and a distributed transaction to do what one database transaction did.

    I've seen a premature split turn a simple change into a four-repo, four-deploy coordination exercise.

    My default is a well-modularised monolith with clean internal boundaries, which gives you most of the team-organisation benefit without the network in the middle.

    I split out a service only when I can point to both a genuinely independent domain boundary and a concrete driver - a part that must scale separately, or a team that needs to own and deploy it independently.

    Q6.4 - Two services disagree about the state of the world. How does that happen and how do you fix it?

    What it really tests: Understanding of eventual consistency, reconciliation, and that distributed state drifts.

    Weak answer: "That shouldn't happen if the services are communicating properly. We'd just make sure they always sync their data correctly."

    Why it fails: - Denies that drift is normal in distributed systems. - "Sync correctly" is not a mechanism. - No reconciliation or source-of-truth concept. - Doesn't consider dropped events or partial failures as causes.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - An event is dropped or processed twice - how do the two states diverge? - Understands drift causes. 2. OWNERSHIP - Which service is the source of truth, and how does the other reconcile? - Reconciliation design. 3. MEASUREMENT - How would you detect the divergence before a user does? - Drift detection. 4. FAILURE - How do you repair state without double-applying? - Idempotent reconciliation.

    Answer framework: 1. Accept drift is normal 2. Causes (lost/duplicate events, partial failures) 3. Define single source of truth 4. Periodic reconciliation job 5. Idempotent repair 6. Detect drift with metrics/audits 7. Alert before users notice

    Rejection signals: - Claims drift shouldn't happen. - No source of truth. - No reconciliation mechanism. - Repair double-applies changes.

    Model answer skeleton: In any event-driven system state drift is normal, not a bug - an event gets dropped, processed twice, or a consumer is down during a change, and two services' views diverge. So I design for it rather than deny it.

    First I name a single source of truth for each piece of data - say the orders service owns order status - and everyone else holds a derived copy they reconcile against it.

    I run a periodic reconciliation job that compares the derived state to the source and repairs mismatches, and crucially that repair is idempotent and set-based, so re-running it or applying it to an already-correct record does nothing.

    To catch drift early I emit a metric for reconciliation mismatches and alert if it climbs, so we find divergence from monitoring rather than from a customer complaint.

    The mental model I keep is: assume messages can be lost or duplicated, keep one authority, and continuously reconcile toward it.

    Q6.5 - How do services find and call each other, and what happens when one is unhealthy?

    What it really tests: Service discovery, health checks, and load balancer behaviour toward unhealthy instances.

    Weak answer: "Services call each other through a load balancer or service discovery. If one is down the load balancer routes around it."

    Why it fails: - "Routes around it" glosses over how unhealthy is detected. - No mention of health checks, readiness vs liveness. - Doesn't address a slow-but-not-dead instance. - No client-side timeout or retry consideration.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - How does the load balancer know an instance is unhealthy? - Health-check mechanics. 2. TRADEOFF - Liveness vs readiness - what's the difference and why care? - Probe semantics. 3. FAILURE - An instance is slow but passing health checks - what happens? - Grey-failure awareness. 4. MEASUREMENT - How do you avoid retrying into an already-overloaded instance? - Retry + circuit breaker interplay.

    Answer framework: 1. Discovery mechanism 2. Health checks (liveness vs readiness) 3. LB removes unhealthy instances 4. Grey failures (slow but up) 5. Client timeouts + circuit breakers 6. Avoid retry amplification 7. Graceful shutdown/draining

    Rejection signals: - "Routes around it" with no detection mechanism. - Confuses liveness and readiness. - No handling of slow-but-alive instances. - Retries blindly into overload.

    Model answer skeleton: Services resolve each other through service discovery or a load balancer, and the LB only routes to instances passing health checks.

    I distinguish liveness - is the process alive, restart it if not - from readiness - is it ready to take traffic, pull it from the pool if not without killing it, which matters during startup or when a dependency is temporarily down.

    The hard case is grey failure: an instance that's slow but still passes a shallow health check keeps getting traffic and drags latency up, so I make health checks meaningful and lean on client-side timeouts plus circuit breakers so callers stop hammering a struggling instance rather than piling on.

    I'm careful that retries don't amplify an overload - a naive retry into an already-saturated instance makes it worse - so retries have backoff and the breaker trips fast.

    On deploys I use graceful draining: an instance goes unready, finishes in-flight requests, then exits, so we don't drop live traffic.

    Q6.6 - How do you trace a single user request across ten services?

    What it really tests: Distributed tracing and correlation - essential for debugging microservices.

    Weak answer: "We had logging in each service, so we'd look at the logs to see what happened as the request went through."

    Why it fails: - Independent logs with no correlation id can't be stitched together. - No trace/span concept. - Manually grepping ten log sets is infeasible at scale. - No propagation of context across service boundaries.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - How does a correlation/trace id get from service 1 to service 10? - Context propagation. 2. TECHNICAL - What's the difference between a trace and a span? - Tracing model. 3. MEASUREMENT - How do you find which of the ten services added the latency? - Latency attribution. 4. FAILURE - What happens to the trace when the request goes async through a queue? - Trace continuity across async.

    Answer framework: 1. Generate a trace id at the edge 2. Propagate via headers 3. Each service creates spans 4. Spans nest under the trace 5. Export to a tracing backend 6. Visualise the waterfall 7. Attribute latency 8. Propagate context across async boundaries

    Rejection signals: - Relies on uncorrelated per-service logs. - No trace id propagation. - Can't distinguish trace from span. - Loses the trace across a queue.

    Model answer skeleton: Independent logs are useless here unless they share a correlation id, so I use distributed tracing. At the edge we generate a trace id, and it propagates through every hop via headers - W3C traceparent with OpenTelemetry - so all ten services stamp the same trace id on their logs and spans.

    A trace is the whole request; a span is one unit of work within it, like one service's handling or one DB call, and spans nest to form a waterfall.

    I export spans to a backend like Jaeger or Datadog, where I can see the request as a timeline and immediately spot which service's span dominates the latency instead of guessing.

    The subtle part is async: when the request crosses a Kafka queue, the trace id has to ride in the message headers and the consumer must continue the trace, otherwise the waterfall breaks at the queue.

    With that in place, debugging a slow request is looking at one trace, not grepping ten log streams.

    Q6.7 - How do you deploy a change to a service that ten other services depend on, without breaking them?

    What it really tests: Backward compatibility, contract testing, and safe rollout across service boundaries.

    Weak answer: "We'd coordinate with the other teams, deploy the change, and let them know so they could update their code if needed."

    Why it fails: - Relies on human coordination instead of compatible contracts. - Implies a lockstep deploy across teams, which doesn't scale. - No backward-compatibility discipline. - No contract testing to catch breakage automatically.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - How do you change the API so consumers don't need a coordinated deploy? - Backward-compatible evolution. 2. TRADEOFF - What is consumer-driven contract testing? - Automated compatibility verification. 3. FAILURE - You must remove a field ten consumers use - what's the sequence? - Expand/contract across services. 4. MEASUREMENT - How do you roll out and roll back safely? - Canary + rollback.

    Answer framework: 1. Backward-compatible-by-default 2. Additive changes need no coordination 3. Contract tests gate the pipeline 4. Expand/contract for removals 5. Deprecate with usage metrics 6. Canary rollout 7. Fast rollback path

    Rejection signals: - Depends on manual cross-team coordination. - Lockstep deploys. - No contract tests. - Removes fields without a migration sequence.

    Model answer skeleton: The goal is that my deploy never requires a coordinated deploy from ten other teams, which means backward compatibility is the discipline, not coordination.

    Additive changes - a new optional field, a new endpoint - go out freely because well-behaved consumers ignore unknowns.

    I gate my pipeline with consumer-driven contract tests: each consumer publishes the shape it expects, and my build fails if I break any of them, so I catch incompatibility in CI rather than in their production.

    To remove a field ten consumers use, I use expand/contract across the boundary: keep serving the old field while adding the new shape, get every consumer migrated (I watch per-field usage metrics to know when it's safe), then remove it in a later release.

    Rollout is a canary - a small slice of traffic first, watching error and latency - with an automated rollback if the canary's metrics regress, so a bad change is contained to a fraction of requests, not all ten dependents at once.

    Q6.8 - What consistency does your system actually give users, and where does it fall short of what they expect?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate can honestly characterise their system's consistency model rather than claim it's "consistent".

    Weak answer: "Our system is consistent - when you write data, you read it back correctly. We didn't really have consistency issues."

    Why it fails: - Claims blanket strong consistency, which is rarely true in a distributed system. - No distinction between different data paths. - Denies eventual consistency exists in their system. - No user-facing example of staleness.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. FAILURE - A user updates their profile and a replica hasn't caught up - what do they see? - Read-your-writes reality. 2. TRADEOFF - Where is your system strongly consistent and where eventually? - Per-path honesty. 3. TECHNICAL - How did you hide eventual consistency from the user? - UX mitigations. 4. MEASUREMENT - How did you measure the staleness window? - Observed lag.

    Answer framework: 1. Map data paths to consistency models 2. Strong where invariants demand 3. Eventual where it's cheap and tolerable 4. Read-your-writes handling 5. UX mitigations for staleness 6. Measured lag 7. Honest about the gaps

    Rejection signals: - Claims everything is strongly consistent. - Denies any eventual consistency. - No read-your-writes handling. - Never measured staleness.

    Model answer skeleton: Honestly, our system is a mix, and pretending it's uniformly strongly consistent would be wrong. The money paths - wallet balance, order status - are strongly consistent because the invariants demand it, so those reads go to the primary.

    But a lot of reads served from replicas or caches are eventually consistent, and the classic gap is read-your-writes: a user updates their display name, then a page reads from a lagging replica and shows the old name for a second, which feels broken to them even though nothing's wrong.

    I mitigated that by routing a user's own reads to the primary for a short window after they write, or by updating the UI optimistically so it reflects their change immediately while the backend catches up.

    I measured the replica lag so I knew the realistic staleness window was sub-second normally but could spike under write load, and I alerted on it.

    The point is I can tell you exactly where it's strong, where it's eventual, and what a user might notice.

    Q6.9 - [DRILL] Design the 'place order' flow across inventory, payment, and shipping services. Name each step, its compensation, and what happens if the service crashes mid-flow.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate can build a complete saga with compensations and crash recovery, not just describe the concept.

    Weak answer: N/A - drill. A weak attempt lists the three calls with no compensations.

    Why it fails: - No compensating action for a step that already committed. - No saga state, so a crash loses track of progress. - Steps aren't idempotent, so recovery re-runs and double-applies. - No handling of the compensation itself failing.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. FAILURE - Payment succeeds, shipping fails - what exactly runs? - Compensation trigger. 2. TECHNICAL - The orchestrator crashes after payment - how does it resume? - Persisted saga state. 3. REFLECTION - A compensation (refund) itself fails - now what? - Compensation-of-last-resort.

    Answer framework: 1. Persist saga state at each step 2. Step 1 reserve inventory / comp: release 3. Step 2 charge payment / comp: refund 4. Step 3 create shipment / comp: cancel 5. Idempotent steps keyed by saga id 6. On crash, resume from persisted state 7. Retry failed compensations 8. Escalate stuck sagas

    Rejection signals: - No compensations defined. - No persisted saga state. - Steps not idempotent. - No plan for a failing compensation.

    Model answer skeleton: Drill output: an orchestrator drives three steps and persists the saga state after each. Step 1 reserves inventory; its compensation releases the reservation. Step 2 charges payment; its compensation issues a refund. Step 3 creates the shipment; its compensation cancels it.

    Every step is idempotent and keyed by the saga id, so a retry can't reserve twice or charge twice.

    If shipping fails after payment committed, the orchestrator runs compensations in reverse - refund payment, release inventory - leaving the system as if the order never happened. If the orchestrator itself crashes after charging payment, it recovers from the persisted saga state on restart and continues from step 3 rather than re-charging.

    The nastiest case is a compensation failing - a refund that won't go through - so failed compensations are retried with backoff, and if they stay stuck they're escalated to a dead-letter/alert for a human, because silently leaving a customer charged for an order that won't ship is the one outcome we never accept.
