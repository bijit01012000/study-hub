    Conflict

    7 sections · 2,972 words

    Module 02 · 6 questions · 1 practical drill

    Show you can disagree hard on the merits, then commit fully without resentment.

    Conflict questions are asked in the majority of behavioural loops, especially at collaboration-heavy companies. The trap is choosing a conflict too trivial to matter, or telling it in a way that makes you the blameless hero and the other person a villain. The follow-ups hunt for the actual disagreement (not a vague "difference of opinion"), your specific position, how the OTHER side saw it, and - the killer - what you did when you LOST. Disagree-and-commit maturity beats being proven right.

    Q2.1 Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague.

    What it really tests. Whether you can hold a position with substance, engage the other side's view fairly, and drive to a resolution without it becoming personal.

    Weak answer

    "A colleague and I disagreed about how to structure some code. We each had our opinion, we talked it through, and eventually we found a compromise in the middle that we were both happy with. It was all very professional."

    Why it fails

    • "How to structure some code" - no actual substance of the disagreement.
    • "Compromise in the middle" is offered as a virtue when the point may have needed a right answer.
    • No statement of your position OR theirs - both are blank.
    • "Both happy, very professional" sands off everything real.
    • No outcome showing the disagreement mattered.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "What exactly were the two options on the table?" - The real substance behind 'a disagreement'.
    2. Tradeoff - "What was their strongest argument - the one that gave you pause?" - Whether you engaged their view or dismissed it.
    3. Measurement - "How did you decide, and did the outcome prove either of you right?" - Resolution and result over opinion.
    4. Reflection - "Was 'meeting in the middle' actually the right call, or a dodge?" - Whether compromise was principled or conflict-avoidant.

    Answer framework

    1. Context.
    2. The actual disagreement (two concrete options).
    3. Your position and reasoning.
    4. Their position, fairly stated.
    5. How you raised it.
    6. How it was decided.
    7. Outcome.
    8. What you learned about the two approaches.

    Rejection signals

    • Can't state the actual technical substance.
    • Frames the colleague as simply wrong or difficult.
    • "Compromise" used to avoid ever having a real position.
    • No outcome.

    Model answer skeleton

    A colleague and I disagreed on how to handle a third-party API that was flaky: he wanted to add aggressive retries in our service, I wanted a circuit breaker plus a fallback. His argument, which genuinely gave me pause, was that retries were a two-line change and we could ship today.

    My position was that blind retries would amplify load on an already-struggling dependency and could turn a partial outage into a full one - a retry storm. I raised it by pulling the dependency's error-rate graph and showing that failures were correlated, not random, so retries would pile on exactly when it was weakest. We agreed to do both but bounded: limited retries WITH a circuit breaker, which was actually better than either original idea. When that dependency had a real outage two months later, we degraded gracefully instead of falling over.

    What I learned is that the other person's constraint - ship speed - was real, and the best answer usually respects it rather than ignoring it.

    Q2.2 Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager or a senior engineer.

    What it really tests. Whether you can push back up the hierarchy respectfully with evidence - and, crucially, commit fully when the decision goes against you.

    Weak answer

    "I usually go along with my manager because they have more context than me. There was one time I wasn't sure about a decision, but I mentioned it once and then went with what they wanted since they're more senior and know best."

    Why it fails

    • Pure deference - signals no independent judgement.
    • "Mentioned it once" is not really raising a disagreement.
    • "They know best" abdicates responsibility for the outcome.
    • No technical substance to the disagreement.
    • No outcome, so it reads as a non-event.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "What was your specific technical argument?" - Substance behind the disagreement.
    2. Ownership - "How did you raise it without undermining them?" - Disagreeing up the hierarchy constructively.
    3. Reflection - "Say they overruled you - how did you commit afterwards?" - Disagree-and-commit maturity.
    4. Measurement - "Did the outcome show either of you right?" - Result over rank.

    Answer framework

    1. Context.
    2. Your position with reasoning.
    3. Their position and their real constraint.
    4. How you raised it (data, privately).
    5. How it was decided.
    6. How you committed either way.
    7. Outcome.
    8. What you learned about when to push and when to yield.

    Rejection signals

    • Always defers; no genuine disagreement.
    • No technical substance.
    • Sulked, slow-walked, or undermined after losing.
    • Can't describe committing to a decision they disagreed with.

    Model answer skeleton

    My tech lead wanted to add a Redis cache to fix a slow endpoint; I disagreed because my profiling showed the cause was an N+1 query, not repeated identical reads, so caching would mask a bug and add invalidation complexity for data that changed constantly.

    I raised it in a one-on-one rather than in the group, and I brought the trace and the EXPLAIN output - framing it as 'here's what the data shows' not 'you're wrong'. He was persuaded and we fixed the query, which dropped p95 further than a cache would have. On a separate call - adopting a particular framework - I disagreed, said my piece once, lost, and then fully backed it: I wrote the onboarding docs for it rather than dragging my feet, because relitigating a decided call helps nobody.

    What I learned is that disagreement lands when it's evidence-based and private, and that committing wholeheartedly after a 'no' matters as much as arguing well before it.

    Q2.3 Tell me about a conflict you handled badly.

    What it really tests. Self-awareness - whether you can own a time your own behaviour made a conflict worse, rather than dressing up a success as a failure.

    Weak answer

    "Hmm, I can't really think of one where I handled it badly. I'm pretty easy-going and I don't really get into conflicts. If anything I'm too accommodating, so any conflicts usually resolve themselves."

    Why it fails

    • Dodges the question - "I don't get into conflicts" is not believable for an experienced engineer.
    • "Too accommodating" is the humble-brag weakness in disguise.
    • No self-awareness about any real misstep.
    • Signals either no reflection or an unwillingness to be honest.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Everyone's mishandled one - what's the closest you've come?" - Willingness to be honest about a real flaw.
    2. Reflection - "What did you do or say that you regret?" - Concrete self-critique.
    3. Measurement - "What was the actual cost of handling it that way?" - Honest about consequences.
    4. Technical - "What do you do differently now because of it?" - A behaviour change, not just remorse.

    Answer framework

    1. The conflict.
    2. What you actually did wrong.
    3. Your honest role in escalating it.
    4. The cost (relationship, delivery, trust).
    5. How it was eventually repaired.
    6. What specifically changed in your behaviour.
    7. Evidence the change stuck.

    Rejection signals

    • Claims to have never mishandled conflict.
    • Reframes a win as the "badly handled" story.
    • Blames the other person even in a story about their own failure.
    • No behaviour change resulted.

    Model answer skeleton

    Early on I handled one badly. A colleague kept approving PRs that I thought lowered our test quality, and instead of talking to him, I started leaving pointed public comments on his reviews - technically correct, but I was clearly scoring points.

    It escalated: he got defensive, our reviews turned into a slow tit-for-tat, and a feature slipped by about a week because we were bottlenecking each other. What I did wrong was making it public and making it about being right rather than fixing the actual standard. It got repaired when a senior pulled us both aside and I realised I'd been the aggressor; I apologised directly and we agreed on a written testing bar so it wasn't personal opinion any more. What changed is that I now raise this kind of thing in a private message first and push for a written standard rather than litigating case by case.

    I've caught myself about to do the public thing since and stopped - that's how I know it stuck.

    Q2.4 Tell me about a time you strongly disagreed with a decision but had to go along with it.

    What it really tests. Disagree-and-commit in its purest form - whether you can back a decision you argued against, wholeheartedly, without sabotaging it.

    Weak answer

    "There was a decision I didn't agree with, so I went along with it but I made sure everyone knew I'd been against it. When it caused problems later, I reminded the team that I'd predicted it. At least I was proven right in the end."

    Why it fails

    • "Made sure everyone knew I was against it" is the opposite of committing.
    • "Reminded the team I predicted it" is 'I-told-you-so', a serious red flag.
    • Being proven right is treated as the goal instead of the team succeeding.
    • Shows no ability to back a decision you lost.
    • Signals someone who protects their ego over the outcome.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "Did you do anything to help it succeed, or just wait to be right?" - Active commitment vs quiet sabotage.
    2. Ownership - "What did 'going along with it' actually look like day to day?" - Genuine commitment vs passive compliance.
    3. Tradeoff - "How did you raise your concern before the decision was final?" - Whether the disagreement was aired properly first.
    4. Reflection - "Looking back, were you actually right - or did it work out?" - Intellectual honesty over ego.

    Answer framework

    1. The decision and your disagreement.
    2. How you argued it before it was final.
    3. The decision going against you.
    4. How you chose to commit.
    5. What you actively did to make it work.
    6. The honest outcome.
    7. What you learned about committing.

    Rejection signals

    • "I told you so" energy.
    • Treats being right as the win.
    • Passive compliance dressed up as commitment.
    • Secretly hoped it would fail.

    Model answer skeleton

    My team decided to build a feature in-house that I argued we should buy off the shelf - I thought we were underestimating the maintenance burden, and I made that case with a rough estimate of the ongoing cost. The team, including people I respected, went the other way for good reasons around control and data residency.

    Once it was decided, I made a deliberate choice not to be the person waiting to be proven right - I volunteered to own the trickiest part of the build, the sync logic, because if we were doing it, I wanted it done well. It turned out better than I'd predicted; the control it gave us actually paid off in a compliance audit later, and I was partly wrong about the maintenance cost because the team invested in tooling I hadn't accounted for.

    What I learned is that committing means actively helping it win, and that 'I was against this' is worth nothing next to 'I made it work anyway'.

    Q2.5 Tell me about a disagreement over a technical approach or a code review.

    What it really tests. Whether you can debate technical substance with rigour, change your mind on evidence, and keep review disagreements about the code rather than the person.

    Weak answer

    "In code reviews I hold a high bar, so sometimes people push back on my comments. Usually I'm right about the technical issue, so I just explain why my way is correct until they understand and make the change. Eventually they come around."

    Why it fails

    • "Usually I'm right" assumes the candidate is the authority by default.
    • "Until they understand" frames review as one-way education, not debate.
    • No instance of the candidate being persuaded or wrong.
    • "They come around" implies wearing people down, not convincing them.
    • No sense that the other engineer might have had a point.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Reflection - "Tell me about a review where THEY changed YOUR mind." - Whether you're persuadable, not just persuasive.
    2. Technical - "What was the actual technical crux you disagreed on?" - Real substance over authority.
    3. Ownership - "How do you keep a review disagreement about the code, not the person?" - Depersonalising conflict.
    4. Tradeoff - "When is 'my way is better' not worth blocking a PR over?" - Distinguishing must-fix from preference.

    Answer framework

    1. Context.
    2. The technical crux (two concrete approaches).
    3. Your position and theirs.
    4. How you argued it on evidence.
    5. Whether you or they moved.
    6. How it was resolved.
    7. The outcome.
    8. How you keep reviews depersonalised.

    Rejection signals

    • Never been persuaded in a review.
    • Treats own preference as objective correctness.
    • Blocks PRs over style, not substance.
    • Frames colleagues as needing to be taught.

    Model answer skeleton

    A junior submitted a PR that handled a batch job with a big in-memory list, and I flagged it as a memory risk and pushed for streaming. He pushed back with something I hadn't weighed: the batch was capped at a few thousand rows by an upstream constraint, so the memory cost was trivial and streaming would add real complexity for no benefit.

    He was right, and I said so on the PR - I changed my comment to a non-blocking note about revisiting it if the cap ever changed, and I added a comment in the code documenting that assumption. The crux was really 'is this data bounded', and I'd assumed it wasn't. I keep reviews about the code by writing 'this list could grow unbounded' rather than 'you should have streamed this', and by separating must-fix from nit so people aren't drowning.

    The outcome was a simpler PR merged same day and, I think, a junior who felt safe pushing back on a senior - which is what you want.

    Q2.6 Practical drill - Conflict

    Write your best conflict story in the Context → actual disagreement → your position → their position → resolution → outcome format, then script your answer to the "you lost the argument - what did you do next" follow-up.

    What it really tests. Forces you to prepare a conflict story with real substance on BOTH sides and to rehearse the disagree-and-commit follow-up that sinks most candidates.

    Weak answer

    N/A - drill. A weak attempt has no real substance to the disagreement, casts the other person as simply wrong, and has no answer for the "you lost" follow-up beyond "I was proven right later".

    Why it fails

    • The disagreement has no concrete two-sided substance.
    • The other person is a villain or an idiot, never reasonable.
    • No answer prepared for the "what if you lost" turn.
    • The story secretly ends in "and I was right", avoiding commitment.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Tradeoff - "State the other person's position as strongly as your own - can you?" - Steel-manning the other side.
    2. Ownership - "In your scripted 'you lost' answer, what did you actively do to help it succeed?" - Active commitment, not passive compliance.
    3. Reflection - "Where in this story could an interviewer catch you blaming?" - Pre-empting the blame red flag.

    Answer framework

    1. Pick a conflict with real technical substance.
    2. Write both positions so each sounds reasonable.
    3. State how you raised it (data, private).
    4. Write the resolution and outcome with a result.
    5. Then write a second version where you LOST.
    6. In the lost version, name what you did to help the decision succeed.
    7. Rehearse both to ~90 seconds.

    Rejection signals

    • Can't state the other side fairly.
    • The "you lost" branch ends in vindication, not commitment.
    • Blame leaks into the framing.
    • No result on either branch.

    Model answer skeleton

    Drill output: Story - 'I wanted a circuit breaker on a flaky dependency; my colleague wanted quick retries (his real constraint: ship today). I showed failures were correlated, so retries would storm it; we shipped bounded retries WITH a breaker and degraded gracefully in a later real outage.' Both positions stated fairly: his ship-speed point was legitimate, not dumb.

    The 'you lost' branch I scripted: 'On a separate call I argued to buy not build, lost, and then volunteered to own the hardest part of the build - the sync logic - so it'd be done well; it worked out and I was partly wrong on the maintenance cost.' The blame risk I flagged is the word "flaky" about the dependency - I'll say "unreliable under load" so I'm describing behaviour, not throwing another team under the bus. Rehearsed both to about 85 seconds.
