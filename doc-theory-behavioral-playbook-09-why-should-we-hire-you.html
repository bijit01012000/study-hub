    Why should we hire you

    7 sections · 2,721 words

    Module 09 · 6 questions · 1 practical drill

    Map evidenced strengths to their actual problems - fit, not flattery.

    These closing questions - why you, why us, strengths, weaknesses - reward candidates who've done their homework on the company and can map their genuine strengths to the role's actual problems. The failure mode is generic self-praise ("I'm a hard worker and a team player") that fits any job, or a fake weakness ("I'm a perfectionist"). The follow-ups push for evidence behind every claimed strength and honesty behind the weakness.

    Q9.1 Why should we hire you?

    What it really tests. Whether you can connect your specific, evidenced strengths to the company's actual needs - making it about solving their problem, not listing your virtues.

    Weak answer

    "You should hire me because I'm a hard worker, I'm passionate about technology, I'm a fast learner, and I work well in a team. I'm confident I'd be a great fit and I'd bring a lot of value to the role. I really want this job."

    Why it fails

    • Generic virtues ("hard worker, fast learner, team player") that fit any candidate.
    • No evidence behind a single claim.
    • Nothing connected to this company's specific needs.
    • "I really want this job" is about the candidate's desire, not their value.
    • No differentiation from any other applicant.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Everyone says 'hard worker' - what specifically can you do that others can't?" - Genuine differentiation.
    2. Technical - "Which of our actual problems would you solve, and how?" - Homework on the role and its needs.
    3. Measurement - "What's the evidence you're a fast learner - an example?" - Proof behind a claim.
    4. Reflection - "Where would you NOT be the right hire?" - Honesty about fit.

    Answer framework

    1. Their key problem (from the JD/research).
    2. Your directly relevant strength.
    3. Evidence you've done exactly this.
    4. What you'd do in the first months.
    5. Your honest differentiator.
    6. An honest note on fit.
    7. Close on the value, not the wanting.

    Rejection signals

    • Generic virtues, no evidence.
    • Nothing tied to the company's needs.
    • Can't differentiate from other candidates.
    • Makes it about wanting the job, not solving their problem.

    Model answer skeleton

    From the JD and our conversations, your core problem right now is scaling the payments platform reliably while the team's still small - you need someone who can own reliability end to end, not just write features. That's exactly what I've done: at my current company I took a payment flow that failed ~2% of orders under peak load and re-architected it to event-driven with idempotent workers, and we then took a 5x-bigger peak with zero dropped orders. So I'm not learning reliability on your dime - I've done this specific thing.

    In my first months I'd focus on your failure paths and on-call load, because that's where the risk hides at your stage. My honest differentiator is that I've lived the incident side, so I design for what breaks, not just the happy path. Where I'd be less of a fit is if you wanted a pure frontend specialist - that's not me. But for owning payments reliability, I've done the exact problem one stage earlier, and I'd bring that straight in.

    Q9.2 Why do you want to work here specifically?

    What it really tests. Whether you've genuinely researched the company and have a real reason tied to their work - not flattery or generic "great culture and growth" that fits anywhere.

    Weak answer

    "I've heard great things about the company and the culture seems really good. It's a well-known company with a strong reputation, and I think it would be a great place to grow my career. I'm excited about the opportunity to work with talented people here."

    Why it fails

    • "Heard great things" and "good culture" are generic and unresearched.
    • "Well-known with a strong reputation" is flattery, not a reason.
    • "Grow my career" makes it about the candidate, not the company's work.
    • Nothing specific about what THIS company actually does.
    • Interchangeable with any other employer.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "What do you actually know about how we work or what we're building?" - Depth of homework.
    2. Technical - "What specifically about our product or tech drew you?" - Real research vs generic praise.
    3. Reflection - "How does this connect to what you want to do next?" - A genuine fit, not just wanting a job.
    4. Tradeoff - "What would make you turn US down?" - Honest evaluation, not desperation.

    Answer framework

    1. A specific thing about their product/tech/mission.
    2. Why it genuinely interests you.
    3. How it connects to your trajectory.
    4. What you know about how they work.
    5. An honest reservation you'd want to explore.
    6. Why the fit is real.

    Rejection signals

    • Generic "great culture and reputation".
    • No specific knowledge of the company.
    • Makes it entirely about their own growth.
    • Interchangeable with any employer.

    Model answer skeleton

    The specific draw is that you're building payments infrastructure for markets with unreliable connectivity, which is a genuinely harder version of the reliability problem I've spent years on - offline-tolerant, idempotent, reconciliation-heavy - and I read your engineering blog post on how you handle duplicate-charge prevention across flaky networks, which is exactly the class of problem I find interesting.

    It connects to my trajectory because I want to go deeper on payments correctness at scale, and that's your core, not a side concern. From researching you, I also like that engineers own services end to end including on-call, which matches how I work. An honest reservation I'd want to explore is how you balance that ownership against burnout, which I'd ask about - but the reason the fit is real is that your central technical problem is the one I most want to be working on, not a generic 'good company'.

    Q9.3 What's your greatest strength?

    What it really tests. Whether you can name a genuine, relevant strength and back it with evidence, rather than claiming a generic virtue.

    Weak answer

    "My greatest strength is probably my problem-solving ability. I'm really good at solving problems and figuring things out. I also work well under pressure and I'm very detail-oriented. I'd say those are my main strengths."

    Why it fails

    • "Problem-solving" is the most generic engineering strength possible.
    • No evidence or example behind it.
    • Lists several strengths, diluting the answer to nothing specific.
    • "Detail-oriented" and "work well under pressure" are filler.
    • Nothing tied to the role.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "How is that strength specifically useful for THIS role?" - Relevance vs a generic virtue.
    2. Ownership - "How do you know it's a strength - has someone told you?" - External validation vs self-assessment.
    3. Measurement - "Give me a concrete example where that strength changed an outcome." - Evidence behind the claim.
    4. Reflection - "What's the flip side of that strength - where does it cost you?" - Self-awareness about the trade-off.

    Answer framework

    1. The specific strength (narrow, real).
    2. A concrete example where it mattered.
    3. The evidence it's genuine.
    4. Why it fits this role.
    5. The honest flip side.
    6. How you manage that.

    Rejection signals

    • Generic "problem-solving".
    • No example.
    • Lists many strengths, commits to none.
    • Not connected to the role.

    Model answer skeleton

    My genuine strength is diagnosing problems in production under pressure - staying methodical when a system's on fire and people are stressed. Concretely, during a checkout outage last year I was on call, and while others were guessing at causes I worked the signals in order - recent deploys, then error patterns, then the database - and traced it to a config change in a recent deploy that had quietly halved a database timeout, so queries that used to run slow were now erroring outright.

    That took about ten minutes to pin down, and I mitigated by rolling the deploy back before digging any further. My manager specifically called it out afterwards, so it's not just my own view - I've become the person people want on the bridge during incidents. It fits this role because you run lean on-call and need people who don't panic.

    The honest flip side is that this calm can make me a bit slow to raise alarms early - I sometimes assume I can handle it solo - so I've had to consciously practise pulling others in sooner rather than quietly firefighting.

    Q9.4 What's your greatest weakness?

    What it really tests. Self-awareness and honesty - whether you can name a real weakness and show active work on it, rather than a disguised strength or a throwaway.

    Weak answer

    "My greatest weakness is probably that I'm a perfectionist - I care too much about getting things exactly right, so sometimes I spend too long polishing. But I'm working on being okay with 'good enough'. I guess I just have high standards."

    Why it fails

    • "Perfectionist" is the most transparent fake weakness there is.
    • Framed as a strength in disguise ("high standards").
    • No real cost or example.
    • "Working on it" is generic with no concrete action.
    • Reads as evasive - refuses to expose a genuine flaw.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "That's a humble-brag - what's a weakness that's actually cost you?" - Willingness to name a real flaw.
    2. Measurement - "Give me a specific time it caused a problem." - A concrete cost.
    3. Technical - "What concretely are you doing about it?" - Active mitigation vs 'working on it'.
    4. Reflection - "How do you know it's improving?" - Evidence of change.

    Answer framework

    1. A genuine weakness.
    2. A specific time it cost something.
    3. Why it's a real limitation.
    4. The concrete steps you're taking.
    5. How you check it's improving.
    6. How you contain it in the meantime.

    Rejection signals

    • Disguised strength ("perfectionist", "work too hard").
    • No real cost or example.
    • Vague "working on it".
    • No evidence of improvement.

    Model answer skeleton

    A real one: I've tended to under-communicate when I'm heads-down on a hard problem - I go quiet for a day or two because I'm absorbed, and it leaves people guessing about status. It cost me concretely once: I was deep in debugging a nasty issue and didn't update my PM, who then got blindsided in her own stakeholder meeting because she thought we were on track and we weren't. That's a genuine limitation, not a humble-brag - silence isn't a virtue.

    What I'm concretely doing is a standing rule for myself: end-of-day status even when there's nothing pretty to report, especially a 'still stuck on X, here's where I am'. I check it's working by asking my PM directly whether she feels in the loop, and she's said the difference is noticeable. In the meantime I contain it by defaulting to over-communicating when a task is at risk, which is the exact moment I used to go dark.

    Q9.5 What makes you different from the other candidates we're seeing?

    What it really tests. Whether you have a genuine, specific differentiator and the self-knowledge to name it - without either arrogance or generic claims.

    Weak answer

    "I think what makes me different is my passion and dedication. I really care about my work and I'll go the extra mile. A lot of people can do the technical side, but I bring enthusiasm and a great attitude, and I think that sets me apart from others."

    Why it fails

    • "Passion, dedication, enthusiasm, great attitude" are claimed by everyone.
    • Implies other candidates lack these, which is presumptuous and unprovable.
    • No specific, verifiable differentiator.
    • No evidence.
    • Confuses attitude with distinctiveness.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "Attitude isn't a differentiator - what specific experience sets you apart?" - A concrete, verifiable difference.
    2. Ownership - "What's the combination of things few people have?" - A genuine, specific edge.
    3. Measurement - "What evidence backs that up?" - Proof, not assertion.
    4. Reflection - "Where might another candidate genuinely beat you?" - Honesty and realism about competition.

    Answer framework

    1. A specific, uncommon combination of skills/experience.
    2. Why that combination is rare.
    3. Evidence you have it.
    4. How it's directly useful here.
    5. Where you'd concede another candidate might be stronger.
    6. Why your edge still matters for this role.

    Rejection signals

    • Generic "passion and attitude".
    • Implies others lack basic virtues.
    • No specific or verifiable edge.
    • No humility about the competition.

    Model answer skeleton

    I won't claim I care more than the next candidate - everyone in your pipeline is probably keen. What's actually specific is the combination I bring: deep payments-reliability experience AND real time embedded in the on-call and incident side of it.

    Plenty of engineers can design a payment system on a whiteboard; fewer have been paged at 3am when it double-charged customers and had to build the reconciliation to stop it. That combination - designing for correctness because I've cleaned up the failures - is rarer than either half alone. The evidence is the re-architecture I led that took a 2% failure rate to zero dropped orders at 5x load.

    Where another candidate might beat me is raw distributed-data depth - if you've got someone who's built a database internally, they'll out-depth me there. But for owning payments reliability end to end including the operational reality, that lived incident experience is my genuine edge.

    Q9.6 Practical drill - Why should we hire you

    Write your "why you, why us" fit statement: name three of the company's actual problems (from the JD, their blog, their product), and map a specific evidenced strength of yours to each.

    What it really tests. Forces the homework that separates a tailored, credible closing pitch from generic self-praise - mapping real strengths to the company's real problems.

    Weak answer

    N/A - drill. A weak attempt lists generic strengths, names no specific company problems, and could be sent to any employer unchanged.

    Why it fails

    • Strengths are generic and evidence-free.
    • No specific company problems identified.
    • The statement is interchangeable across employers.
    • No mapping between a strength and a problem.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "Name the three problems - from where, specifically?" - Real research into the company's needs.
    2. Measurement - "For each, what's the evidence you can solve it?" - Evidence-backed mapping.
    3. Reflection - "Which of the three mappings is weakest, and how would you shore it up?" - Self-audit of the pitch.

    Answer framework

    1. Research three real problems (JD, blog, product, news).
    2. For each, name a specific strength of yours.
    3. Attach concrete evidence to each strength.
    4. Write the mapping as three tight statements.
    5. Identify the weakest mapping.
    6. Prepare to shore it up.
    7. Rehearse the whole thing to under a minute.

    Rejection signals

    • Generic strengths, no company problems.
    • No evidence in the mappings.
    • Reusable for any employer.
    • No self-audit of the weakest link.

    Model answer skeleton

    Drill output - three problems and mappings: (1) Problem from the JD: 'scale payments reliably with a small team.' My strength: reliability re-architecture. Evidence: took 2% order-failure to zero at 5x peak. (2) Problem from their blog: 'duplicate-charge prevention over flaky networks.' My strength: idempotency and reconciliation design. Evidence: built the outbox-plus-idempotency mechanism that guaranteed no double-charges during a migration. (3) Problem from the product/careers page: 'engineers own on-call.' My strength: incident diagnosis under pressure. Evidence: traced and mitigated a checkout outage in ~10 minutes, now the go-to on the incident bridge.

    Weakest mapping: number three is a bit thin as a single anecdote, so I'd shore it up with a second on-call example before the interview. Rehearsed the three to about 50 seconds total so it's a crisp close, not a ramble.
