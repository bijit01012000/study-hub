    Tell me about yourself

    7 sections · 2,822 words

    Module 01 · 6 questions · 1 practical drill

    Win the opener with a deliberate pitch that lands on this role, not a life story.

    This is the opener, and most candidates waste it - either a chronological life story or a nervous ramble. The interviewer is really asking "why should I keep listening, and why you for this role". The follow-ups probe whether your narrative is a deliberate pitch or an accident, and whether the "why leaving / why us" holds together. A crisp 60-90 second present→past→future pitch that lands on the role sets the tone for the whole loop.

    Q1.1 Tell me about yourself.

    What it really tests. Whether you can deliver a tight, deliberate narrative that positions you for THIS role, rather than a chronological data dump.

    Weak answer

    "So, I was born and did my degree in computer science, then I joined a startup, then I moved to my current company where I've been for three years doing backend work. I've used a lot of technologies - Java, Python, some React. I enjoy coding and solving problems, and I'm now looking for a new opportunity."

    Why it fails

    • Chronological life story, no throughline or theme.
    • "A lot of technologies" listed with no depth or relevance to the role.
    • Nothing tailored to the company or the job applied for.
    • "I enjoy solving problems" is a cliché that says nothing.
    • Ends flat with no forward-looking hook.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Reflection - "You've done a lot - what's the through-line that connects it?" - Whether the narrative is deliberate or accidental.
    2. Ownership - "Why is that background a fit for THIS role specifically?" - Tailoring vs a canned pitch.
    3. Technical - "Of everything you listed, what are you actually best at?" - Self-knowledge and a real specialism.
    4. Measurement - "What's the one project you'd want me to remember from that?" - A concrete anchor with impact.

    Answer framework

    1. Present (who you are now, one line).
    2. What you own / are known for.
    3. One concrete proof point with a result.
    4. Relevant past that built to it.
    5. Why now / why this role.
    6. Hand back to the interviewer.

    Rejection signals

    • Pure chronology with no theme.
    • Runs well past two minutes with no landing.
    • Nothing connects to the role or company.
    • No single concrete achievement mentioned.

    Model answer skeleton

    I'm a backend-leaning full-stack engineer with about five years' experience, and the thread through my work is taking systems that fall over under load and making them boringly reliable.

    Right now I own the payments-integration service at my company - most recently I led the move from synchronous charge processing to an event-driven flow with idempotent workers, which took us through a Black-Friday peak with zero dropped orders where the year before we'd failed about 2% of them. Before that I was at an earlier-stage startup where I wore every hat, which is where I learned to ship pragmatically and own things end to end. I'm looking now because I want to work on systems at a bigger scale than my current company will reach, and your payments platform is exactly that problem one order of magnitude up - which is why I applied. Happy to go deeper on any of that; where would you like to start?

    Q1.2 Why are you looking to leave your current role?

    What it really tests. Whether your reason is a considered pull toward something, not just a bitter push away - and whether you badmouth your employer.

    Weak answer

    "Honestly my current company is a bit of a mess. Management keeps changing priorities, the codebase is a nightmare, and I'm underpaid for what I do. I just need to get out and find somewhere better."

    Why it fails

    • Entirely push, no pull - nothing you're moving toward.
    • Badmouths the employer, which reads as how you'll talk about them later.
    • "Underpaid" as the headline reason signals money-only motivation.
    • No self-reflection on your own part in the situation.
    • Gives the interviewer nothing about what you actually want.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Reflection - "What would need to change for you to stay?" - Whether the problem is fixable or a genuine ceiling.
    2. Ownership - "What are you moving TOWARD, not just away from?" - A pull motivation, not just grievance.
    3. Technical - "Have you tried to change any of that from the inside?" - Agency vs passive complaint.
    4. Measurement - "What did you actually get out of your time there?" - Ability to stay fair about a job you're leaving.

    Answer framework

    1. One fair sentence on the current role.
    2. What you've gained / are grateful for.
    3. The specific ceiling you've hit.
    4. What you want next that they can't offer.
    5. Why this role provides exactly that.
    6. No badmouthing.

    Rejection signals

    • Only complaints, no forward pull.
    • Badmouths people or the company.
    • Money is the sole stated reason.
    • No acknowledgement of anything learned there.

    Model answer skeleton

    I've had a good run at my current company - it's where I grew from mid to senior and where I learned to own a service end to end, and I'd genuinely recommend it. The honest reason I'm looking is a scale ceiling: we serve a regional market, our traffic has plateaued, and the hard distributed-systems problems I want to work on just don't come up any more because the load doesn't demand them. I tried to create that challenge internally - I pushed for us to consolidate a couple of services and take on more traffic - but the business direction is deliberately steady-state, which is fine, it's just not where I want to spend the next few years. I want to work somewhere the scale forces those problems, and that's specifically why your platform interests me. It's a pull, not an escape.

    Q1.3 Walk me through the project on your CV you're proudest of.

    What it really tests. Whether you can pick a genuinely substantial project and narrate YOUR role and its impact, versus giving a feature tour.

    Weak answer

    "I'm proud of a dashboard we built for internal analytics. It was a big project with a lot of moving parts and the whole team worked really hard on it. It came out well and people use it every day now. It was a good learning experience."

    Why it fails

    • "We built" throughout - no statement of your specific part.
    • "A lot of moving parts" is filler, not substance.
    • "People use it" with no number or business outcome.
    • "Good learning experience" is the tell of nothing memorable.
    • No hard problem named, no decision described.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Which part of that did you personally design and build?" - Separating your contribution from the team's.
    2. Technical - "What was the single hardest problem in it, and how did you crack it?" - Real depth vs surface tour.
    3. Measurement - "What number tells me it mattered?" - Quantified impact.
    4. Reflection - "What would you build differently now?" - Ongoing critical judgement.

    Answer framework

    1. The project and who it served.
    2. Why it mattered to the business.
    3. Your specific scope.
    4. The hardest problem and how you solved it.
    5. The measured result.
    6. What made it yours.
    7. One honest reflection.

    Rejection signals

    • Cannot isolate their own contribution.
    • No hard technical problem surfaces.
    • No metric, only "people liked it".
    • Picks a small project because a big one would expose gaps.

    Model answer skeleton

    It's the analytics dashboard our support team used to triage escalations - I'm proud of it because it changed how a whole team worked, not just because it shipped. My specific scope was the data pipeline and the query layer behind it; a colleague owned the front end.

    The hardest problem was that support needed near-real-time numbers but our warehouse was batch-updated hourly, so the data was always stale when it mattered most. I solved it by putting a thin streaming layer in front for the last hour of events and reconciling against the warehouse on read, which gave them fresh figures without rebuilding the warehouse. The result was that median triage time on escalations dropped from around 25 minutes to under 8, because they stopped waiting on stale reports.

    It was mine in that I designed the reconciliation and wrote the query layer. If I did it again I'd have pushed for proper streaming end to end rather than the hybrid, which got fragile as usage grew.

    Q1.4 What are you looking for in your next role?

    What it really tests. Whether you have a clear, honest picture of what you want - and whether it actually matches what this role offers.

    Weak answer

    "I'm looking for a role with good work-life balance, a nice team, interesting problems, growth opportunities, and good compensation. Basically somewhere I can keep learning and progressing in my career."

    Why it fails

    • A generic wish-list that fits any job and commits to nothing.
    • "Interesting problems" and "growth" are undefined.
    • No prioritisation - everything is equally important, so nothing is.
    • Doesn't connect to what this specific role actually offers.
    • Reveals no genuine self-knowledge.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "If you had to pick the ONE thing that matters most, what is it?" - Real priorities vs a wish-list.
    2. Technical - "What does 'interesting problems' mean concretely for you?" - Specificity about the actual work.
    3. Reflection - "What would make you leave a role within a year?" - Self-awareness about dealbreakers.
    4. Tradeoff - "How does this role match that - and where might it not?" - Honest fit assessment, not flattery.

    Answer framework

    1. Your top one or two priorities, ranked.
    2. What each means concretely.
    3. How this role maps to them.
    4. An honest note on any gap.
    5. Why the trade-off still works for you.
    6. What you'd bring in return.

    Rejection signals

    • Undifferentiated wish-list.
    • Can't rank what matters.
    • No connection to the actual role.
    • Priorities that clearly clash with the job (e.g. "no on-call" for an SRE role).

    Model answer skeleton

    If I'm honest and rank it, the top thing is technical scope - I want to own a meaty system end to end and go deep on reliability and scale, rather than being handed narrow tickets. Second is working alongside people I'll learn from, specifically engineers stronger than me in areas I'm weak, like distributed data. Concretely, 'interesting problems' for me means things like consistency under partition and load-shedding - the stuff that only shows up at real scale.

    This role maps well on the first because you've said the payments service is mine to own; where I'd want to be honest is that I'm lighter on the data-infrastructure side, so I'd be leaning on the team there early. That trade-off works for me because that's exactly the gap I want to close. In return I bring the reliability and incident-response experience the JD is asking for.

    Q1.5 I see a gap / a short stint / a pivot on your CV - talk me through it.

    What it really tests. Whether you can address a potential red flag directly and honestly without defensiveness or an over-rehearsed cover story.

    Weak answer

    "Oh, that. That role just wasn't a good fit and things didn't work out, so I moved on pretty quickly. I'd rather not dwell on it - it wasn't really my fault, the company was disorganised. But I'm in a much better place now."

    Why it fails

    • Defensive and evasive - "I'd rather not dwell on it" invites suspicion.
    • "Not my fault" with no reflection reads as blameshifting.
    • No honest account of what actually happened.
    • Doesn't say what, if anything, was learned.
    • Treats a normal question as an accusation.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Looking back, what was your part in it not working?" - Accountability vs pure blame.
    2. Reflection - "What did you actually learn from that period?" - Whether a setback produced insight.
    3. Technical - "What would you do differently before joining somewhere next time?" - Better judgement about fit.
    4. Tradeoff - "How do I know the same thing won't happen here?" - Whether the pattern is understood and closed.

    Answer framework

    1. State the fact plainly, no flinch.
    2. What genuinely happened.
    3. Your honest part in it.
    4. What you did during a gap / took from a short stint.
    5. What you learned that changed your approach.
    6. Why it won't repeat here.

    Rejection signals

    • Defensive or evasive about a plain question.
    • Blames entirely externally.
    • Over-rehearsed cover story that unravels on the second question.
    • No self-reflection at all.

    Model answer skeleton

    Happy to - I left that role after seven months, which I know is short. What happened is I joined for a platform-engineering brief that, once I was in, turned out to be mostly maintaining a legacy monolith with no roadmap to change it. My honest part in it: I didn't dig hard enough in the interviews - I took 'we're modernising' at face value and never asked to see the actual backlog.

    Rather than coast, I left cleanly once it was clear, and I used the six weeks between roles to finally learn Kafka properly, which I'd been putting off. What I changed is that I now ask pointed questions before accepting - 'show me the last quarter's tickets for this team' - which is partly why I'm asking you detailed questions today. It won't repeat here because I've done that diligence and the work you've described is the real thing, not a slide.

    Q1.6 Practical drill - Tell me about yourself

    Write and time your 90-second "tell me about yourself" pitch using present → past → future, then record yourself and cut it to under 90 seconds.

    What it really tests. Forces you to build a deliberate, timed opener instead of improvising a ramble under pressure - the single highest-leverage thing to rehearse before any loop.

    Weak answer

    N/A - drill. A weak attempt is a chronological CV read-out, over two minutes, with no throughline and nothing tailored to the role.

    Why it fails

    • Chronological "born, studied, joined, joined" structure instead of present→past→future.
    • Runs long - over 90 seconds - and buries the point.
    • One generic pitch reused for every company, tailored to none.
    • No concrete proof point with a number.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Measurement - "What's the single sentence someone remembers after you finish?" - A sharp, memorable throughline.
    2. Ownership - "Which sentence is tailored to THIS company, and could you swap it per application?" - A deliberate, adaptable tailoring slot.
    3. Reflection - "Where does it run long - what can you cut without losing the point?" - Ruthless editing to time.

    Answer framework

    1. Draft one present sentence (who you are, what you own).
    2. One past arc (how you got here, one line).
    3. One proof point with a number.
    4. One future sentence tying to THIS role.
    5. Read aloud and time it.
    6. Cut to under 90 seconds.
    7. Mark the one sentence you'll swap per company.

    Rejection signals

    • Still chronological, not present→past→future.
    • No number in the proof point.
    • Same pitch for every company, none tailored.
    • Over 90 seconds and unrehearsed.

    Model answer skeleton

    Drill output: Present - 'I'm a backend engineer, five years in, and my thing is making shaky systems boringly reliable.' Past - 'I cut my teeth at an early-stage startup owning everything, then went deep on payments reliability at my current company.' Proof - 'Most recently I moved us from synchronous to event-driven charge processing and we took a Black-Friday peak with zero dropped orders, versus ~2% failures the year before.' Future (the swappable line) - 'I want that same problem an order of magnitude bigger, which is exactly what your payments platform is, so I applied.' I timed it at 82 seconds reading aloud, cut a rambling clause about my degree, and marked the future sentence as the one I rewrite per company.

    My weakest bit was the past arc running long, so I tightened it to one clause.
