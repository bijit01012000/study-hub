    Failure

    7 sections · 2,865 words

    Module 03 · 6 questions · 1 practical drill

    Own a real failure, name its cost, and prove the lesson changed what you did next.

    The failure question is where candidates either lie ("my weakness is I work too hard") or pick a fake failure that's secretly a win. Interviewers can smell both. What they want is a real failure YOU caused or contributed to, honest ownership of your part, a real cost, and a lesson that visibly changed a later action. The follow-ups drill on "what did YOU get wrong" and "did anything actually change" - deflection to circumstances is the fast route to a no.

    Inside this module

    • Owned 'I' causation
    • The real cost named
    • The lesson that stuck

    Q3.1 Tell me about a time you failed.

    What it really tests. Whether you can name a genuine failure, own your specific part in it, and show a concrete lesson without deflecting or picking a fake failure.

    Weak answer

    "I'd say my biggest failure is that I sometimes take on too much because I care so much about the work. Once I was so committed to a project that I burned myself out a bit. So I guess my failure is caring too much."

    Why it fails

    • Classic humble-brag - "failure" is really a virtue in disguise.
    • No real cost to anyone but a vague personal burnout.
    • No specific decision the candidate got wrong.
    • No lesson that changed a technical or professional behaviour.
    • Dodges the question's actual intent.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "That's a strength dressed as a flaw - what's a real failure with a cost?" - Willingness to name a genuine failure.
    2. Technical - "What did YOU specifically decide or miss that caused it?" - Personal causation, not circumstance.
    3. Measurement - "What did it actually cost - time, money, users, trust?" - A real, quantified consequence.
    4. Reflection - "What have you done differently since because of it?" - A lesson that changed behaviour.

    Answer framework

    1. The situation.
    2. What you were responsible for.
    3. What you specifically got wrong.
    4. How it surfaced.
    5. The concrete cost.
    6. What you'd do differently.
    7. How you've applied that lesson since.

    Rejection signals

    • Humble-brag failure ("I care too much").
    • Blames circumstances entirely.
    • No measurable cost.
    • No evidence the lesson changed anything.

    Model answer skeleton

    We shipped a reporting feature that nobody used and retired within a quarter - a genuine failure, and my part in it was concrete. I jumped straight into building a flexible, over-engineered pipeline without validating the actual query users needed, so I built for a broad use case that turned out narrow and different.

    I half-saw it early - we hadn't spoken to a single end user - and I raised it once, got 'we'll figure it out', and let it drop instead of insisting. The cost was about six engineer-weeks on something we deleted. What I'd do differently is spend two days building a throwaway prototype in front of a real user before writing any production code. I've applied that religiously since: I now refuse to build anything sizeable without a concrete example of the real usage in hand, and it's already saved me from a similar mistake on a later project where the 'obvious' feature turned out not to be what the user meant.

    Q3.2 Tell me about a project that failed. What was your part in it?

    What it really tests. Ownership of a collective failure - whether you take your slice of responsibility rather than hiding behind "the requirements changed".

    Weak answer

    "We had a project that didn't succeed, mostly because the requirements kept changing and leadership kept shifting priorities. We didn't get enough time either. It wasn't really anyone's fault on the team - the circumstances were against us."

    Why it fails

    • Blames external factors entirely - requirements, leadership, time.
    • "Not anyone's fault" avoids all personal ownership.
    • No specific thing the candidate would have done differently.
    • No lesson that changed their own behaviour.
    • Passive victim framing.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Set the circumstances aside - what did YOU get wrong?" - Personal accountability within a team failure.
    2. Technical - "Did you see it going wrong early, and did you raise it loudly enough?" - Proactivity vs passive drift.
    3. Measurement - "How did it actually impact users or the business?" - Honest consequences.
    4. Reflection - "What have you changed since so you'd catch it earlier?" - An applied lesson.

    Answer framework

    1. The project and goal.
    2. Why it failed.
    3. Your specific contribution to the failure.
    4. What you saw and whether you spoke up.
    5. The impact.
    6. What you'd do differently.
    7. How you've applied it since.

    Rejection signals

    • Blames only external factors.
    • Takes no personal ownership.
    • No concrete lesson.
    • Won't admit real impact.

    Model answer skeleton

    We spent a quarter building a customer-facing integrations marketplace that launched with almost no adoption and got quietly shelved. Yes, priorities shifted - but my part was specific: I owned the technical design and I optimised for a plugin architecture that was elegant and extensible when the real need was to ship three concrete integrations fast and see if anyone wanted them.

    I over-built the general case. I sensed we were gold-plating and I mentioned it in a planning meeting, but I framed it as a mild concern rather than putting a hard number on the wasted effort, so it didn't land. The impact was roughly a quarter of a small team's time and a missed window while a competitor shipped the simple version.

    Since then I've changed how I raise these - I bring the cost in concrete terms ('this abstraction is two weeks we don't have') rather than a soft 'I wonder if', because a quantified concern gets heard and a vague one doesn't.

    Q3.3 Tell me about a bug or an outage you caused in production.

    What it really tests. Whether you own a mistake with real blast radius, how you responded under pressure, and whether you fixed the class of problem, not just the instance.

    Weak answer

    "There was an outage once but it wasn't really down to me specifically - the deploy process was fragile and there weren't enough safeguards in place. I pushed a change and something broke, but honestly the system should have caught it before it went out."

    Why it fails

    • Immediately deflects to "the process", not their change.
    • "Not really down to me" despite having pushed the change.
    • No account of how they responded during the incident.
    • Blames the safeguards rather than owning the mistake AND improving the safeguards.
    • No lesson beyond "the system should have caught it".

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "You pushed the change - what exactly did it do wrong?" - Owning the actual mistake.
    2. Ownership - "What did you do in the first ten minutes once it broke?" - Incident response under pressure.
    3. Measurement - "How many users or how much revenue did it affect?" - Honest about blast radius.
    4. Reflection - "What did you change so this class of bug can't recur?" - Systemic fix, not just a patch.

    Answer framework

    1. The change and why you made it.
    2. What went wrong.
    3. How you detected it.
    4. What you did first (mitigate).
    5. Root cause.
    6. The blast radius.
    7. The systemic fix (yours to own).
    8. What you learned.

    Rejection signals

    • Blames the process instead of owning the change.
    • No mitigation story - just "someone rolled it back".
    • Downplays or won't quantify the impact.
    • Fixed only the instance, added no guardrail.

    Model answer skeleton

    I once took down checkout for about 20 minutes. I shipped a migration that added a NOT NULL column with a default to a large table, and I didn't realise on our Postgres version it would rewrite the whole table and hold a lock - checkout writes queued up and timed out.

    I caught it fast because I watched the deploy and saw the error rate spike within a minute. My first move was to mitigate, not diagnose: I rolled the migration back immediately, which released the lock and recovered checkout, and only then dug into why. Blast radius was roughly 20 minutes of failed checkouts, a real revenue hit I didn't try to minimise in the postmortem.

    The systemic fix I drove was adding a check to our migration tooling that flags table-rewriting operations and forces them into a safe expand/contract pattern, so no one - including future me - can do this by accident. What I learned is to treat schema changes on big tables as inherently dangerous and always ask 'does this rewrite the table' before shipping.

    Q3.4 Tell me about a decision you made that turned out to be wrong.

    What it really tests. Whether you can evaluate your own past judgement honestly with hindsight, rather than defending it or picking a trivial "mistake".

    Weak answer

    "I think most of my decisions have been pretty solid, to be honest. If I had to pick, maybe I once chose a library that we later swapped out, but that was more the library's fault for being deprecated. Overall I'd stand by my calls."

    Why it fails

    • Reluctant to name a genuinely wrong decision.
    • Blames the library, not the decision to choose it.
    • "I'd stand by my calls" signals no capacity for hindsight.
    • The example is trivial and externalised.
    • No cost, no reflection.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Reflection - "What information did you have then, and what do you know now?" - Whether the decision was reasonable at the time.
    2. Measurement - "What did being wrong actually cost?" - A real consequence.
    3. Technical - "Did you correct it, or is it still running?" - Follow-through on the correction.
    4. Ownership - "Who else did that decision affect?" - Awareness of blast radius.

    Answer framework

    1. The decision.
    2. Why it seemed right then.
    3. What surfaced the problem.
    4. The cost incurred.
    5. What you'd choose now.
    6. Whether you actually corrected it.
    7. The generalised lesson.

    Rejection signals

    • Can't name a real wrong decision.
    • Blames the outcome on something external.
    • The "mistake" is trivial (naming, formatting).
    • No evidence they measured or corrected it.

    Model answer skeleton

    I chose to store our workflow state as a single JSON blob in one Postgres column because it was fast to ship and the shape was still changing. The information I lacked was that the access pattern would flip from write-heavy to query-heavy once ops started using it.

    Within six months we needed 'all workflows stuck at step 3', and that meant full scans over the blob - a report that should be instant took 40 seconds, and ops felt it daily. I'd now model the state transitions as proper rows from the start, or at least promote the queried fields into indexed columns. I did correct it: we migrated the hot fields into a projection table and got that report back under 200ms, though the migration cost us a fiddly week we wouldn't have spent if I'd modelled it right initially.

    The generalised lesson is that 'the schema is still changing' justifies flexibility on cold data, never on anything you'll filter or sort by.

    Q3.5 Tell me about a time you missed a deadline or didn't deliver what you promised.

    What it really tests. Whether you own a commitment you broke, how you handled it with the people depending on you, and whether you fixed how you estimate or communicate.

    Weak answer

    "I missed a deadline once but it was because the scope kept growing and other teams were slow to give me what I needed. I delivered as fast as I could given the blockers. In the end it was only a bit late and no one was too bothered."

    Why it fails

    • Blames scope creep and other teams for the miss.
    • "As fast as I could given the blockers" claims zero responsibility.
    • "No one was too bothered" minimises the impact of a broken commitment.
    • No account of how or when they warned stakeholders.
    • No change to estimation or communication.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "When did you first know you'd miss, and when did you tell people?" - Early, honest communication vs last-minute surprise.
    2. Technical - "What did you personally underestimate?" - Owning the estimation error.
    3. Measurement - "Who was left exposed by the slip?" - Awareness of downstream impact.
    4. Reflection - "How do you estimate or flag risk differently now?" - A concrete process change.

    Answer framework

    1. The commitment.
    2. What went wrong.
    3. What you specifically misjudged.
    4. When you knew and how you communicated it.
    5. The impact on others.
    6. How you recovered.
    7. What changed in how you estimate or flag risk.

    Rejection signals

    • Blames the miss entirely on scope or other teams.
    • Sprang the slip on stakeholders at the last minute.
    • Minimises the impact.
    • No change to estimation or communication practice.

    Model answer skeleton

    I committed to a two-week turnaround on an API for a partner integration and it took nearly four. What I underestimated was the auth handshake with their legacy system - I'd assumed standard OAuth and it turned out to be a bespoke flow that ate a week alone.

    My mistake wasn't only the estimate; it was that I discovered the auth problem in week one and didn't escalate it until week two, hoping I'd claw the time back. The impact was that the partner-facing team had promised a date to the partner based on my estimate and had to walk it back, which was embarrassing for them. Once I owned it, I gave a revised date with a buffer and hit it.

    What changed is two things: I now spike the riskiest unknown first before committing to a date, and the moment an estimate is at risk I flag it the same day rather than hoping - a slip is survivable, a surprise slip damages trust.

    Q3.6 Practical drill - Failure

    Write a failure story where the lesson visibly changed a LATER action, then stress-test it by answering "what did YOU specifically get wrong" without using the word "we".

    What it really tests. Forces you to prepare a real failure with personal ownership and a demonstrable behaviour change, and to rehearse stripping "we" out of the accountability.

    Weak answer

    N/A - drill. A weak attempt is a disguised humble-brag, blames circumstances, and has a lesson ('communicate better') that never shows up in a later action.

    Why it fails

    • The "failure" is secretly a strength.
    • Ownership is in "we", diffusing the blame.
    • The lesson is generic and never demonstrated later.
    • No measurable cost.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "Say the whole 'what I got wrong' part using only 'I' - can you?" - Personal accountability without hiding in 'we'.
    2. Measurement - "Point to the specific later moment the lesson changed what you did." - A demonstrated, not claimed, behaviour change.
    3. Reflection - "Is this a real failure or a win in disguise - be honest?" - Self-audit against the humble-brag trap.

    Answer framework

    1. Pick a real failure with a cost.
    2. Write your part using only "I" for the mistake.
    3. State the concrete cost with a number.
    4. Name the lesson as a rule, not a platitude.
    5. Write a SECOND, later situation where you applied the rule.
    6. Show the different outcome.
    7. Rehearse the "what did you get wrong" answer with zero "we".

    Rejection signals

    • Can't tell the failure without "we".
    • The lesson never reappears in a later action.
    • The failure is a humble-brag.
    • No cost stated.

    Model answer skeleton

    Drill output: Failure - 'I built an over-engineered reporting pipeline without validating the real user query; it went unused and I wasted about six engineer-weeks before we retired it.' Said in 'I': 'I chose to build the general case, I skipped talking to a user, and when I half-sensed the gap I raised it too softly to matter.'

    The rule: 'never build anything sizeable without a concrete example of the real usage in hand.' The later action: 'On a subsequent alerting project I spent two days prototyping the actual alert in front of the on-call engineer first, discovered the "obvious" threshold was wrong, and shipped the right thing in one iteration instead of three.' That's the demonstrated change.

    Honest audit: it's a real failure - something got deleted and time was lost - not a win in disguise. Rehearsed the 'what did you get wrong' answer with no 'we' in it.
