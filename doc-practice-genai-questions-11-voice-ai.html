      Module 11 · 13 questions · 1 practical drill

      Build for a conversation that breaks if you are slow.

      Q11.1 — Draw a voice agent end to end. What sits between the caller's voice and the reply they hear?

      Weak answer: "Audio comes in, we transcribe it, send the text to the LLM, get a reply, and convert that to speech with a TTS model, then play it back."

      Why it fails: - Treats the chain as sequential, missing orchestration: cancelling stale work, tracking turn state, coordinating each stage. - No mention of transport, the phone or WebRTC leg, as its own component with its own latency and failure modes.

      Instant rejection: - Can't name a transport layer at all, as if audio just appears as text. - No idea that STT, LLM and TTS run concurrently in production, not one after another.

      Model answer: 1. The call comes in over a transport leg, usually WebRTC or a telephony trunk over WebSocket, which is the only part actually carrying raw audio in both directions. 2. Streaming STT turns that audio into partial and final transcripts as the caller speaks, not one transcript after they finish, so downstream stages can start early. 3. The LLM gets the growing transcript plus conversation state and starts generating from partial input where the design allows it, rather than waiting for a complete sentence. 4. TTS turns the reply into audio incrementally, streaming sentence by sentence back over the same transport leg instead of waiting for the whole reply to finish. 5. Orchestration sits above all of it, tracking whose turn it is, cancelling stale generation and playback the instant the caller interrupts, and holding state across turns.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Which stage owns cancelling a TTS playback mid-sentence when the caller interrupts? · Whether orchestration is understood as an active coordinator, not just glue code between three models. 2. FAILURE — The STT stage goes down mid-call. What does the caller actually experience? · Whether failure in one stage has a defined, non-silent behaviour for the person on the line. 3. TRADEOFF — Would you run STT, LLM and TTS as one service or three? What do you give up either way? · Reasoning about deployment coupling versus per-stage scaling and independent failure isolation. 4. MEASUREMENT — How would you find out which stage is adding the most delay to a slow call? · Whether per-stage timing exists, or the whole pipeline is one unmeasured black box.

      Tip: If you've built any streaming pipeline, log processing, video transcoding, WebSocket chat, describe it the same way: stages passing partial output, not batch steps. Don't claim you've tuned a specific STT or TTS vendor if you haven't. Say you understand the architecture and would benchmark vendors against the latency budget.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 5 pipeline stages, one call - ~1s to first audio, or it breaks - STT, LLM, TTS run concurrently - Orchestration owns turn state

      Q11.2 — What's your latency budget for a natural-feeling voice conversation, and where does each part of it go?

      Weak answer: "We just try to make it as fast as we can everywhere. Faster is always better, so we optimise every stage as much as the budget allows."

      Why it fails: - "As fast as possible" isn't a budget. There's no target number to design the pipeline against or trade off between stages. - Optimising every stage equally ignores that some stages, endpointing, network, cost fixed time no matter what you do.

      Instant rejection: - No awareness that perceived wait starts degrading around 600 to 800 milliseconds, not multiple seconds. - Cannot name a single stage-level latency figure, even an illustrative one from their own work.

      Model answer: 1. I work to a rough budget of under a second to first audio, because callers start perceiving the exchange as broken somewhere between 700 and 800 milliseconds of silence. 2. Endpointing alone, confirming the caller actually finished rather than just paused, costs me 150 to 400 milliseconds before the LLM turn even starts. 3. STT streaming gives me a transcript with almost no added delay once endpointing fires, so most of my remaining budget sits with the LLM's time to first token. 4. TTS is not free either: model time is small, but network delivery and a player buffer on the caller's side both eat into the same budget before sound reaches their ear. 5. When I'm over budget I look at which stage ate the overrun first, usually endpointing being too cautious or a tool call sitting inside the turn, rather than shaving everything evenly.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Which stage in your pipeline is the hardest floor you can't optimise below, and why? · Recognising fixed costs like endpointing confirmation time versus genuinely optimisable stages. 2. MEASUREMENT — How do you measure time to first audio specifically, versus total response time? · Separating the metric that governs perceived responsiveness from total generation time. 3. TRADEOFF — Would you rather shave 100ms off STT or 100ms off TTS network delivery? Does it matter which? · Whether they reason about where in the budget a saving is most felt, not treat milliseconds as fungible. 4. FAILURE — A retrieval call inside the LLM turn adds 400ms. What breaks first? · Connecting an upstream slow step to a blown budget and a caller who thinks the line dropped.

      Tip: Talk about a latency budget from any realtime system you've built: trading, gaming, video calls. Deciding a number then tracing where it goes is the same logic. Don't invent a stage-by-stage millisecond breakdown for a voice pipeline you've never built. Say you'd measure per-stage timing before trusting any number.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 600-800ms, wait feels broken - Endpointing: 150-400ms - ~1s budget to first audio - TTS: model + network + buffer

      Q11.3 — Why do streaming pipelines beat sequential ones for voice, and what actually breaks in a sequential design?

      Weak answer: "Streaming is just faster because you're not waiting around. Sequential would still work, it would just feel a bit slower to the caller."

      Why it fails: - Undersells it badly. Waiting for a full transcript, then a full reply, then full audio, stacks real delay onto every stage. - Doesn't explain the mechanism: each stage waits for the previous stage's full output, stacking every stage's latency.

      Instant rejection: - Thinks sequential is a valid, just-slower alternative rather than something that fails production voice entirely. - Can't explain that streaming passes partial output downstream while the previous stage is still working.

      Model answer: 1. Sequential means STT finishes the whole transcript, then the LLM generates the whole reply, then TTS renders the whole clip, and each stage's full duration stacks on the last. 2. Streaming overlaps stages instead: the LLM can start generating from a stable partial transcript, and TTS can start speaking the first sentence while the rest is still being written. 3. The failure of sequential isn't just "slower", it's that total delay becomes the sum of every stage's full-output time, which reliably lands past the point a caller assumes the call dropped. 4. I still keep the cascaded shape, STT then LLM then TTS as separate services, because it lets me swap a vendor or debug one stage without touching the others. 5. When I've inherited a sequential design, the fix isn't "make each stage faster", it's restructuring so stages consume and emit partial output at all.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What partial output does the LLM stage consume from STT before the caller has finished speaking? · Grasp of streaming transcripts feeding generation before endpointing fires, where design allows it. 2. TRADEOFF — Cascaded STT-LLM-TTS still has stage boundaries. What do you give up versus a fused model? · Awareness that cascaded pipelines trade some latency for per-stage fault isolation and vendor flexibility. 3. MEASUREMENT — How would you prove, with numbers, that streaming beats sequential on this same call? · Whether the claim is backed by a measured time-to-first-audio comparison, not just intuition. 4. FAILURE — One stage can't produce partial output yet. What happens to the rest of the pipeline? · Whether they've considered a non-streaming stage becoming the bottleneck that reintroduces sequential waiting.

      Tip: If you've streamed anything, server-sent events, chunked HTTP responses, video segments, explain that same overlap: consumer starts before producer finishes. Don't claim you've built a streaming voice pipeline specifically. Say you understand why sequential fails here and would design for partial output from the start.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Sequential = sum of stage times - Streaming = stages overlap - Partial transcript feeds LLM - TTS speaks before LLM finishes

      Q11.4 — The transcript from STT is wrong. What does your system do downstream when the words are simply mistaken?

      Weak answer: "We use a good STT model so transcription errors are pretty rare. If something odd comes through, the LLM usually figures out what was meant."

      Why it fails: - Relies on the LLM silently guessing intent instead of explicit handling, producing confident wrong answers. - Not a design position. Error rate depends on accent, noise and vocabulary, and is never zero on a phone line.

      Instant rejection: - No mechanism to confirm an ambiguous or high-stakes field, an amount, an address, before acting on it. - No mention of domain vocabulary, custom terms or numbers as the specific place ASR reliably fails.

      Model answer: 1. I treat STT as a channel with a known, non-zero error rate, not a solved problem, so the design has to assume some words will come through wrong. 2. For low-stakes content, casual chat, a request to repeat, I let the LLM interpret the noisy transcript and move on, because a small misunderstanding there is cheap to recover from. 3. For high-stakes fields, an amount, an account number, a date, I have the agent read the value back and ask for explicit confirmation before acting on it. 4. I bias the STT model with domain vocabulary, product names, account terms, numbers in the expected format, because generic models mishear exactly these terms most often. 5. I track word error rate on sampled calls against a labelled set, because a rising error rate on one accent or one line quality shows up there long before it shows up as a complaint.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — How do you bias STT towards domain words, like product names it keeps mishearing? · Knowledge of custom vocabulary or phrase hints as a concrete mitigation, not just use a better model. 2. FAILURE — An order number gets one digit misheard. What stops the agent confirming the wrong order? · Whether high-stakes fields get an explicit confirmation step rather than being trusted as transcribed. 3. TRADEOFF — Confirming every field back to the caller is safer but slower. How do you decide what to confirm? · Reasoning about which fields are cheap to get wrong versus which justify the added turn and latency. 4. MEASUREMENT — How would you even find out your word error rate is a problem before a caller complains? · Whether transcript quality is monitored on an ongoing basis, or only discovered through complaints.

      Tip: If you've handled noisy or malformed input anywhere, OCR text, user typos, flaky sensor data, describe the same pattern: confirm high-stakes fields, tolerate the rest. Don't claim a specific word error rate figure you haven't measured. Say you'd sample calls against a labelled transcript set to find the real number first.

      Numbers worth quoting: - WER varies by accent, never 0% - Confirm high-stakes fields only - Domain vocabulary biasing helps - Sample calls to track WER drift

      Q11.5 — How does your agent know the caller has actually stopped speaking, not just paused?

      Weak answer: "We use voice activity detection, so as soon as the audio goes quiet the system treats that as the end of turn and starts generating a response."

      Why it fails: - Treats silence detection alone as sufficient, when a thinking pause sounds identical to a finished turn to a naive threshold. - Implies zero added delay, ignoring that confirming a pause is really the end of turn costs real time.

      Instant rejection: - No awareness of the two failure poles: cutting in too early over a mid-sentence pause, or waiting so long the line feels dead. - Can't explain why endpointing adds latency rather than being an instant, free signal.

      Model answer: 1. Endpointing is deciding, from an audio stream, that the caller's turn has actually ended, and it's a genuine judgement call, not just detecting silence. 2. A pure silence timer fails at both ends: too short and the agent talks over someone mid-thought, too long and the caller thinks the line went dead. 3. I budget roughly 150 to 400 milliseconds for endpointing to confirm a pause is really the end of turn, which is real added latency the rest of the pipeline has to account for. 4. For use cases with numbers or spelled-out details, an order number, an address, I lean the threshold longer, because cutting in mid-digit is worse there than a slightly longer pause. 5. I'd rather tune the threshold per use case than use one global value, since a quick yes or no confirmation and a caller reading out a long account number have very different natural pause lengths.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What signal, beyond silence, tells your system a pause is hesitation, not end of turn? · Awareness of semantic or prosodic cues beyond a fixed silence timer, even at a conceptual level. 2. FAILURE — A caller says 'my account number is, let me check, 4-4-5-2.' What does naive endpointing do here? · Recognising a concrete case where a fixed silence threshold cuts in during a genuine mid-thought pause. 3. TRADEOFF — Interrupt callers occasionally, or leave dead air occasionally? What drives that choice? · Reasoning about which failure mode is worse for the specific use case, not assuming one universal answer. 4. MEASUREMENT — How would you tune your silence threshold, and how would you know if it's set wrong? · Whether there's a measurable way to catch early cut-ins or late responses, not a fixed value.

      Tip: If you've built turn-taking in chat, typing indicators, or input-finished detection, explain the same early-versus-late trade-off. Don't claim you've tuned an endpointing threshold on real call data. Say you understand it's a measured trade-off, not a default, and would tune it against real calls.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Endpointing costs 150-400ms - Two failure poles: early or dead - Threshold varies by use case - Silence alone is not enough

      Q11.6 — The caller interrupts mid-sentence while your agent is talking. Walk me through what has to happen.

      Weak answer: "We detect that the caller started talking again and stop playing the current audio, then let them finish and process their input as normal."

      Why it fails: - Addresses only TTS output, not the LLM generation still running or a stale transcript in flight upstream. - Skips the hard part: reconciling what the agent actually said before being cut off, not what it intended.

      Instant rejection: - No mention of cancelling in-flight LLM generation, only stopping playback, leaving stale state. - No idea what the agent tells the caller next, or that TTS can report spoken versus unspoken text.

      Model answer: 1. Barge-in detection itself happens upstream, in STT and VAD picking up the caller's new speech while the agent's audio is still playing. 2. Once detected, every stage with stale work has to cancel: stop TTS playback immediately, cancel the LLM generation still producing text, and drop any transcript still being confirmed from before. 3. I make TTS report what was actually spoken against what was cut off, so conversation state reflects what the caller genuinely heard, not the full reply the agent intended to say. 4. That distinction matters because if the agent believes it already told the caller something it never finished saying, the next turn builds on a false premise. 5. For a small number of cases, reading a mandatory disclaimer, I deliberately reduce barge-in sensitivity, because letting that get cut off short has a real compliance cost.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — Barge-in is detected. Which specific things need to be cancelled, and in what order? · Whether cancellation covers STT confirmation, LLM generation and TTS playback, not just audio. 2. MEASUREMENT — How do you know what the caller actually heard, versus what the full reply would have said? · Knowledge that TTS can report spoken-versus-remaining text, which matters for honest conversation state. 3. FAILURE — Playback is cancelled but the LLM call keeps running in the background. What goes wrong? · Recognising uncancelled generation can still commit to history or trigger a side effect. 4. TRADEOFF — More or less barge-in sensitivity for an agent reading a legal disclaimer? · Reasoning about when allowing interruption is actually undesirable, not treating barge-in as always good.

      Tip: If you've cancelled in-flight requests, aborted a fetch, killed a running job when a user navigated away, describe that same full-stack cancellation logic. Don't claim you've implemented barge-in specifically. Say you understand it needs cancellation at every stage, not just muting audio, and name the stages.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Barge-in: cancel every stage - Detection happens in STT/VAD - TTS reports spoken vs remaining - Some content shouldn't be cut

      Q11.7 — You're picking a TTS provider for latency. Why isn't the model's own latency number the one that matters?

      Weak answer: "We compared the model latency numbers each vendor published and picked the fastest one. That should give us the fastest audio to the caller."

      Why it fails: - Only one part of the chain. Ignores network delivery time and the player buffer, both adding real delay. - A vendor's benchmark is measured under their conditions, not the caller's network path or the app's buffering.

      Instant rejection: - Cannot name the other components of end-to-end TTS delay beyond the model's own generation time. - No mention of testing the vendor under the actual deployment's network conditions rather than trusting a published number.

      Model answer: 1. The number vendors publish is model generation time alone, commonly 90 to 200 milliseconds, and that is the smallest piece of the delay a caller actually experiences. 2. On top of that sits network delivery, roughly 20 to 200 milliseconds depending on the caller's connection and how far the audio has to travel. 3. Then there's a player buffer on the receiving side, commonly around 500 milliseconds, that exists specifically to smooth out playback and avoid stutter. 4. So a vendor with the fastest model number can still lose to a slightly slower model that's better integrated, closer geographically, or paired with a leaner buffer. 5. I test candidates end to end, from text-ready to audio-audible on a call that matches our real deployment, not on the vendor's own published benchmark.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What eats more time, the model generating audio or the player buffer before playback? · Concrete grasp that a common player buffer, often around 500ms, can dwarf a sub-100ms model time. 2. MEASUREMENT — How would you measure end-to-end TTS latency for a real call, not just the model's own number? · Whether they'd measure text-ready to audio-audible on the caller's line, the number that matters. 3. TRADEOFF — Could you cut the player buffer to save latency? What do you risk if you do? · Awareness that the buffer exists to smooth playback, and shrinking it too far risks audible stutter or gaps. 4. FAILURE — Model latency tests great but callers still say the agent feels slow. What would you check? · Diagnostic instinct to look at network path and buffering, not just re-benchmark the model.

      Tip: If you've picked infrastructure by benchmarking the whole path, a CDN, a database, an API, rather than trusting a vendor number, describe that same instinct here. Don't quote a specific TTS vendor's latency figure as your own measured result if you haven't tested it. Say you'd test end to end before trusting any number.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Model time: roughly 90-200ms - Network: roughly 20-200ms - Player buffer: ~500ms common - Measure end-to-end, not the spec

      Q11.8 — How do you prompt an LLM differently when its output becomes speech instead of text on a screen?

      Weak answer: "We use basically the same prompt as our chat product, since the model's reasoning doesn't change just because the output goes through TTS."

      Why it fails: - TTS reads formatting aloud badly or skips it, so markdown and bullet points sound broken or lose structure. - The issue isn't reasoning, it's length and pacing. A chat-length answer read aloud is a wall of speech.

      Instant rejection: - No mention of instructing the model to avoid markdown, lists, or visual structure entirely, since none of it survives TTS. - No awareness that spoken answers need to be shorter and more conversational than an equivalent written answer.

      Model answer: 1. I explicitly tell the model it's speaking, not writing, and to never use markdown, bullet points, numbered lists or headers, because none of that survives being read aloud. 2. I cap response length hard in the prompt, aiming for one or two short sentences per turn, because a chat-length paragraph read aloud loses the caller before it finishes. 3. For anything with genuine steps or multiple items, I restructure the instruction to have the model offer one item at a time and check in, rather than list them all in one turn. 4. I ask for conversational phrasing explicitly, contractions, simple words, the way a person actually talks, because written-style prose sounds stilted read aloud. 5. I sample real call transcripts regularly to check the model isn't drifting back towards list-shaped or long-form answers under prompt pressure from edge cases.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What system prompt instructions stop the model producing markdown or bullet lists? · Concrete prompt-level mitigation, not a vague we tell it to keep things short. 2. FAILURE — The model returns a numbered list of three steps. What does the caller actually hear through TTS? · Whether they understand the concrete, ugly result of unformatted structure hitting a speech synthesiser. 3. TRADEOFF — A short spoken answer drops detail a written one would keep. How do you decide what to cut? · Reasoning about which information genuinely needs to survive a shorter spoken format. 4. MEASUREMENT — How would you check the voice prompt stays speech-appropriate at scale, not by hand? · Whether output length and formatting are monitored on real traffic, not just checked once by hand.

      Tip: If you've adapted output for a different channel, an SMS character limit, a notification, a voice assistant skill, describe adapting content to the medium's constraints. Don't claim you've tuned a voice-specific prompt in production. Say you understand spoken output needs short, list-free, conversational phrasing and would test for it.

      Numbers worth quoting: - 1-2 short sentences per turn - No markdown survives TTS - Offer one item, then check in - Sample transcripts for drift

      Q11.9 — A tool call takes three seconds mid-conversation. What does the caller experience while it runs?

      Weak answer: "The tool call just runs and once it's done, the agent replies with the result. It's the same as any backend call, so the user waits for it."

      Why it fails: - Inside a live call, waiting means dead air past the point a caller assumes the line dropped. - No mention of filling the gap, an acknowledgement or filler phrase, so silence is the whole experience.

      Instant rejection: - No recognition that retrieval latency inside a voice turn differs from the same latency off a call. - No plan for what the agent says or does while waiting, only what it says once the call finishes.

      Model answer: 1. I treat any tool call or retrieval as latency that lands directly inside the conversational budget, not as background work the caller is unaware of. 2. The agent says something immediately, a short acknowledgement like "let me check that", before the tool call starts, so the caller isn't met with sudden silence. 3. If the call is genuinely slow, I use a brief filler phrase or a light hold pattern rather than dead air, because silence past a second or so reads as a dropped line. 4. I set a hard timeout on the tool call itself and have a fallback spoken response ready, so a slow backend doesn't turn into indefinite silence. 5. I measure latency per tool call separately, because one specific lookup being consistently slow is a fixable, attributable problem, not just the agent sometimes goes quiet.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What does the agent say in the first half second after deciding a tool call is needed? · Whether there's a concrete filler or acknowledgement pattern, not just silence until the result arrives. 2. TRADEOFF — Wait through a slow lookup, or give a less complete answer immediately? What decides that? · Reasoning about when a fast partial answer beats a slow complete one for the specific use case. 3. FAILURE — The tool call fails after two seconds of silence. What does the caller hear next? · Whether failure inside a retrieval step has a defined spoken outcome, not a dead line or a generic error. 4. MEASUREMENT — How would you find out which of your tool calls are actually blowing the conversational budget? · Whether tool-call latency is measured and attributed per tool, or only discovered anecdotally.

      Tip: If you've handled a slow API call in a UI, a loading spinner, an optimistic update, a progress message, describe the same idea: never leave the user with nothing. Don't claim you've built voice filler phrases specifically. Say you understand silence during a slow call reads as a dropped line and would design around it.

      Numbers worth quoting: - Retrieval latency: inside budget - Ack first, before the lookup - Hard timeout + spoken fallback - Measure latency per tool

      Q11.10 — For a realtime voice agent, when would you choose WebRTC over a plain WebSocket, and when not?

      Weak answer: "We'd use WebRTC everywhere since it's built for real-time audio and video, so it's naturally the better choice for any voice product."

      Why it fails: - Skips the real trade-off: WebRTC brings NAT traversal, signalling and codec complexity WebSocket avoids. - Ignores that a phone call often arrives over a carrier trunk, making this protocol choice irrelevant there.

      Instant rejection: - Can't name a concrete cost of WebRTC, like NAT traversal or signalling, as a reason it isn't automatic. - No mention that the source of the call, a browser versus a telephony trunk, changes which protocol is even relevant.

      Model answer: 1. For a browser-based caller, I'd lean towards WebRTC because it handles jitter buffering, packet loss concealment and codec negotiation for real audio conditions out of the box. 2. That comes with real cost: a signalling server to set up the connection, and STUN and often TURN infrastructure for NAT traversal, which is genuine added complexity. 3. For a phone call over a telephony trunk, the browser protocol question doesn't apply to that leg at all, since the carrier already delivers audio over its own path. 4. If I only need a simple, controlled internal tool, a demo, a single-network deployment, plain WebSocket streaming raw audio chunks is genuinely simpler and often enough. 5. I'd choose based on where callers actually connect from and how much network variability I need to tolerate, not by defaulting to WebRTC as the realtime-audio brand name.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — What does WebRTC give audio that a raw WebSocket streaming PCM chunks doesn't? · Concrete knowledge of jitter buffering, packet loss concealment and codec negotiation built into WebRTC. 2. TRADEOFF — What do you give up in engineering complexity by choosing WebRTC over WebSocket? · Awareness of signalling servers, STUN and TURN, NAT traversal as real added complexity, not a free upgrade. 3. FAILURE — On a restrictive corporate network, what happens to WebRTC, and what's your fallback? · Recognising WebRTC can fail behind strict firewalls and needs a TURN relay or fallback path. 4. MEASUREMENT — How would you decide, with data, whether you need WebRTC's resilience or WebSocket is enough? · Whether the choice is backed by actual network condition and quality data, not a default assumption.

      Tip: If you've compared two protocols for a realtime feature, WebSocket versus polling, gRPC streaming versus REST, describe that same capability-versus-complexity trade-off. Don't claim hands-on WebRTC signalling experience you don't have. Say you understand what it buys and costs, and would validate the choice against real usage.

      Numbers worth quoting: - WebRTC: jitter/loss handling - Needs STUN/TURN + signalling - Telephony trunk: separate leg - Choose by network, not by brand

      Q11.11 — How would you handle accents, code switching and Indian language support in a voice agent?

      Weak answer: "We'd use a strong multilingual STT model, and since it already supports most Indian languages, accents and mixing languages should mostly work."

      Why it fails: - Not a design position. ASR error rates genuinely differ across accents and languages, and that gap needs measuring. - Code switching, English mixed mid-sentence with an Indian language, is harder than either alone, and it's skipped.

      Instant rejection: - No mention of measuring word error rate separately per accent or language rather than trusting one aggregate number. - No handling at all for a caller switching languages mid-sentence, treated as if it doesn't happen.

      Model answer: 1. I don't treat accent and language support as solved by picking a multilingual STT model. I'd measure word error rate separately per accent and per language on real sampled calls. 2. Code switching, a caller starting in English and dropping into Hindi or another language mid-sentence, is its own failure mode, and I'd test it explicitly rather than assume it's handled. 3. Where one accent or language shows a materially worse error rate, I'd bias vocabulary or consider a different model for that segment rather than accept a lower experience silently. 4. I'd prioritise language investment by actual caller volume and stakes, supporting the two or three languages that cover most calls well, rather than a shallow pass across every one. 5. For a caller the agent keeps mishearing, I'd have an escalation path to a human or a simpler confirm-by-repeating flow, rather than letting the agent guess repeatedly.

      Follow-ups: 1. TECHNICAL — How does your STT handle English switching to Hindi mid-sentence? · Whether code switching is a considered case with a real answer, not something assumed away. 2. MEASUREMENT — How would you find out your agent performs worse for one accent than another? · Whether word error rate and task success are broken down per accent or language, not measured in aggregate. 3. FAILURE — One word keeps getting misheard for a regional accent. What's your fix path? · A concrete fix: vocabulary biasing, model choice or escalation, not accepting the miss rate. 4. TRADEOFF — Supporting five Indian languages well costs more than English. How would you sequence that? · Reasoning about prioritising language support by call volume and stakes, not everything at once.

      Tip: If you've handled localisation anywhere, date formats, translated UI copy, measuring a feature's quality per locale, describe that same per-segment measurement instinct. Don't claim specific Indian-language ASR accuracy numbers you haven't measured. Say you'd break down error rate per accent and language before trusting one figure.

      Numbers worth quoting: - WER differs by accent/language - Code switching: separate failure - Prioritise by call volume - Escalate on repeated mishears

      Q11.12 — What needs to be in place for consent, call recording and transcript retention before go-live?

      Weak answer: "We record calls for quality purposes like most support lines do, and we keep the transcripts in our database in case there's ever an issue."

      Why it fails: - Assumes consent without an actual mechanism, an announcement or opt-out, telling the caller recording is happening. - No stated retention period or access control treats storage as indefinite and unrestricted by default.

      Instant rejection: - No mention of informing the caller recording is happening before or at the start of the call. - No retention period or deletion policy at all, implying transcripts and audio are kept forever by default.

      Model answer: 1. I put a clear, spoken consent notice at the start of the call, stating that it may be recorded, before any recording or transcript capture actually begins. 2. I build a real opt-out path, not just a notice, so a caller who declines gets a different flow rather than being recorded anyway with a disclaimer they never agreed to. 3. I set an explicit retention period for both audio and transcripts, driven by an actual business or legal need, rather than keeping everything indefinitely by default. 4.
