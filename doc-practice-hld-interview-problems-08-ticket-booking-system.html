    Design a Ticket Booking System

    3 sections · 3,395 words

    Three levels: Level 1 — The Shape, Level 2 — The Defence, Level 3 — The Edges.

    Level 1 — The Shape (Foundation)

    Pacing: 3 min ask · 5 min trace flow · 7 min read why.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Browse shows/events and seat maps — the BookMyShow problem
    • Select seat(s), place a temporary hold during payment

    Non-Functional

    • A seat must never sell twice ← forces an atomic claim
    • A hold must not block forever ← forces a TTL'd lease
    • A hot show bursts claims on 500 keys ← forces per-seat locking

    Agree These First

    500 000 bookings/day baseline · a hot show: 10 000 concurrent attempts on one 500-seat show in the first 60 s · a 5-min hold window. Round, illustrative numbers, not any named company's.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client(s) — Thousands, same hot show, same 60 seconds
    • (1) claim attempt — 166.7 /s, one hot show
    • API Gateway — Routes browse traffic apart from claim traffic
    • Booking Service — Checks availability · creates a hold on claim
    • Seat Store — Source of truth · per-seat status: available / held / booked
    • Payment Service — Out of scope — a boundary you don't own transactionally
    • (5) Rejected — 409 · fails fast, never queues behind the winner
    • (9) 200 OK · booking confirmed — never a second claim
    • (6) TTL 5 min

    Data model: seats seat_id PK → show_id, status.

    95% of attempts — by design.

    Line = claim vs traffic: CLAIM · Seat Store write; READ · view or confirm.

    Say It Like This

    "Unlike a typical read-heavy service, I'd choose consistency over availability here — a double-sold seat is a real failure, not a stale read. So every claim is a per-seat conditional write against the Seat Store, not one lock for the whole show — that lock would be held across a human's checkout. A hold gets a short TTL so an abandoned checkout doesn't block the seat forever. And anyone just looking at the seat map reads from a separate, cache-backed view — they never touch the strict path."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (3) Per-seat claim Conditional write per seat — 500 keys, each sees ~0.33/s One show-level lock → held across a 5-min checkout, it freezes all 500 seats
    (6) Hold + TTL Short lease, auto-expires — abandoned checkout frees the seat No hold → grabbed mid-checkout. Unbounded → blocks it forever
    (5) Fail fast Explicit 409, immediately — 95% can't win no matter what Queue/retry silently → 10 000 requests serialize, time out slowly

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Baseline 500 000 ÷ 86 400 s — platform-wide, unremarkable 5.8 /s
    Hot show 10 000 ÷ 60 s — one show, ~29× the platform's average 166.7 /s
    Contention 10 000 attempts ÷ 500 seats 20 : 1
    Guaranteed-fail (10 000 − 500) ÷ 10 000 — true regardless of build quality 95%
    Per-seat load 10 000 attempts spread over 500 seats, 60 s ≈0.33 /s/seat
    Max holds bounded by seat count — one hold per seat, ever ≤500

    Average load tells you nothing about surviving a hot show. 5.8/s platform-wide vs 166.7/s on 500 keys — and 95% of those attempts cannot possibly succeed, no matter how well the system is built.

    Before You Draw Anything — The Four Moves

    1. Clarify, don't assume. Single seat or multiple? General-admission or assigned-seat — say assigned-seat, it's the harder, more interesting case. — Two minutes of questions buys the whole design.
    2. Agree the scale out loud. "500 K bookings/day baseline, but a hot show is 10 000 attempts on 500 seats in 60 s." Now every choice has a reason. — An unstated number is an undefendable design.
    3. Draw the happy path only. Client → gateway → booking service → seat store → payment. Get agreement before adding anything. — Boxes you add before agreement are boxes you'll defend alone.
    4. Name one trade-off unprompted. "I'd choose consistency over availability here — a double-sold seat is a real failure, not a stale read." — This is the single move that separates L1 from a junior answer.

    What L1 Is Testing

    • Do you name the atomic claim mechanism unprompted?
    • Do you choose consistency over availability, and say why?
    • Did you ask assigned-seat vs general-admission before drawing?

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. Two people click "book" on the same seat in the same millisecond. What actually decides who gets it? (refs 3,12 — tests: an atomic-claim mechanism, not a hand-wave)
    2. 10 000 people try to buy 500 seats in the first minute. What's your plan for the 9 500 who can't get one? (refs 5,10 — tests: fail-fast awareness, not "the database handles it")
    3. A user holds a seat, then closes their browser without paying. What happens to that seat, and when? (refs 6,7 — tests: TTL / hold-expiry awareness)

    At L1 you are being judged on one thing: can you produce a coherent shape and defend three choices inside it. Depth is page 2 — do not reach for it here. Ghosted boxes are what arrives next; you should be able to guess two of them.

    Level 2 — The Defence (Intermediate)

    Pacing: 5 min ask · 15 min trace flow · 10 min read why · 30 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Browse shows/events and seat maps — the BookMyShow problem
    • Select seat(s), place a temporary hold during payment
    • Confirm on success; auto-release the hold on failure or timeout
    • Near-real-time taken / held / free view for everyone watching

    Non-Functional

    • A seat must never sell twice ← forces an atomic claim
    • A hold must not block forever ← forces a TTL'd lease
    • A hot show bursts claims on 500 keys ← forces per-seat locking
    • Viewing shouldn't hammer the source of truth ← forces a cached view

    Out of Scope — Say It Aloud

    • Payment internals — card networks, PSP integration
    • Seating chart / venue design tooling
    • Pricing, discounts, dynamic pricing

    Agree These First

    500 000 bookings/day baseline · a hot show: 10 000 concurrent attempts on one 500-seat show in the first 60 s · a 5-min hold window. Round, illustrative numbers, not any named company's.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client(s) — Thousands, same hot show, same 60 seconds
    • (1) claim attempt — 166.7 /s, one hot show
    • API Gateway — Routes browse traffic apart from claim traffic
    • Booking Service — Checks availability · creates a hold on claim
    • (8) Seat-Map View — Cache / pub-sub — what viewers actually poll; browse traffic — most viewers, not buyers; publish update; free
    • Seat Store — Source of truth · per-seat status: available / held / booked; 500 seats · at most 500 outstanding holds, ever
    • Hold Sweeper — Expires holds past TTL · returns the seat to available; sweep + release, async
    • Payment Service — Out of scope — a boundary you don't own transactionally
    • (5) Rejected — 409 · fails fast, never queues behind the winner
    • (9) 200 OK · booking confirmed — never a second claim
    • (6) TTL 5 min
    • (10) Admission Queue — A bounded trickle into Booking Service

    Data model:

    • seats seat_id PK · show_id · status · held_by · hold_expires_at
    • bookings booking_id PK · seat_id · user_id · payment_status

    95% of attempts — by design.

    Line = claim vs traffic: CLAIM · Seat Store write; READ · view or confirm; ASYNC · off critical path.

    Say It Like This

    "Unlike a typical read-heavy service, I'd choose consistency over availability here — a double-sold seat is a real failure, not a stale read. So every claim is a per-seat conditional write against the Seat Store, not one lock for the whole show — that lock would be held across a human's checkout. A hold gets a short TTL so an abandoned checkout doesn't block the seat forever. And anyone just looking at the seat map reads from a separate, cache-backed view — they never touch the strict path."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (3) Per-seat claim Conditional write per seat — 500 keys, each sees ~0.33/s One show-level lock → held across a 5-min checkout, it freezes all 500 seats
    (6) Hold + TTL Short lease, auto-expires — abandoned checkout frees the seat No hold → grabbed mid-checkout. Unbounded → blocks it forever
    (5) Fail fast Explicit 409, immediately — 95% can't win no matter what Queue/retry silently → 10 000 requests serialize, time out slowly
    (8) Seat-map view Cache / pub-sub — most traffic is looking, not claiming Read the Seat Store on every view → load unrelated to real contention
    (10) Admission queue A bounded trickle into Booking Service — controlled, not a stampede Let all 10 000 hit Booking Service directly → most time out anyway
    (7) Hold expiry Active sweeper — freed the instant the TTL lapses Lazy check-on-read only → a seat looks held long after it's free

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Baseline 500 000 ÷ 86 400 s — platform-wide, unremarkable 5.8 /s
    Hot show 10 000 ÷ 60 s — one show, ~29× the platform's average 166.7 /s
    Contention 10 000 attempts ÷ 500 seats 20 : 1
    Guaranteed-fail (10 000 − 500) ÷ 10 000 — true regardless of build quality 95%
    Per-seat load 10 000 attempts spread over 500 seats, 60 s ≈0.33 /s/seat
    Max holds bounded by seat count — one hold per seat, ever ≤500

    The system's job for that 95% is to fail them fast and cleanly — not let 10 000 requests serialize through a lock and time out slowly. That is what the admission queue and the 409 path exist for.

    What Breaks — Say It Before They Ask

    • (3) Hot-key contention. One show's seat map sees 166.7/s against 500 keys — one show-level lock serialises seats that never conflict, and is held across checkout. — Fix: per-seat conditional writes, spreading contention across independent keys
    • (7) Held-forever seat. A user abandons checkout mid-payment; without a TTL that seat is never released. — Fix: lease-based hold with auto-expiry
    • (8) Seat-map view lag. The read-optimized view is briefly behind the source of truth. — Fix: accept a small, stated staleness window on the VIEW only — the claim is always checked fresh, so this never causes a double-sale

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. Two people click "book" on the same seat in the same millisecond. What actually decides who gets it? (refs 3,12 — tests: an atomic-claim mechanism, not a hand-wave)
    2. 10 000 people try to buy 500 seats in the first minute. What's your plan for the 9 500 who can't get one? (refs 5,10 — tests: fail-fast awareness, not "the database handles it")
    3. A user holds a seat, then closes their browser without paying. What happens to that seat, and when? (refs 6,7 — tests: TTL / hold-expiry awareness)
    4. Why not just lock the whole show while someone checks out? (ref 3 — tests: coarse vs fine locking — the lock spans a human's checkout and blocks 499 seats nobody wants)
    5. The seat-map view says a seat is available. The user clicks it. It's gone. Is that a bug? (ref 8 — tests: staleness-on-read vs correctness-on-write)

    Cover The Sheet · Answer Aloud

    Use it as revision: cover the right rail and the two panels to your left, then walk the numbered path out loud. If you cannot say why a box is there and what breaks it, that box is your gap.

    Level 3 — The Edges (Advanced)

    Pacing: 10 min ask · 25 min trace flow · 15 min read why · 40 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Browse shows/events and seat maps — the BookMyShow problem
    • Select seat(s), place a temporary hold during payment
    • Confirm on success; auto-release the hold on failure or timeout
    • Near-real-time taken / held / free view for everyone watching

    Non-Functional

    • A seat must never sell twice ← forces an atomic claim
    • A hold must not block forever ← forces a TTL'd lease
    • A hot show bursts claims on 500 keys ← forces per-seat locking
    • Viewing shouldn't hammer the source of truth ← forces a cached view
    • Booking and payment must agree across a boundary you don't own ← forces a saga

    Out of Scope — Say It Aloud

    • Payment internals — card networks, PSP integration
    • Seating chart / venue design tooling
    • Pricing, discounts, dynamic pricing

    • (11) Hold-expiry vs payment-success race. A hold expires at nearly the same instant payment succeeds. — Fix: the claim is checked atomically again at payment-confirmation time, not only at hold-creation
