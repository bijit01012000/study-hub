    Project and ownership deep dives

    9 sections · 3,495 words

    Module 01 - 9 questions - 1 practical drill

    Prove where the team's work ended and your decisions began.

    The interviewer picks one line off your CV and drills for 30 minutes. The whole round is a test of whether you owned decisions or merely stood near them. Every follow-up is designed to find the seam between "we" and "I".

    Inside this module: Individual scope - Technical trade-offs - Measured impact - Reflection

    Q1.1 - Walk me through the most technically challenging project you worked on in the last two years.

    What it really tests: Whether you can select a project with real depth and narrate it as a sequence of your decisions rather than a feature tour.

    Weak answer: "We built a payments reconciliation system. It was a microservice in Spring Boot that read from a database and generated reports. It was challenging because there was a lot of data and we had to make it fast. We used caching and it worked well."

    Why it fails: - "We built" with no statement of which parts were the candidate's. - "Challenging because there was a lot of data" is a nonspecific difficulty; no number, no bottleneck named. - "Used caching and it worked" skips the decision, the alternative, and the failure mode. - No consumer of the system named, no metric moved. - No mention of what went wrong or what was hard about the reconciliation logic itself.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. OWNERSHIP - Which specific part of that system did you personally design and write? - Separating individual contribution from team output. 2. TRADEOFF - What was the single hardest technical decision, and what did you reject? - Whether a real alternative was weighed. 3. FAILURE - What broke first when load went up, and how did you find it? - Debugging under real constraints. 4. MEASUREMENT - How did you know it worked - what number did you watch? - Outcome tied to a metric, not a feeling. 5. REFLECTION - If you rebuilt it today, what would you do differently? - Learning and honest self-critique.

    Answer framework: 1. Problem and who felt it 2. Key constraint (latency/consistency/cost) 3. Your specific scope 4. Personal decision 5. Alternative rejected and why 6. What failed and how you caught it 7. Metric before/after 8. Reflection

    Rejection signals: - Cannot point to code or a component they alone owned. - Describes the architecture but no decision they personally made. - "It just worked" - no failure ever surfaced. - Uses "we" for every sentence including the hard parts.

    Model answer skeleton: I owned the reconciliation matching engine in our payments service. The hard constraint was that we had to match ~4M settlement rows against ledger entries within a 15-minute window before the next batch, and duplicates cost us real money.

    I chose a two-phase approach: hash-join in Postgres for exact matches, then a fuzzy pass in application code for the ~2% that had amount or timestamp drift. I rejected doing it all in-database because the fuzzy logic needed business rules that were painful in SQL and impossible to unit test.

    In production the first version silently double-counted refunds because I hadn't keyed on the reversal flag; I caught it when the daily variance report crossed a threshold I'd added, and fixed it by making the match key include transaction direction.

    That dropped unmatched entries from ~3% to under 0.1% and cut the manual finance review from two hours a day to about ten minutes.

    Q1.2 - Tell me about a design decision on that project that you now think was wrong.

    What it really tests: Intellectual honesty and whether you can evaluate your own past work with hindsight instead of defending it.

    Weak answer: "Honestly I think most of our decisions were pretty solid. Maybe we could have added more tests, but overall the design held up fine."

    Why it fails: - Deflects the question - refuses to name a real wrong decision. - "More tests" is the generic safe answer that signals no reflection. - No specific technical trade-off revisited. - Implies the candidate never revisits or measures their own choices.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. REFLECTION - What did that wrong decision cost you - time, an incident, rework? - Whether the cost was real and observed. 2. TECHNICAL - What information did you not have then that you have now? - Understanding of why the mistake was reasonable at the time. 3. OWNERSHIP - Did you migrate off it, or is it still running? - Follow-through on the correction. 4. OWNERSHIP - Who else was affected by that decision? - Awareness of blast radius.

    Answer framework: 1. The decision 2. Why it seemed right then 3. What surfaced the problem 4. Cost incurred 5. What you'd choose now 6. Whether you actually corrected it 7. Lesson generalised

    Rejection signals: - Cannot name any decision they'd change. - Blames the wrong choice entirely on someone else or "the deadline". - The "mistake" is trivial (naming, formatting) to avoid real exposure. - No evidence they measured the cost.

    Model answer skeleton: I chose to store our workflow state as a single JSON blob in one Postgres column because it was fast to ship and the shape was still changing. Within six months we needed to query 'all workflows stuck at step 3', and that meant full-table scans over the blob - a report that should take milliseconds took 40 seconds.

    The information I lacked was that the access pattern would shift from write-heavy to query-heavy once ops started using it.

    I'd now model the state transitions as proper rows from the start, or at least promote the frequently-queried fields into indexed columns.

    We did eventually migrate the hot fields out into a projection table, which brought that report back under 200ms.

    The lesson I took is that 'the schema is still changing' justifies flexibility on cold data, not on anything you'll filter by.

    Q1.3 - You mentioned you improved performance. Take me from the first symptom to the fix.

    What it really tests: Whether "improved performance" is a real measured story or a resume phrase.

    Weak answer: "The API was slow so we profiled it and optimised the queries and added an index. After that it was much faster and everyone was happy."

    Why it fails: - No starting number and no ending number. - "Optimised the queries" - which query, why was it slow? - No hypothesis, no measurement method named. - "Everyone was happy" replaces a metric with a vibe.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. MEASUREMENT - What was p95 before and after? - Real baseline and outcome. 2. TECHNICAL - How did you locate the slow path - profiler, tracing, EXPLAIN? - Method over guesswork. 3. FAILURE - What did you try that didn't move the number? - Honest dead-ends, not a clean fairy tale. 4. TRADEOFF - Why an index and not a cache or a query rewrite? - Considered alternatives.

    Answer framework: 1. Symptom and who reported it 2. How you measured baseline 3. Hypothesis 4. Tool used to confirm 5. Change made 6. Alternative rejected 7. Before/after number 8. Guardrail added to prevent regression

    Rejection signals: - No numbers at any point. - Jumps straight to the fix with no diagnosis. - Cannot say how they confirmed the bottleneck. - Every attempt worked first try - implausible.

    Model answer skeleton: Our order-history endpoint had crept to a p95 of 2.1s and support was getting complaints.

    I pulled the endpoint's traces in Datadog and saw ~90% of the time was in one query. EXPLAIN ANALYZE showed a sequential scan over 12M rows because we filtered on user_id, status but only had an index on user_id.

    I first tried adding LIMIT and pagination, which helped the tail but not the median.

    The real fix was a composite index on (user_id, status, created_at) which let Postgres satisfy both the filter and the sort from the index. p95 dropped from 2.1s to ~110ms.

    I added a slow-query alert at 500ms so we'd catch the next regression before customers did.

    Q1.4 - Who depended on the system you built, and what would have happened to them if it went down for an hour?

    What it really tests: Whether you understand your system's place in a larger business, not just its code.

    Weak answer: "Other teams used it through the API. If it went down they'd probably get errors, but we had good uptime so it wasn't really an issue."

    Why it fails: - "Other teams" - cannot name a single consumer. - Never quantifies the downstream impact. - "Good uptime so not an issue" dodges the actual question. - No awareness of criticality tiering or SLA.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - Name the top consumer and the exact call they made. - Concrete knowledge of dependents. 2. TRADEOFF - Was this on a critical user path or a background one? - Understanding of blast radius. 3. MEASUREMENT - Did it have an SLA, and did you ever breach it? - Operational maturity. 4. FAILURE - What was the fallback for consumers when you were unavailable? - Failure-path awareness.

    Answer framework: 1. Named consumers 2. Their call pattern 3. Criticality tier 4. SLA if any 5. Impact of an hour down 6. Fallback that existed 7. What you owned in keeping it up

    Rejection signals: - Cannot name a consumer. - No sense of whether the system was critical or peripheral. - No fallback story. - Treats availability as someone else's concern.

    Model answer skeleton: The biggest consumer was our checkout service, which called my inventory-reservation API synchronously on every 'place order' click - roughly 300 requests/second at peak.

    If I went down for an hour on a weekday, checkout would either fail outright or oversell stock, both of which hit revenue directly, so we were tiered as a critical path service with a 99.95% SLA.

    We breached it once, for about 20 minutes, when a bad deploy exhausted the connection pool.

    After that I added a fast-fail path: if reservation timed out past 200ms, checkout fell back to optimistic reservation and reconciled asynchronously, so a future outage degraded rather than blocked.

    Owning that fallback design is the part I'm proudest of, because it changed our worst case from 'checkout is down' to 'checkout is slightly riskier for a few minutes'.

    Q1.5 - What part of that project did you disagree with the team on?

    What it really tests: Whether you have technical opinions and can hold them without being combative.

    Weak answer: "I generally went along with what the team decided. We had good discussions and usually reached consensus, so there wasn't much disagreement."

    Why it fails: - Signals no independent technical judgement. - "Went along" reads as passivity, not collaboration. - No specific technical position ever stated. - Avoids the question entirely.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - What was your technical argument, specifically? - Substance of the position. 2. OWNERSHIP - How did you raise it without stalling the project? - Disagreeing productively. 3. REFLECTION - You lost the argument - what happened next? - Disagree-and-commit maturity. 4. MEASUREMENT - Did production later prove either side right? - Outcome, not just opinion.

    Answer framework: 1. The point of disagreement 2. Your technical case 3. How you raised it 4. How it was decided 5. Whether you committed 6. What the outcome showed 7. What you learned about picking battles

    Rejection signals: - Never disagrees with anyone. - Disagreed but couldn't articulate a technical reason. - Sabotaged or sulked after losing. - No follow-through on the outcome.

    Model answer skeleton: The team wanted to introduce Kafka for a feature that produced maybe 50 events a minute, and I argued that was operational weight we didn't need - a Postgres outbox table plus a poller would give us the same reliability with nothing new to run.

    I put both options on one page with the operational cost of each and shared it before the design review rather than debating it live.

    The team still chose Kafka because they wanted the pattern in place for a larger project coming next quarter, and I committed to it and built the producer.

    In hindsight they were partly right: that next project did arrive, and having Kafka already bedded in saved us weeks.

    What I learned is to weigh not just today's load but the roadmap when I argue about infrastructure.

    Q1.6 - How did you test that project, and what did your tests miss?

    What it really tests: Testing maturity and the honesty to admit tests are never complete.

    Weak answer: "We had good test coverage, around 85%, with unit tests and some integration tests. QA also tested it before release."

    Why it fails: - Coverage percentage as a proxy for quality - a known anti-signal. - "Some integration tests" is vague about the real risk areas. - Cannot name a single bug tests missed. - Offloads correctness to QA.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. FAILURE - What kind of bug slipped past your tests into production? - Awareness of testing blind spots. 2. TECHNICAL - How did you test the failure paths - timeouts, retries, partial writes? - Beyond happy-path testing. 3. TRADEOFF - What did you deliberately not test, and why? - Pragmatic prioritisation. 4. REFLECTION - After the miss, what did you add? - Feedback loop closure.

    Answer framework: 1. Test strategy 2. What each layer covered 3. A real escape 4. Why tests missed it 5. Trade-off on what you skipped 6. What you added after 7. General lesson on test design

    Rejection signals: - Coverage number is the whole answer. - Cannot name a bug that escaped. - No concept of testing failure paths. - Treats testing as QA's job.

    Model answer skeleton: We had unit tests on the pricing logic and integration tests against a real Postgres in CI, but our tests missed a concurrency bug: two simultaneous requests could both read stock as available and both reserve it, oversell by one.

    It escaped because every test ran serially, so the race never appeared.

    In production it showed up as occasional negative inventory during flash sales.

    I added a test that fired concurrent reservations against the real DB and asserted the invariant, and fixed the code with a SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the stock row.

    I'd deliberately not written UI-level end-to-end tests because they were slow and flaky and the team maintained a separate Cypress suite.

    The lesson was that anything touching shared mutable state needs an explicitly concurrent test, not just a serial one.

    Q1.7 - What was the state of that project when you joined versus when you left?

    What it really tests: Whether you can articulate delta - the change you personally caused - rather than describe a static system.

    Weak answer: "It was already a mature system when I joined and I helped maintain and improve it. By the time I left it was running well and we'd added several features."

    Why it fails: - No before-state and no after-state with any specificity. - "Helped maintain and improve" attributes nothing to the candidate. - "Added several features" - which, and did they matter? - No metric of change.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. OWNERSHIP - What was the single biggest thing that was different because you were there? - Individual delta. 2. MEASUREMENT - What metric moved on your watch? - Quantified impact. 3. TECHNICAL - What did you inherit that was broken, and did you fix it? - Engagement with existing debt. 4. REFLECTION - What did you leave behind for the next person? - Stewardship.

    Answer framework: 1. Inherited state 2. Problems you found 3. What you chose to fix first 4. Change you drove 5. Metric before/after 6. What you left better 7. Handover done

    Rejection signals: - Cannot describe a before/after delta. - Nothing specifically attributable to them. - No metric. - Left no documentation or improvements for others.

    Model answer skeleton: When I joined, our nightly ETL took about six hours and frequently failed past 6am, which meant analysts started their day without fresh data two or three times a week.

    I profiled it and found it was re-processing the full history every night instead of just the delta.

    I reworked it to incremental loads keyed on an updated-at watermark, and split the monolithic job into idempotent stages that could retry independently.

    By the time I left, the run was about 40 minutes and hadn't missed its 6am deadline in four months.

    I also wrote the first runbook for it, because before me the recovery steps lived only in one senior engineer's head.

    Q1.8 - If I called a teammate from that project, what would they say you specifically contributed?

    What it really tests: Self-awareness and whether your self-description survives an external check.

    Weak answer: "They'd say I was a good team player, hardworking, and that I always delivered my tasks on time."

    Why it fails: - Generic soft-skill adjectives, no technical specificity. - "Delivered tasks on time" describes an executor, not an owner. - Nothing a teammate could uniquely attribute to this person. - No mention of a system, decision, or save.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. OWNERSHIP - What technical thing would they credit you for that nobody else could have done? - Unique contribution. 2. REFLECTION - What would they say was your weakness on that project? - Honest self-view. 3. TECHNICAL - Who did you unblock, and how? - Force-multiplier behaviour.

    Answer framework: 1. Your defining contribution 2. Why it was uniquely yours 3. A time you unblocked someone 4. A weakness they'd name 5. How you worked on it 6. Evidence of the reputation

    Rejection signals: - Only soft adjectives, no technical anchor. - Cannot name a weakness. - Nothing uniquely theirs. - The described contribution is generic to any team member.

    Model answer skeleton: They'd say I was the person who finally made our deploys boring.

    Before me, releases were manual and we rolled back by hand roughly once a month; I built the migration-and-rollback tooling and the canary check that gated every deploy.

    They'd also, fairly, say I over-engineered the first version of it - I added feature flags and staged rollout before we had the traffic to justify the complexity, and a couple of people found it fiddly.

    I unblocked the mobile team specifically by adding contract tests so their client stopped breaking silently when we changed a response shape.

    The weakness I've worked on since is shipping the simplest version first and adding machinery only when the pain is real.

    Q1.9 - [DRILL] Take the project on the top line of your CV and write, in five bullet points, exactly what YOU decided - not what the team built.

    What it really tests: Forces the candidate to convert a "we" narrative into an "I" inventory before the interview, exposing where they were a passenger.

    Weak answer: N/A - this is a preparation drill, not an interview question. A weak attempt produces bullets that all start with "We" or describe features rather than decisions.

    Why it fails: - Bullets describe what the system does, not what the candidate chose. - Every bullet is a "we". - No alternative-rejected appears against any decision. - No metric attached to any decision's outcome.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TRADEOFF - For each bullet, what did you reject to get there? - Every decision has a discarded alternative. 2. MEASUREMENT - For each bullet, what number would prove it worked? - Decisions map to outcomes. 3. REFLECTION - Which bullet would collapse first under interviewer drilling? - Self-audit of weak spots.

    Answer framework: 1. Pick one project 2. List five decisions in first person 3. Attach rejected alternative to each 4. Attach a metric to each 5. Rank them by how well you can defend them 6. Rehearse the weakest 7. Convert any surviving "we" to "I" or delete it

    Rejection signals: - Cannot fill five bullets without repeating "the team". - No decision survives the "what did you reject" pass. - No metric anywhere.

    Model answer skeleton: Drill output example: (1) I chose cursor pagination over offset for the feed API because offset drifted under concurrent inserts - measured by zero duplicate-item complaints after launch. (2) I decided to cache the user-permissions lookup in Redis with a 60s TTL rather than per-request DB hits, cutting authz p95 from 40ms to 3ms. (3) I rejected a shared library for event schemas in favour of a schema registry, because the library coupled deploy cycles across four teams. (4) I made the payment webhook idempotent with a stored idempotency key after a duplicate-charge incident. (5) I set the consumer to manual offset commit so a crash mid-batch reprocessed rather than dropped messages.

    Weakest bullet to defend: (3), because the registry added ops overhead I'd need to justify.
