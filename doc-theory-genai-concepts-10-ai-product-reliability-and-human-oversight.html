    AI product reliability and human oversight

    1 sections · 599 words

    Module 10 · 10 questions · 1 practical drill

    Ship something useful, not just a working pipeline.

    This module tests something most GenAI material skips entirely: whether a candidate can ship a feature that fails safely, not just one that works in a demo. Nearly every technical module in this guide asks about capability, this one asks about restraint, about what the system does when it doesn't know, who is allowed to decide a wrong answer is too expensive to risk, and what happens the moment a monitored number crosses a line someone actually chose in advance. A good answer here sounds almost boring on purpose: thresholds set before launch, fallbacks that were actually tested, a rollback rehearsed rather than assumed. A weak answer sounds fluent and confident, right up until you ask what happens when the model is wrong.

    Inside this module: LLM or not · Abstention · Fallbacks · Launch criteria

    How to work this module: Answer each question out loud in 90 seconds before reading the model answer. Then check you named a number, the alternative you rejected, and what actually failed. Those three are what the follow-ups are hunting for.

    Concept checkpoints

    Q10.1 — Why would you use an LLM here instead of writing deterministic, rule-based code?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate picked the LLM because it fits the problem, or defaulted to it because it's fashionable, without ever comparing alternatives.

    Q10.2 — What failure rate is acceptable for this feature, and whose call is that?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate treats the failure budget as a deliberate, owned product decision, not an engineering afterthought.

    Q10.3 — When should this system abstain instead of giving an answer?

    What it really tests: Whether abstention is treated as a designed, valuable outcome, or as a failure mode the candidate is trying to engineer away entirely.

    Q10.4 — How do you design the deterministic fallback for when the model fails?

    What it really tests: Whether the fallback is a real, separately engineered path, or just a retry and an error message dressed up as a plan.

    Q10.5 — Why can't you trust the model's own confidence score, and what do you use instead?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate knows self-reported confidence is uncalibrated, and has a real independent signal to gate on instead.

    Q10.6 — Which actions need human approval before they happen, and where's that line?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate has a real, reasoned rule for where automation stops, not a vague "risky stuff needs a human."

    Q10.7 — How do you collect user feedback on this feature without poisoning your own data?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate knows naive feedback signals are biased and can reinforce mistakes rather than correct them.

    Q10.8 — How do citations, explanations and disclosure change how users trust this feature?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate sees these as trust tools that must earn honesty about real confidence, not decoration that creates false reassurance.

    Q10.9 — What has to be true before you're willing to launch this feature?

    What it really tests: Whether launch readiness covers monitoring, fallback and ownership, or is a single accuracy number crossed off a list.

    Q10.10 — Design launch criteria, failure handling and rollback for a feature that auto-approves refunds.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate assembles everything in this module into one coherent, high-risk launch plan, not just the pieces recited in isolation.
