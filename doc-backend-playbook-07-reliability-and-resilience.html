    Reliability and resilience

    9 sections · 3,757 words

    Module 07 - 9 questions - 1 practical drill

    Build for inevitable failure without creating a second-order outage.

    This round tests whether you build for the failure that will happen, not the sunny path. Follow-ups probe the second-order effects: retries that cause storms, circuit breakers that never close, timeouts that cascade. Naming patterns isn't enough - you must know their failure modes.

    Inside this module: Retry discipline - Circuit breakers - Bulkheads - Timeout budgets

    Q7.1 - A downstream dependency you call is slow or failing. Walk me through how your service behaves.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate has timeouts, circuit breakers, and fallbacks - or lets a slow dependency take them down.

    Weak answer: "We'd retry the call a few times, and if it kept failing we'd return an error to the user. We also had monitoring to alert us."

    Why it fails: - Retries without backoff can amplify the downstream's overload. - No timeout mentioned, so a slow dependency ties up threads. - No circuit breaker to stop hammering a dead service. - No fallback or graceful degradation.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. FAILURE - No timeout on that call - what happens to your thread pool? - Resource-exhaustion cascade. 2. TECHNICAL - How does a circuit breaker help versus just retrying? - Fail-fast under sustained failure. 3. TRADEOFF - Retrying a struggling service - how do you avoid making it worse? - Backoff + jitter + budget. 4. REFLECTION - What can you serve when the dependency is down? - Fallback / degradation.

    Answer framework: 1. Timeout on every call 2. Bounded retries with backoff + jitter 3. Circuit breaker to fail fast under sustained failure 4. Fallback / cached / degraded response 5. Bulkhead to isolate the dependency 6. Alert 7. Recover when breaker half-opens

    Rejection signals: - No timeout on external calls. - Retries without backoff. - No circuit breaker. - No fallback; user just gets an error.

    Model answer skeleton: The first line of defence is a timeout on every outbound call, because without one a slow dependency holds my request threads open until my own pool exhausts and I take down my whole service over one struggling downstream. On failure I retry, but bounded and with exponential backoff plus jitter, because fixed immediate retries from every instance at once just pile onto an already-overloaded service and turn a blip into an outage.

    On top of that I run a circuit breaker: after a threshold of failures it opens and I fail fast for a cooldown instead of sending doomed calls, then half-opens to test recovery before closing.

    While the breaker is open I serve a fallback - a cached value, a default, or a degraded response - so the user gets something useful rather than a spinner.

    I also bulkhead that dependency onto its own thread pool so its failure can't starve unrelated requests.

    The principle is contain the blast radius: one sick dependency should degrade one feature, not the service.

    Q7.2 - Explain how you'd configure retries so they help instead of causing an outage.

    What it really tests: Deep understanding of retry storms, backoff, jitter, and retry budgets.

    Weak answer: "We set retries to three attempts. If the call failed we'd retry up to three times before giving up."

    Why it fails: - Fixed immediate retries create synchronised retry storms. - No backoff or jitter. - No retry budget across the fleet. - Retries non-idempotent operations blindly.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. FAILURE - Every instance retries at the same interval - what happens to the downstream? - Thundering-herd retries. 2. TECHNICAL - Why add jitter to backoff? - De-synchronising retries. 3. TRADEOFF - What's a retry budget and why cap total retries? - Fleet-level retry control. 4. MEASUREMENT - Which operations should you never retry? - Idempotency awareness.

    Answer framework: 1. Only retry idempotent/safe operations 2. Exponential backoff 3. Add jitter 4. Cap attempts 5. Enforce a retry budget across the fleet 6. Respect Retry-After 7. Combine with circuit breaker 8. Distinguish retryable vs fatal errors

    Rejection signals: - Fixed-interval immediate retries. - No jitter. - No budget, so retries can dominate traffic. - Retries non-idempotent writes.

    Model answer skeleton: Retries are dangerous configured naively. If every instance retries three times at a fixed interval, a downstream hiccup causes all of them to retry in lockstep, tripling load exactly when the service is weakest - a self-inflicted outage.

    So I use exponential backoff, doubling the wait between attempts, and add jitter - randomising the delay - so retries spread out instead of synchronising into a wave.

    I cap attempts, and at the fleet level I use a retry budget: retries may only be, say, 10% of total requests, so under widespread failure the system stops retrying rather than drowning the downstream. I only retry idempotent or safe operations - a GET or an idempotent-keyed write - and never blindly retry a non-idempotent charge, because a retry might duplicate work.

    I honour a Retry-After when the server sends one, and I pair retries with a circuit breaker so once failures are sustained I stop retrying entirely and fail fast.

    And I distinguish retryable errors like a timeout or 503 from fatal ones like a 400, which I never retry.

    Q7.3 - Your circuit breaker opened in production. Then what?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate understands breaker states, recovery, and the user experience while it's open.

    Weak answer: "The circuit breaker opens when there are too many failures, so it stops calling the failing service. Once the service recovers it closes again."

    Why it fails: - Describes the concept but not the half-open recovery test. - No mention of what the user gets while it's open. - Doesn't consider a breaker that never closes (permanent failure). - No fallback behaviour specified.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - What's the half-open state doing? - Recovery-probing mechanics. 2. FAILURE - While the breaker is open, what does the user experience? - Fallback during open state. 3. MEASUREMENT - The breaker flaps open/closed repeatedly - what's wrong? - Threshold tuning / grey failure. 4. REFLECTION - What if the downstream is permanently gone? - Long-term degradation strategy.

    Answer framework: 1. Open = fail fast, no calls 2. Serve fallback 3. Tune thresholds to avoid flapping 4. Probe with limited calls 5. Close on success / reopen on failure 6. After cooldown, half-open 7. Plan for permanent-failure degradation 8. Alert on open state

    Rejection signals: - Doesn't know the half-open state. - No fallback during open. - Can't explain flapping. - No plan for a permanently dead dependency.

    Model answer skeleton: When the breaker opens, it fails fast - it stops sending calls to the failing dependency and immediately returns the fallback, which protects both my service (no threads blocked) and the downstream (no pile-on while it recovers).

    While it's open the user should get a graceful fallback, not an error page - a cached value, a queued action, or a 'temporarily unavailable, we'll retry' depending on the feature.

    After a cooldown the breaker goes half-open and lets a small number of trial calls through: if they succeed it closes and normal traffic resumes, if they fail it reopens for another cooldown.

    If I see it flapping open and closed rapidly, that usually means my thresholds are too twitchy or the downstream is in a grey state where some calls succeed, so I tune the failure-rate window.

    And I plan for the dependency being gone for a long time - the fallback has to be sustainable, not just a stopgap - and I alert the moment a breaker opens, because an open breaker is a real signal that a dependency is down, not something to notice hours later.

    Q7.4 - How do you stop one slow feature from taking down your whole service?

    What it really tests: Bulkheading and resource isolation - the idea that failures should be contained.

    Weak answer: "We'd optimise the slow feature so it wasn't slow, and add caching to speed it up so it didn't affect the rest."

    Why it fails: - Treats the symptom (slowness) not the containment problem. - No isolation of resources between features. - One shared thread/connection pool means one feature can starve all. - No bulkhead concept.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. FAILURE - One shared thread pool, one slow endpoint saturates it - what happens to the fast endpoints? - Shared-resource starvation. 2. TECHNICAL - What's a bulkhead and how does it isolate this? - Bulkhead pattern. 3. TRADEOFF - How do you size separate pools per dependency? - Capacity partitioning. 4. MEASUREMENT - How does this interact with load shedding? - Combining resilience patterns.

    Answer framework: 1. Identify shared resources (threads, connections) 2. Partition them per feature/dependency (bulkhead) 3. Separate pools so one can't starve others 4. Size pools by criticality 5. Add circuit breakers per bulkhead 6. Load-shed non-critical work first 7. Verify isolation under load test

    Rejection signals: - Only optimises, no isolation. - One shared pool for everything. - Never heard of bulkheads. - No prioritisation of critical vs non-critical.

    Model answer skeleton: Optimising the slow feature is worth doing but it's not containment - the real risk is a shared resource. If every endpoint draws from one thread pool or one DB connection pool, a single slow endpoint saturates the pool and every other endpoint, including the critical ones, starts timing out even though they're perfectly healthy.

    The fix is the bulkhead pattern: partition the resources so each feature or dependency gets its own pool, and one drowning doesn't sink the others - like watertight compartments in a ship.

    I size the pools by criticality, giving the checkout path a guaranteed slice and capping the risky third-party integration to a small pool so it can starve only itself. Each bulkhead gets its own circuit breaker so a failing dependency trips independently.

    And I combine this with load shedding: under pressure I drop non-critical work - analytics, recommendations - before anything touches the core path.

    I verify the isolation with a load test that hammers the slow endpoint and confirms the fast ones stay healthy.

    Q7.5 - What's your timeout strategy across a chain of service calls?

    What it really tests: Understanding that timeouts must be budgeted down a call chain, or the outer caller times out while inner work continues.

    Weak answer: "We set a timeout on each call, something like 30 seconds, so calls wouldn't hang forever."

    Why it fails: - A uniform 30s timeout at every hop means the chain can far exceed the client's patience. - No timeout budget decreasing down the chain. - 30s is far too long for most calls - hides slowness. - Doesn't cancel downstream work when the caller gives up.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - Client waits 5s, but your chain is A->B->C each at 30s - what's wrong? - Timeout budgeting. 2. FAILURE - The caller times out - is C still doing work? - Cancellation propagation. 3. MEASUREMENT - How do you set the timeout value in the first place? - Data-driven from latency percentiles. 4. TRADEOFF - How do timeouts interact with retries in the budget? - Combined time budget.

    Answer framework: 1. Start from the client's deadline 2. Budget it down the chain (each hop gets less) 3. Set values from p99 latency, not guesses 4. Propagate a deadline/cancellation 5. Cancel downstream work on timeout 6. Account for retries in the budget 7. Fail fast when budget exhausted

    Rejection signals: - Same long timeout at every hop. - No timeout budget concept. - Downstream keeps working after caller gives up. - Timeout values are round-number guesses.

    Model answer skeleton: Timeouts have to be budgeted down the chain, not set uniformly. If the user's client waits 5 seconds but A calls B calls C each with a 30-second timeout, the chain can run for 90 seconds doing work nobody's waiting for anymore - the client bailed at 5.

    So I start from the outermost deadline and hand each hop a shrinking slice: if A has 5s, it gives B maybe 4s, which gives C maybe 3s, leaving headroom for A's own work.

    I set the actual values from observed latency - a bit above p99 for that call - rather than round guesses, because a 30s timeout on a call that normally takes 50ms just hides problems.

    Critically, when the caller times out I propagate a deadline or cancellation token so C stops working instead of burning resources on a doomed request.

    And I fold retries into the budget - a retry can't push the total past the deadline - so the whole chain fails fast and predictably rather than dribbling past the point the user cared.

    Q7.6 - How did you make sure your service degraded gracefully instead of failing hard under load?

    What it really tests: Load shedding, graceful degradation, and prioritising critical functionality.

    Weak answer: "We autoscaled to add more instances when load went up, so we could handle the extra traffic without failing."

    Why it fails: - Autoscaling has lag and limits - it doesn't help in the first minutes of a spike. - No load shedding when even scaled capacity is exceeded. - No prioritisation of critical vs optional work. - Assumes you can always scale out of a problem.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. FAILURE - Traffic spikes faster than autoscaling can react - now what? - Load shedding as the immediate defence. 2. TRADEOFF - Under overload, what do you drop first? - Prioritisation of features. 3. TECHNICAL - How do you shed load fairly across users? - Fair shedding / prioritised queues. 4. MEASUREMENT - How do you signal clients to back off before you hit the wall? - Backpressure.

    Answer framework: 1. Autoscaling as the slow lever 2. Load shedding as the fast lever 3. Prioritise critical over optional work 4. Shed non-critical first 5. Fair shedding across tenants 6. Backpressure signals (429 early) 7. Protect a core capacity 8. Recover as load falls

    Rejection signals: - Autoscaling is the only answer. - No load shedding. - No feature prioritisation under overload. - No backpressure to clients.

    Model answer skeleton: Autoscaling is part of it but it's the slow lever - instances take a minute or two to spin up, and a sharp spike or a scaling ceiling means I need an immediate defence, which is load shedding. Under overload I actively drop the least important work first: recommendations, non-essential analytics, and background enrichment get shed so that checkout and payments keep their capacity.

    I shed fairly so one heavy tenant can't consume everyone's headroom, using per-tenant limits, and I prioritise so degraded still means the core journey works.

    Before I hit the wall I apply backpressure - returning 429 with Retry-After at, say, 80% capacity - so well-behaved clients throttle themselves rather than all arriving at once and toppling me.

    The mindset is that a service under extreme load should get simpler and slower but stay up for the important paths, rather than trying to serve everything and collapsing entirely.

    As load falls, shedding relaxes and the dropped features come back.

    Q7.7 - How do you know your resilience actually works before a real outage tests it?

    What it really tests: Chaos engineering, failure injection, and game days - proactive vs hopeful resilience.

    Weak answer: "We tested it in staging and made sure the error handling worked. We were pretty confident it would hold up in production."

    Why it fails: - Staging rarely reproduces production failure conditions. - No deliberate failure injection. - "Pretty confident" is hope, not verification. - Never tested the fallback path actually firing under real conditions.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TECHNICAL - How do you verify the fallback fires when the dependency is really down? - Testing the failure path, not the happy path. 2. TRADEOFF - What's chaos engineering and how did you use it? - Proactive failure injection. 3. FAILURE - How do you inject failure safely in production? - Blast-radius control. 4. REFLECTION - What did a game day reveal that you didn't expect? - Learning from injected failure.

    Answer framework: 1. Test failure paths explicitly, not just happy path 2. Inject failures (latency, errors, kills) 3. Start in staging, then controlled production 4. Limit blast radius 5. Run game days 6. Verify fallbacks and alerts fire 7. Feed findings back into fixes

    Rejection signals: - Only tested the happy path in staging. - Never injected failure. - "Confident it'll work" with no evidence. - Never tested a fallback actually triggering.

    Model answer skeleton: The uncomfortable truth is that resilience code you never trigger is resilience code you don't know works - plenty of fallbacks have bugs that only surface the first time the dependency actually dies. So I test the failure paths deliberately.

    In staging I inject failures - kill a dependency, add latency, return errors - and assert that the timeout fires, the circuit breaker opens, the fallback serves, and the alert pages someone.

    Then I do controlled chaos experiments in production with a tight blast radius: fail a small percentage of calls to one dependency and watch that the system degrades as designed rather than cascades.

    We ran periodic game days where we'd take down a component on purpose and have the on-call team respond, which is as much about testing the humans and the runbooks as the code.

    One game day revealed our fallback cache had gone stale because nothing refreshed it when the primary was up, so it 'worked' but served week-old data - exactly the kind of thing you only find by pulling the plug on purpose.

    Q7.8 - Fail open or fail closed - how did you decide for a given check?

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate understands that the safe default depends on what the check protects.

    Weak answer: "We'd fail closed to be safe - if something failed we'd block the request so nothing bad could happen."

    Why it fails: - Blanket "fail closed is safe" isn't always right - it can cause a total outage. - No distinction between security checks and availability features. - Doesn't weigh the cost of blocking legitimate traffic. - No per-check reasoning.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TRADEOFF - Your auth service is down - fail open or closed, and why? - Security-critical fail-closed. 2. TECHNICAL - Your feature-flag service is down - which way? - Availability-oriented fail-open. 3. FAILURE - What's the risk of failing closed on a non-critical check? - Unnecessary outage from over-caution. 4. MEASUREMENT - How do you make the default explicit and safe? - Deliberate default design.

    Answer framework: 1. Identify what the check protects 2. Security/correctness 3. fail closed 4. Availability/enhancement 5. fail open with a safe default 6. Weigh cost of blocking vs cost of allowing 7. Make the default explicit 8. Alert either way 9. Avoid single points that fail the whole system closed

    Rejection signals: - "Always fail closed" or "always fail open" as a rule. - No per-check reasoning. - Doesn't consider the outage risk of failing closed. - No safe default for availability features.

    Model answer skeleton: It depends entirely on what the check protects, and treating it as one blanket rule causes outages.

    For anything security- or correctness-critical I fail closed: if the authorization service is unreachable, I deny the request, because allowing unauthenticated access to protected data is far worse than a brief outage.

    But for availability and enhancement features I fail open with a safe default: if the feature-flag service is down, I fall back to the flags' default values and keep serving, because taking the whole product down just because a flag lookup failed is absurd.

    The trap is reflexively failing closed on non-critical checks - I've seen a recommendation-service dependency failing closed and blocking the entire page, an entirely self-inflicted outage.

    So per check I ask 'what's worse: blocking a legitimate request or allowing a risky one?' and I make the default explicit in code rather than accidental, and I alert on either path so a silent fail-open doesn't hide a broken dependency for weeks.

    Q7.9 - [DRILL] Take a checkout endpoint that calls payment, inventory, and a fraud service. Annotate each call with its timeout, retry policy, breaker, and fallback.

    What it really tests: Whether the candidate can apply the full resilience toolkit differentially - not the same policy everywhere.

    Weak answer: N/A - drill. A weak attempt applies identical retry+timeout to all three.

    Why it fails: - Same policy for a critical payment call and an optional fraud check. - Retries a non-idempotent payment blindly. - No fallback distinction between must-have and nice-to-have. - Uniform timeouts ignore each call's latency profile.

    Follow-up ladder: 1. TRADEOFF - Fraud service is down - do you block checkout or proceed? - Fail-open on non-critical. 2. FAILURE - Do you retry the payment call, and how safely? - Idempotency on money. 3. MEASUREMENT - Why different timeouts for each of the three? - Per-dependency tuning.

    Answer framework: 1. Payment: idempotency-keyed, tight timeout, cautious retry, fail closed 2. Inventory: short timeout, retry, breaker, fail closed 3. Fraud: short timeout, no/one retry, breaker, fail open to a default 4. Bulkhead each 5. Budget total time under client deadline 6. Alert per breaker

    Rejection signals: - Identical policy across all three. - Retries payment without idempotency. - Fails closed on the fraud check, blocking checkout. - Timeouts ignore per-call latency.

    Model answer skeleton: Drill output: the three calls get different policies because they matter differently.

    Payment: idempotency-keyed so a retry can't double-charge, a tight timeout (~2s), at most one cautious retry only on a clear no-op error like a 503, circuit breaker on, and fail closed - no payment, no order.

    Inventory: short timeout (~500ms), a couple of backoff retries since the reservation is idempotent, breaker on, fail closed because overselling is unacceptable.

    Fraud: short timeout (~300ms), no retry or a single one, breaker on, and crucially fail open - if fraud scoring is down, I proceed with a default 'allow but flag for async review' rather than blocking every checkout on a non-critical enhancement.

    Each dependency sits in its own bulkhead so one can't starve the others, the combined timeouts plus retries stay under the client's deadline, and each breaker alerts when it opens.

    The through-line is that resilience is differential: cautious and fail-closed on money and stock, permissive and fail-open on the optional check.
