    Q34. How would you design observability for a microservices platform end to end?

    4 sections · 684 words

    Core message: I'd design observability as four layers plus a payoff layer — metrics, logs, traces, topology, all joined and consumed by alerts that carry context and RCA automation — because observability is the platform's debugging API.

    Framework

    Framework. I've built this at Rakuten, so I'll give you the playbook I'd actually run, not a textbook one. Four data layers, one join layer, one payoff layer.

    • Metrics: every service emits golden signals — latency, traffic, errors, saturation — and every service gets an SLO, because a metric without an objective is trivia. Dashboards are per-service-owner, not platform-owned.

    • Logs: structured JSON only, with correlation IDs threading every line back to a request — and sampling at ingest, because log storage billed by the gigabyte will silently become your biggest observability cost.

    • Traces: context propagation everywhere — including the boundaries that kill it silently: Kafka consumers and thread pools. Head sampling for volume control, tail sampling to keep every error trace.

    • Topology/service map as the join layer: this is what turns four separate data stores into one answer — "which downstream is degraded, and who calls it."

    • Cost discipline from day one: cardinality budgets per service, sampling tiers, per-team telemetry quotas. I learned this the hard way — an unbounded-attribute rollout once blew up our telemetry backend. Cost that isn't designed in becomes the reason observability gets gutted later.

    • The payoff layer is where the value lives: alerts that carry context — trace hints, owner routing, severity tiers — and RCA automation on top of all four layers. Our Auto-RCA agent consumes logs, metrics, topology, and thread dumps to synthesize ranked hypotheses: 500+ tickets/day, root cause in under 5 minutes. Without clean layers underneath, that's impossible — garbage in, hallucination out.

    Read it straight

    I've built this at Rakuten, so I'll give you the playbook I actually ran rather than a textbook one. Four data layers: metrics — golden signals with an SLO per service, because a metric without an objective is trivia; logs — structured JSON with correlation IDs and sampling at ingest, since storage will silently become your biggest cost; traces — context propagation everywhere, especially the boundaries that kill it silently like Kafka consumers and thread pools, with head sampling for volume and tail sampling to keep every error trace; and topology — the service map that joins all three, turning separate data stores into one answer about which downstream is degraded and who calls it. Then cost discipline from day one: cardinality budgets per service, sampling tiers, per-team quotas. I learned that the hard way — an unbounded-attribute rollout once blew up our telemetry backend. And the payoff layer is where the value lives: alerts that carry context and RCA automation on top of all four layers. Our Auto-RCA agent synthesizes logs, metrics, topology, and thread dumps into ranked hypotheses — 500-plus tickets a day, root cause in under five minutes. That's impossible without clean layers underneath. The philosophy: observability is the debugging API of the platform. Design it like a product with customers — the on-call engineers — and measure it by their outcomes: MTTD down 35%, MTTR down 25%.

    Revision bullets

    • Four layers: metrics, logs, traces, topology as join

    • Golden signals with SLO per service owner

    • Propagation at Kafka and thread-pool boundaries

    • Cost designed in: cardinality budgets, quotas

    • Auto-RCA consumes all layers: 500+ tickets/day

    • Observability is the debugging API; measured by MTTD/MTTR

    If they push deeper

    • "Where would you start on a greenfield?" — Traces plus correlation IDs first — they're the join key everything else hangs off; metrics and SLOs follow within weeks.

    • "How do you measure whether observability is good?" — By its users' outcomes: MTTD and MTTR. Ours moved -35% and -25% — that's the product metric, not span counts.

    • "Build vs buy?" — Buy the pipeline, own the discipline: instrumentation standards, cardinality budgets, and the RCA layer are where our differentiation is.
