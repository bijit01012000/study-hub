    Salary negotiation

    7 sections · 3,052 words

    Module 10 · 6 questions · 1 practical drill

    Negotiate on real market data with scripts that never bluff, stonewall or lie.

    This module is where good engineers leave money on the table out of discomfort. The principles are consistent across every reputable guide: anchor on market data, defer numbers until you have an offer, never lie about competing offers, and negotiate the whole package, not just base. The follow-ups here are the recruiter's real tactics - "what are you looking for", "what's your current salary", "is this offer acceptable" - and the weak answers are the ones that give away leverage early or negotiate against yourself. Advice here is deliberately non-reckless: honest, data-driven, and relationship-preserving.

    Inside this module

    • Anchor on data
    • Defer · counter · pivot
    • No bluffs, no lies

    Diagram: anchors MARKET DATA / if base is capped YOUR NUMBER / THE LEVERS.

    Anchor every number on real data - then defer, counter, or pivot the levers. Never bluff.

    Q10.1 What are your salary expectations? (asked early, by a recruiter)

    What it really tests. Whether you can avoid anchoring yourself low or boxing yourself in before you have leverage - deferring gracefully while staying collaborative.

    Weak answer

    "I'm looking for around £75,000. That's roughly what I'm on now plus a bit, so that would work for me. I'm pretty flexible though, so if that's too high just let me know what you had in mind."

    Why it fails

    • Gives a specific number before any leverage exists - anchors the whole negotiation low.
    • Bases the ask on current salary, not market value.
    • "Pretty flexible, let me know if it's too high" instantly signals willingness to go lower.
    • Hands the recruiter the anchor and the permission to lowball.
    • No data behind the figure.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Ownership - "The recruiter presses: 'I need a number to move forward' - what do you say?" - Deferring under pressure without stonewalling.
    2. Technical - "If you must give a range, how do you set the bottom of it?" - Anchoring the range on data, floor at your target.
    3. Reflection - "Why not just answer with your current salary plus a bit?" - Understanding why current-salary anchoring hurts you.
    4. Tradeoff - "How do you keep this from feeling adversarial with the recruiter?" - Preserving the relationship.

    Answer framework

    1. Acknowledge the question warmly.
    2. Defer with a reason (early in the process).
    3. Redirect to fit / learning more.
    4. If pressed, give a researched range with your target at the BOTTOM.
    5. Base it on market data, not current pay.
    6. Keep it collaborative.
    7. Hand back to the process.

    Rejection signals

    • Blurts a single low number.
    • Anchors on current salary.
    • Immediately signals flexibility downward.
    • Treats the recruiter as an adversary.

    Model answer skeleton

    I'd say: 'Good question - honestly it's a bit early for me to put a firm number on it before I understand the full scope and the level, but I'm sure we can find something that works if it's the right fit. What's the band you've allocated for this role?' If the recruiter genuinely needs something to proceed, I don't stonewall - I give a researched range with my target at the FLOOR, not the middle: 'Based on levels.fyi and Blind data for this level and location, I'd be looking in the region of £90-105k, and that's flexible as I learn more about the scope.' I deliberately don't anchor on my current salary, because that ties my market value to my last employer's budget rather than the role.

    And I keep it warm - the recruiter's on my side if I treat them that way. The key move is deferring a firm number until I have an offer, because that's when I actually have leverage.

    Q10.2 The offer is £X. Is that acceptable to you? (counter-offer moment)

    What it really tests. Whether you can counter professionally with a data-backed anchor and justification, rather than accepting immediately or countering with an arbitrary number.

    Weak answer

    "Oh, that sounds great, thank you so much! Yes, that works for me, I'm really happy with that. I'm just excited to join, so wherever you land is fine - I don't want to be difficult about the money."

    Why it fails

    • Accepts instantly, leaving likely money on the table.
    • "Wherever you land is fine" abandons all negotiating position.
    • "Don't want to be difficult" treats normal negotiation as rudeness.
    • No counter, no data, no attempt to improve the offer.
    • Signals the candidate can be underpaid without pushback.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Measurement - "You didn't counter at all - what's the cost of accepting on the spot?" - Understanding the value left on the table.
    2. Technical - "How would you counter with a number that isn't arbitrary?" - A data-anchored, justified counter.
    3. Tradeoff - "What if they say the base is fixed?" - Negotiating other levers.
    4. Ownership - "How do you counter without souring the relationship?" - Collaborative, non-adversarial framing.

    Answer framework

    1. Warm thanks and genuine enthusiasm.
    2. Ask for time to consider (don't accept on the spot).
    3. Counter with a specific number anchored on data.
    4. Justify it with your value and market evidence.
    5. Signal flexibility across the package.
    6. Keep it collaborative and forward-looking.
    7. Confirm you want to make it work.

    Rejection signals

    • Accepts immediately.
    • No counter or justification.
    • Treats negotiating as being difficult.
    • Anchors the counter on nothing.

    Model answer skeleton

    I'd never accept on the spot, however good it feels. I'd say: 'Thank you, I'm genuinely excited about this and I want to make it work. Could I take a day to review the full package?' Then I'd come back with a data-anchored counter, not a number from the air: 'I've looked at levels.fyi and Blind for this level and location, and comparable roles are landing around £X+15%. Given the payments-reliability experience I'd bring straight in - the re-architecture that took a 2% failure rate to zero at 5x load - I was hoping we could get the base to £Y.' I anchor with the market data first, then justify with specific value.

    If they say base is capped, I pivot to other levers rather than digging in - sign-on bonus, equity, or an earlier review. And I keep it warm throughout: 'I want to join, I'm just trying to get this right for both of us.' The point is a justified counter almost always nets more and rarely costs you the offer if it's reasonable and well-framed.

    Q10.3 Do you have any competing offers?

    What it really tests. Whether you can convey leverage honestly - using real competing interest without lying or bluffing about offers you don't have.

    Weak answer

    "Yeah, I've got a couple of other offers on the table that are quite a bit higher, so you'd need to beat those. One of them is at a top company for way more money. So you'll have to come up strong to compete with what I've already got."

    Why it fails

    • Likely a bluff - "a couple of offers, quite a bit higher" with no specifics.
    • Bluffing is dangerous: many companies ask to see the offer letter.
    • Aggressive, adversarial framing ("you'll have to come up strong").
    • Vague, unverifiable claims read as untrustworthy.
    • Risks the offer being pulled or the bluff being called.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "What if they ask to see the competing offer letter?" - Why bluffing is reckless - offers get verified.
    2. Ownership - "How do you convey leverage honestly if you DON'T have another offer?" - Truthful leverage without a competing offer.
    3. Tradeoff - "How do you use a real competing offer without being adversarial?" - Leverage that preserves the relationship.
    4. Reflection - "What's the risk of overplaying this?" - Understanding the downside of bluffing.

    Answer framework

    1. Be truthful about your actual situation.
    2. If you have a real offer, state it factually and let it speak.
    3. If you don't, convey genuine competing interest / timeline honestly.
    4. Never fabricate numbers or offers.
    5. Keep the framing 'help me choose you', not 'beat them'.
    6. Redirect to why you prefer this role.
    7. Stay collaborative.

    Rejection signals

    • Bluffs about offers that don't exist.
    • Vague, unverifiable claims.
    • Adversarial "beat this or else" framing.
    • Would be caught out if asked for proof.

    Model answer skeleton

    I never bluff here - it's both wrong and reckless, because plenty of companies will ask to see the offer letter, and getting caught fabricating one ends the process.

    If I genuinely have another offer, I state it plainly and let it do the work: 'I do - I've a written offer from another company at £X, and I'm weighing it against this. Honestly I'd rather come here because of the payments work, so I'm hoping we can close the gap.' If I DON'T have a competing offer, I don't invent one; I convey real leverage truthfully: 'I'm in final stages with a couple of other companies and I've a decision timeline of about two weeks, so it'd help to understand your flexibility.' Either way I frame it as 'help me choose you', not 'beat them or else', because I want this to end with a good relationship, not a standoff.

    The risk of overplaying it is real - a called bluff can lose the offer entirely, and it's never worth it.

    Q10.4 What's your current salary?

    What it really tests. Whether you can decline to anchor on your current pay gracefully - pivoting to expectations based on the role's value - without lying.

    Weak answer

    "I'm currently on £68,000. So obviously I'd be looking for a bit more than that to move - maybe £73,000 or £75,000 would be enough to make the switch worthwhile for me."

    Why it fails

    • Discloses current salary, anchoring the new offer to an old, possibly below-market number.
    • "A bit more than that" ties the raise to current pay, not market value.
    • Names a low target based on incremental logic.
    • Gives away the single most useful piece of information for lowballing.
    • (In some jurisdictions the employer can't even ask - the candidate volunteers it anyway.)

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Reflection - "Why is anchoring the new offer to your old salary a mistake?" - Understanding market-value vs current-pay anchoring.
    2. Ownership - "How do you decline to share it without being evasive or lying?" - A graceful, honest deflection.
    3. Technical - "What do you redirect to instead?" - Pivoting to role-based expectations.
    4. Tradeoff - "What if they insist it's required?" - Handling firm insistence professionally.

    Answer framework

    1. Politely decline to anchor on current pay.
    2. Give a reason (want to be evaluated on the role's value).
    3. Redirect to your researched expectations for THIS role.
    4. Never lie about the number.
    5. If they insist, share a total-comp figure or a range framed forward.
    6. Keep it warm.

    Rejection signals

    • Volunteers current salary and anchors on it.
    • Sets the target as "current plus a bit".
    • Ties market value to the last employer's budget.
    • Lies about the number instead of deflecting.

    Model answer skeleton

    I'd deflect this gracefully rather than either lying or handing over the anchor: 'I'd prefer to focus on the value of this role rather than my current package, partly because they're quite different scopes. Based on my research for this level, I'm looking in the region of £90-100k.' I redirect to market-based expectations because anchoring a new offer to my current salary just imports my old employer's budget into a new company's decision - if I'm underpaid now, that's not a reason to be underpaid next.

    I never lie about the actual number, because that can unravel; I simply decline to make it the anchor. If they genuinely insist it's required by their process, I'll share total compensation including equity and frame it forward - 'my total comp is around £X, and for a role at this scope I'm targeting £Y' - so even then the conversation stays about the role's worth, not a raise on a stale base.

    Q10.5 We can't move on base salary - this is our final number. How do you respond?

    What it really tests. Whether you can negotiate the whole package creatively when one lever is stuck, rather than caving immediately or fixating only on base.

    Weak answer

    "Oh okay, if the base is fixed then I understand, I'll just take it as it is. I don't want to push too hard and risk the offer. The base is the main thing anyway, so if that can't move then that's fine, I accept."

    Why it fails

    • Caves the moment one lever is declined.
    • "Base is the main thing anyway" ignores the rest of the package.
    • "Don't want to risk the offer" - a reasonable ask on other levers rarely risks it.
    • Explores none of the other negotiable components.
    • Leaves total compensation value unclaimed.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Technical - "Base is one lever - what else is negotiable?" - Knowing the full package (sign-on, equity, review, etc.).
    2. Tradeoff - "How do you test whether 'final' is really final?" - Politely probing without brinkmanship.
    3. Reflection - "Which non-base lever is worth most to you, and why?" - Prioritising the whole package to your situation.
    4. Ownership - "How do you ask without seeming to disregard their 'no'?" - Respecting the constraint while still negotiating.

    Answer framework

    1. Acknowledge and respect the base constraint.
    2. Pivot to other levers (sign-on, equity, review timing, start date, title, PTO, remote, learning budget).
    3. Prioritise the lever that matters most to you.
    4. Ask specifically and reasonably.
    5. Frame it as making the total work.
    6. Get any agreement in writing.
    7. Confirm your enthusiasm regardless.

    Rejection signals

    • Caves instantly.
    • Fixates on base, ignores the package.
    • Explores no alternative levers.
    • Treats any further ask as too risky.

    Model answer skeleton

    I'd respect the constraint but not treat 'base is fixed' as 'the negotiation's over': 'That's completely fair, I understand base is capped at this level - could we look at the rest of the package instead?' Then I pivot to the levers that ARE usually movable: a sign-on bonus to bridge the gap, a larger equity grant, or an accelerated performance review at six months to revisit base. I'd prioritise based on my situation - if I trust the company's trajectory I'll push on equity; if I want certainty I'll push on sign-on or the early review.

    I ask specifically and reasonably - 'would a £10k sign-on be possible to close the gap?' - rather than vaguely. I frame it as making the total work for both of us, and I make clear I'm enthusiastic either way so it never sounds like an ultimatum. And whatever we agree, I get it in writing in the offer.

    Caving the moment base is capped leaves real money - often several thousand in sign-on or equity - completely untouched.

    Q10.6 Practical drill - Salary negotiation

    Write your three negotiation scripts: (1) deferring a number in the recruiter screen, (2) countering an offer with a data-anchored justification, (3) pivoting to other levers when base is capped. Base every number on real market data you've looked up.

    What it really tests. Forces you to pre-write and rehearse the exact lines for the three moments people fumble live - turning negotiation from an anxious improvisation into a prepared, principled conversation.

    Weak answer

    N/A - drill. A weak attempt pulls numbers from thin air, defers by stonewalling, counters with no justification, and has no plan for when base is capped.

    Why it fails

    • Numbers aren't grounded in market data.
    • "Deferring" comes across as evasive stonewalling.
    • The counter has no data or value justification.
    • No script at all for the capped-base pivot.

    Follow-up ladder

    1. Measurement - "What's the actual market range you're anchoring on, and from which source?" - Real data (levels.fyi, Blind), not a guess.
    2. Technical - "Does your counter lead with data and value, or just a number?" - A justified anchor.
    3. Reflection - "Are any of these scripts dishonest or reckless - a bluffed offer, a lie about current pay?" - Keeping the advice sound and non-reckless.

    Answer framework

    1. Look up the real market range for your level/location (levels.fyi, Blind, Glassdoor).
    2. Script 1: warm deferral with a reason, target at range floor if pressed.
    3. Script 2: thanks + time + data-anchored counter + value justification.
    4. Script 3: respect the cap + pivot to sign-on/equity/early review.
    5. Check no script lies or bluffs.
    6. Rehearse all three aloud.

    Rejection signals

    • Numbers not grounded in data.
    • Deferral is stonewalling.
    • Counter lacks justification.
    • Any script relies on a bluff or a lie.

    Model answer skeleton

    Drill output, anchored on levels.fyi/Blind data for my level and city (say the range reads £85-110k): Script 1 (defer): 'It's a bit early to fix a number before I understand the scope, but I'm sure we'll find a fit - what band have you set for the role? If you need a figure, I'm in the region of £95-105k based on market data, flexible as I learn more.' (Target at the floor, never current-salary-based.) Script 2 (counter): 'Thank you - I'm excited and want to make it work. Comparable roles on levels.fyi are around £105k, and I'd bring the payments-reliability work directly in, so I was hoping we could reach £108k on base.' (Data first, then value.) Script 3 (capped base): 'Understood that base is fixed - could we look at a sign-on bonus or an accelerated six-month review instead?' Honesty check: no bluffed competing offer, no lie about current pay, every number traceable to a real source. Rehearsed all three aloud so they come out calm, not shaky.
