    Design a News Feed

    3 sections · 3,346 words

    Three levels: Level 1 — The Shape, Level 2 — The Defence, Level 3 — The Edges.

    Level 1 — The Shape (Foundation)

    Pacing: 3 min ask · 5 min trace flow · 7 min read why.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Post an update (text)
    • Follow / unfollow a user
    • View home timeline — newest first

    Non-Functional

    • p99 timeline load < 200 ms ← forces a pre-computed timeline
    • Read : write ≈ 20 : 1 ← forces fan-out on write
    • Fan-out unbounded in followers ← forces a celebrity split

    Agree These First

    200M DAU · 0.3 posts/day · 6 opens/day. Round, illustrative numbers — not a real company's traffic. Every number here falls out of these three.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client — Mobile app / web
    • (1) POST /posts — 694 rps
    • API Gateway — Auth · quota · path split (post vs. timeline)
    • Post Service — POST /posts · writethrough to Post Store
    • Post Store — post_id, author_id, text, created_at — source of truth
    • (7) GET /timeline — 13,889 rps · ~13.9K/s
    • Timeline Service — GET /timeline
    • Timeline Store — Wide sorted list per user, scored by time · Redis-like

    Data model: posts post_id PK → author_id, text, created_at.

    Line weight = traffic: READ · 13.9K rps; WRITE · 694 rps.

    Say It Like This

    "Two operations — post and read the timeline — and reads win, about 20 to 1. So I'd fan out on write: push the post ID into every follower's pre-computed timeline, so a read is one lookup — that's what buys the 200 ms budget. Timeline Store holds IDs only, not content, so an edit is one write. The one thing I'd flag unprompted: a follower count in the millions breaks this, and I'd come back to it."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (1) Service split Post and Timeline are separate services — their load differs One service for both → the write path inherits the read path's latency-critical constraints for nothing
    (7) Pre-computed timeline Fan-out on write — reads beat writes 20:1, and the budget is 200 ms Live join across follows → every read fans in, far too slow
    (8) Index / content split Timeline Store holds IDs only — an edit or delete is one write, not N Inline content in every timeline → an edit propagates to millions of copies

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Writes 200M DAU × 0.3 posts/day ÷ 86 400 s 694 /s
    Reads 200M DAU × 6 opens/day ÷ 86 400 s 13.9K /s
    Ratio 13,889 ÷ 694 — vs. sheet 01's 100:1 20 : 1

    Round hard, out loud. Reads beat writes about 20 to 1 — nowhere near sheet 01's 100:1, and that gap is the first hint this architecture looks different.

    Before You Draw Anything — The Four Moves

    1. Clarify, don't assume. What counts as "the feed" — chronological or ranked? Say ranking is out of scope for now. — Two minutes of scoping buys the whole design.
    2. Agree the scale out loud. "200M DAU, 0.3 posts/day, 6 opens/day — reads beat writes 20 to 1." Now every later choice has a reason. — An unstated number is an undefendable design.
    3. Draw the happy path only. Client → gateway → post service / timeline service → stores. Get agreement before adding anything. — Boxes you add before agreement are boxes you'll defend alone.
    4. Name one trade-off unprompted. "I'd fan out on write, because reads outnumber writes 20 to 1." — This is the single move that separates L1 from a junior answer.

    What L1 Is Testing

    • Can you produce a shape without being led to it?
    • Does every box have a reason you volunteered?
    • Did you reach for the 20:1 ratio before being asked?

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. Fan-out on write, or fan-out on read? (refs 4,7 — tests: do you know the trade-off exists at all)
    2. An account has 10 million followers and posts. Walk me through what happens. (ref 5 — tests: can you find where the naive approach breaks, unprompted)
    3. How would you rank the feed instead of showing it chronologically? (ref 3 — tests: awareness that ranking is a separate scoring layer)
    4. Why split into a Post Service and a Timeline Service instead of one? (refs 2,3 — tests: do you split on load shape, or out of habit)
    5. Day one, a brand-new user follows nobody. What does their timeline show? (ref 7 — tests: can you walk the empty path, not only the happy one)

    At L1 you are being judged on one thing: can you produce a coherent shape and defend three choices inside it. Depth is page 2 — do not reach for it here. Ghosted boxes are what arrives next; you should be able to guess two of them.

    Level 2 — The Defence (Intermediate)

    Pacing: 5 min ask · 15 min trace flow · 10 min read why · 30 min cover & answer.

    The Ask

    Functional

    • Post an update (text)
    • Follow / unfollow a user
    • View home timeline — newest first
    • Like / react — eventually consistent count
    • Near-real-time delivery — seconds, not a refresh cycle

    Non-Functional

    • p99 timeline load < 200 ms ← forces a pre-computed timeline
    • Read : write ≈ 20 : 1 ← forces fan-out on write
    • Fan-out unbounded in followers ← forces a celebrity split
    • Delivery in seconds, not minutes ← forces an async pipeline
    • Read-your-writes for own post ← forces a fan-out shortcut

    Out of Scope — Say It Aloud

    • Media attachments (images / video)
    • Ranked / algorithmic feed — chronological only
    • Comments, DMs, stories

    Agree These First

    200M DAU · 0.3 posts/day · 6 opens/day. Round, illustrative numbers — not a real company's traffic. Every number here falls out of these three.

    The Shape

    Components and flow:

    • Client — Mobile app / web
    • (1) POST /posts — 694 rps
    • API Gateway — Auth · quota · path split (post vs. timeline)
    • Post Service — POST /posts · writethrough to Post Store
    • Post Store — post_id, author_id, text, created_at — source of truth
    • (4) Fan-out Queue — the async hop
    • (5) Fan-out Worker — writes into every follower's list
    • Follow Graph — follower_id → followee_id · fan-out's hidden dependency
    • (7) GET /timeline — 13,889 rps · ~13.9K/s
    • Timeline Service — GET /timeline
    • Timeline Store — Wide sorted list per user, scored by time · Redis-like; ≈24 KB/user · ~4.8 TB total
    • Engagement Counters — like / react · eventually consistent

    Data model:

    • posts post_id PK · author_id · text · created_at
    • timeline user_id PK · sorted (post_id, ts)
    • follows follower_id · followee_id
    • reactions post_id · count, rolled up

    Line weight = traffic: READ · 13.9K rps; WRITE · 694 rps; FAN-OUT · ~104K/s, async.

    Say It Like This

    "Two operations — post and read the timeline — and reads win, about 20 to 1. So I'd fan out on write: push the post ID into every follower's pre-computed timeline, so a read is one lookup — that's what buys the 200 ms budget. Timeline Store holds IDs only, not content, so an edit is one write. The one thing I'd flag unprompted: a follower count in the millions breaks this, and I'd come back to it."

    Defend It

    Decision Prefer Avoid
    (1) Service split Post and Timeline are separate services — their load differs One service for both → the write path inherits the read path's latency-critical constraints for nothing
    (7) Pre-computed timeline Fan-out on write — reads beat writes 20:1, and the budget is 200 ms Live join across follows → every read fans in, far too slow
    (8) Index / content split Timeline Store holds IDs only — an edit or delete is one write, not N Inline content in every timeline → an edit propagates to millions of copies
    (4) Async fan-out A queue between Post Service and the worker — the write returns in ms Synchronous fan-out → your POST waits on N slow downstream writes
    (6) Follow graph freshness Worker reads current membership at write time, not a cached snapshot Stale snapshot → an unfollow mid-fan-out leaves a stray entry behind
    (9) Your own post Merged in immediately for your own view — doesn't wait on fan-out Wait for fan-out → you post and don't see it yourself for seconds
    (10) Like counts Async increment, eventually consistent — never blocks a read Synchronous counter update → serialises writes on a hot post

    Capacity Math

    Quantity Derivation Value
    Writes 200M DAU × 0.3 posts/day ÷ 86 400 s 694 /s
    Reads 200M DAU × 6 opens/day ÷ 86 400 s 13.9K /s
    Ratio 13,889 ÷ 694 — vs. sheet 01's 100:1 20 : 1
    Fan-out 60M posts/day × avg 150 followers (power-law) 9.0 B/day
    Fan-out rate 9.0 B ÷ 86 400 s ≈104K /s
    Per-user list 800 entries × ~30 B (id + score/ts) ≈24 KB
    Storage 24 KB × 200M DAU ≈4.8 TB

    694 writes/s is nothing. What one write becomes is the problem. A post fans out to ~104K downstream list-inserts/s on average — that gap is why fan-out has to be async, not the raw write rate.

    What Breaks — Say It Before They Ask

    • (4) Fan-out storm. A high-follower account posts; the queue backs up for minutes behind millions of list-inserts. — Fix: the celebrity split (L3, 11,12) removes this class of account from the write-fan-out path entirely
    • (5) Unfollow race. A user unfollows someone mid-fan-out of that person's post — a stray entry in their timeline? — Fix: the worker checks current follow-graph membership at write time; a rare stray entry is filtered at read time as a cheap backstop
    • (6) Follow Graph is the hidden dependency. If it's slow or down, fan-out stalls even though Post Service and Post Store are healthy. — Fix: alert on Follow Graph specifically — it's easy to miss which store fan-out actually depends on

    The Follow-Ups That Decide It

    1. Fan-out on write, or fan-out on read? (refs 4,7 — tests: do you know the trade-off exists at all)
    2. An account has 10 million followers and posts. Walk me through what happens. (ref 5 — tests: can you find where the naive approach breaks, unprompted)
    3. How would you rank the feed instead of showing it chronologically? (ref 3 — tests: awareness that ranking is a separate scoring layer)
    4. Someone unfollows a user half a second after that user posts. What does their timeline show? (ref 5 — tests: race-condition instinct)
    5. Your fan-out queue backs up by ten minutes. What does a follower see, and do they know it's stale? (ref 4 — tests: staleness-window honesty)
    6. Two people like the same post in the same second. Does the count race? (ref 10 — tests: concurrency instinct on an async counter)

    Cover The Sheet · Answer Aloud

    Use it as revision: cover the right rail and the two panels to your left, then walk the numbered path out loud. If you cannot say why a box is there and what breaks it, that box is your gap.
